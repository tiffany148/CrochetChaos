Crochet turns a flexible strand into a structure that can hold its shape, stretch, fold, or drape. A hook draws a new loop through an existing loop or through an opening in the fabric. The path of that yarn determines the stitch. The place where you insert the hook determines how that stitch connects to the rest of the piece. Learn those two ideas separately and a complicated pattern becomes a series of understandable decisions.

You do not need to memorize a dictionary before beginning. In this chapter you will learn to recognize the yarn, the live loop, and the parts of a completed stitch. Chapter 9 takes you through the actual movements. Reading your fabric from the beginning makes it easier to recognize a mistake while it is still one stitch away.

## One strand and two very different ends

Pull a length of yarn from a ball and set the ball beside you. The end you can hold freely is the tail. The strand that still leads to the ball is the working yarn. Unless a direction specifically says otherwise, make stitches with the working yarn. A tail can look deceptively useful when it is long; if you crochet with it by mistake, you will simply run out of yarn after a few stitches.

Crochet is not made by repeatedly pushing the hook through the yarn itself. The hook normally goes through an existing stitch opening, under selected strands, or into a space between stitches. Splitting the plies of the yarn is usually accidental. A split stitch can trap the hook, make a weak connection, and produce a ragged surface.

The hook catches a small bend of working yarn and draws it through another loop. The new loop becomes the next live loop or an intermediate loop in a taller stitch. Ordinary crochet usually returns to one loop on the hook after each completed stitch. Tunisian crochet and several specialty techniques deliberately hold more loops; the one-loop rule is a learning aid for ordinary crochet, not a definition of every crochet method.

## The live loop is not a completed chain

The loop around the hook is the active or live loop. If the hook slips out, the fabric does not instantly disintegrate, but that loop can enlarge and begin to unravel if the working yarn is pulled. Insert a locking marker through the live loop before setting the project down. Close the marker. The hook can then travel separately without becoming a tiny yarn-based escape artist.

When you count a foundation chain, count the completed chain links below the hook. Do not count the live loop or the slip knot. A chain of 12 contains 12 completed links, even though another loop rests on the hook. This distinction matters because most first rows deliberately skip one or more chains to establish the height of the first stitch.

The last chain made is the chain nearest the hook. The first chain made is nearest the beginning tail. For example, “second chain from hook” means skip the nearest completed chain and insert into the next one. The live loop never occupies the first place in this count.

## Anatomy of a chain and a stitch

Lay a chain down with its smooth side facing you. Each link looks like a small V. Turn the chain over and you will find a line of bumps along its back. Those bumps belong to the same links; they are not extra chains. A first row can be worked through selected strands of the V or into the back bumps. Different entry choices affect the appearance and stretch of the lower edge. The pattern should identify a special entry choice; otherwise use a consistent ordinary chain entry.

In a row of completed single crochet stitches, each stitch has a top that also looks like a V. Under ordinary “work in the next stitch” instructions, insert under both strands of that top V. The strand nearer you is the front loop and the strand farther from you is the back loop. These names describe the stitch as it faces you while you work. When the fabric is turned, the viewer’s near and far sides change.

Below the top V is the body or post of the stitch. In a tall stitch the post is easy to see as a vertical column. Front post and back post instructions work around that column instead of into the top. Do not treat the hole beside a post as the stitch top. Working into that hole changes the fabric even if the stitch count remains the same.

| Part | How to recognize it | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Live loop | Loop currently on the hook | Secure it during pauses; do not count it as a chain |
| Top V | Two strands across the top of a finished stitch | Usual insertion point for the next row |
| Front loop | Near strand of the top V as you work | FLO placement leaves the other strand visible |
| Back loop | Far strand of the top V as you work | BLO placement can produce ridges and flexibility |
| Post | Body below the top V | Post stitches surround it |
| Chain space | Opening formed by one or more chains | Several stitches may be placed into the opening |
| Beginning tail | Free end at the starting knot or ring | Must eventually be secured |
| Working yarn | Strand leading to the supply | Feeds each new stitch |

## Stitches are actions as well as objects

The word stitch can mean the action you are making or the completed unit you are making it into. “Work 1 sc in the next st” separates those meanings: make one single crochet, placing it in the next existing stitch. “Work 2 sc in the next st” puts two new stitches into one old stitch, creating an increase. “Skip next st” leaves an old stitch unworked.

An opening can be a legitimate destination without being a stitch. “Work 3 dc in the ch-2 space” means insert into the opening under the two-chain arch, rather than individually into the two chain links. Afterward, the three double crochets count as three stitches. The arch may be counted as a space in a pattern’s separate space total. Never assume that a space equals one stitch unless the pattern explicitly uses that convention.

The direction “work evenly” usually concerns distribution, especially along a side edge. It does not automatically mean one new stitch per row end. Short rows and tall rows need different edge treatment. You will learn to determine a suitable edging rate through a sample rather than forcing a universal number.

## Right side and wrong side

The right side, or RS, is the intended public face of the fabric. The wrong side, or WS, is the reverse. Neither means morally correct or technically defective. A reversible scarf may have two equally attractive faces. A garment may place the smoother or more textured face outward according to its design.

Turned rows expose alternating faces as you crochet. Working continuously around a tube generally keeps the same face toward you, although the tube can accidentally turn inside out. Use a different-colored marker on the intended RS. That small decision becomes useful later when you attach sleeves, embroider a face, or seam matching panels.

Right-handed and left-handed crocheters produce directions that are often mirror images. When adapting instruction, “next stitch,” “near loop,” and “turn the work” are more dependable than “the stitch on the left.” Written stitch counts do not change simply because you hold the hook in the opposite hand. An asymmetric color chart, garment opening, or directional motif may need a separate decision about mirroring.

## How a piece grows

A flat rectangle begins with a foundation, grows across a row, then turns so another row can be added above it. A circle begins at a center and gains enough stitches to expand outward. A tube maintains approximately the same number of stitches around its circumference. A shaped piece changes width by adding or removing stitches and changes height by adding rows or rounds.

Every change has a physical result. More stitches distributed around a circle make a larger circumference. Too many can create ruffles. Too few can produce a cup. These results depend on stitch height, tension, yarn, and spacing. A familiar six-increase circle recipe in single crochet is a useful starting model; it is not a law of geometry that fits every fabric.

The object’s size comes from both stitch count and gauge. Twenty stitches in a thick, loose fabric may occupy more space than thirty stitches in a fine, dense fabric. Hook size contributes to gauge but does not determine it alone. Your movements, yarn construction, and finishing method also matter.

## Your first fabric-reading exercise

Use a scrap of smooth, light-colored yarn. You may borrow a small crocheted swatch or make one after completing Chapter 9. Avoid fluffy yarn for this exercise because the surface fibers hide the structure you are trying to see.

1. Find the live loop and secure it with a locking marker.
2. Follow the strand from the live loop to the ball. Identify it as working yarn.
3. Find the starting tail at the opposite end. Trace where the foundation begins.
4. Locate one top V. Slide a blunt needle under both strands without splitting them.
5. Remove the needle and identify the near and far strands separately.
6. Follow the body of that stitch downward to its base.
7. Count the top Vs across one complete row. Count again from the other end.

The two counts should agree. If they do not, mark every fifth stitch and count the smaller groups. A row- end turning chain can imitate a stitch. For this exercise, use the convention stated in the practice pattern; do not decide from appearance alone whether it belongs in the total.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Put the marker in the top V you need to find again, not vaguely around a nearby strand. A marker dangling from a post can remind you that something happened there, but it cannot reliably identify the exact insertion point.
>
> Photograph a piece before you unravel it. The picture records the appearance of a mistake and the direction in which the fabric was worked. A useful mistake collection can teach you more than a stack of perfect squares with no labels.
>
> When everything looks like an indistinguishable yarn cloud, make the problem larger and simpler. Choose smooth yarn, a lighter color, better light, and a small sample. Changing the learning conditions is a practical tool, not a failure of patience.

## Check your understanding

You make 15 chains and still have one loop on the hook. How many chains are there? Fifteen. You work two new stitches into one old stitch. What changed? The new row gained one stitch relative to ordinary one-for-one placement. You turn the work and see the opposite face. Did the RS change? No; the intended public face remains the same, although it now faces away from you.

Before moving on, be able to point to the tail, working yarn, active loop, top V, and post without guessing. You do not need a beautiful square yet. You need a vocabulary for what your hands are doing.

## Quick reference: the parts of crochet

| Term | What it is | Where to see it |
|---|---|---|
| Working yarn | The strand coming from the ball | Runs from your hand to the hook |
| Tail | The cut end at the start | Hangs from the slip knot |
| Live loop | The loop on the hook | Never counted as a stitch |
| Top V | Two loops on top of each stitch | Look straight down at the row |
| Post | The vertical body of a stitch | Look at the fabric face-on |
| Turning chain | Chains that raise the hook at a row start | Edge of rows |
| Right side | The side meant to show | Often marked with a clip-on marker |

## Crochet compared with other yarn crafts

Crochet works one live loop at a time with a single hook. Every stitch is finished before the next begins, so there is usually only one loop to keep safe. That is why crochet is easy to put down, easy to undo a little at a time, and able to build almost any shape: flat, round, tubular or sculpted. Tunisian crochet is the exception that holds many loops on a long hook, and it is covered in Chapter 57.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — The loop on the hook never counts
> When you count stitches, skip the loop on your hook. It is a working loop, not a finished stitch. Counting it is the most common reason a first row comes out one stitch "too many".

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Put a marker on the right side
> Clip a stitch marker to the right side of your work as soon as you know which side it is. After a few rows of single crochet, the two sides look almost identical.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Before you learn ten stitches, learn to see one. Hold a finished stitch up to the light, find its top V and its post, and the rest of crochet starts to make sense.

## A two-minute fabric-reading habit

At the end of every session, look at the last row for ten seconds:

1. Count the stitches, or count to the next marker.
2. Check that the first and last stitches are where you expect.
3. Note the row number on a sticky note or in a project app.

This small habit catches most mistakes within one row, when they are still easy to fix.


> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Pull from the center
> Most machine-wound balls have a center end. Pull from the center and the ball sits still instead of rolling across the floor.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Keep the ball band
> Tuck the ball band into your project bag. It holds the dye lot, fiber, care and yardage you will need later.

> [!HINT] Good light, fewer mistakes
> Crochet under a bright, neutral light. Dark yarn under warm lamplight hides stitches and makes counting tiring.
