Choose yarn for the life the object will have. A dishcloth must survive wet handling. A cardigan must carry its own weight. A dog sweater must tolerate movement and its actual cleaning routine. A decorative flower may need crisp petals more than softness. Fiber is part of that decision, but the way the yarn is constructed and the density of the crochet can matter just as much.

This chapter describes tendencies, not guarantees. A smooth, tightly spun wool and a lightly spun wool can behave differently even when both labels say 100 percent wool. A cotton blend can stretch because of its construction. A machine-washable fiber can be sold in a yarn whose dye or finish requires gentler care. Treat the label as the care authority and the washed swatch as evidence of the fabric you will actually make.

## Animal fibers

Wool comes from sheep. Many wool yarns have a springy character that helps loops recover after being stretched. The amount of recovery varies with breed, processing, spin, and fabric. Wool can be useful for warm garments, textured stitches, and fabric that benefits from resilience. Some people find particular wools comfortable next to the skin; others find them irritating. Test a small sample against the intended wearer rather than deciding from softness in the ball alone.

Untreated wool can felt when conditions encourage the fibers to entangle. Do not assume a hand-crocheted object can be restored merely by stretching it after accidental felting. Wool sold with a machine-washable care claim has been processed or constructed for a particular care regime; that claim still needs to be followed exactly. “Superwash” is not permission to use every wash cycle or dryer setting.

Alpaca, mohair, cashmere, and other animal fibers have distinct qualities. Alpaca yarns often feel fluid and warm but may not recover like a springy wool yarn. Mohair can create a halo that obscures stitch detail and makes unravelling more difficult. Fine luxury fibers may be blended to improve handling or reduce cost. Evaluate the actual blend, not an idealized reputation attached to one ingredient.

Silk is also an animal fiber. Many silk-containing yarns bring sheen and drape. Smooth yarns can make woven ends more mobile; heavily textured silk yarns behave differently. If a design depends on a firm edge or crisp three-dimensional shape, test whether the silk blend supplies that structure.

## Plant fibers

Cotton is a seed fiber. It is commonly used where absorbency and clear stitch definition are useful. Compared with many springy wool yarns, an ordinary cotton yarn may offer less elastic recovery. A dense cotton sweater can therefore feel heavy, and a cotton rib may not behave like a commercial elastic cuff.

Mercerized cotton is processed to change properties including appearance and dye response. It often has a lustrous surface, but the word mercerized does not tell you its exact stiffness, thickness, or suitability for a particular project. Crochet a sample rather than treating a finish name as a pattern specification.

Linen and hemp are bast fibers. Yarns containing them can feel firm initially and may soften with handling and appropriate washing. Their limited spring can be attractive in a draping summer garment but less suitable for a design that depends on elastic recovery. A plant-fiber sleeve can fit beautifully when constructed for its behavior and sag when copied from a resilient wool design without adjustment.

## Regenerated cellulose

Many yarns marketed with the word bamboo are regenerated cellulose, such as bamboo-derived viscose or rayon. The plant source and the finished fiber are not the same thing. Read the fiber declaration rather than assuming the yarn is mechanically processed bamboo bast fiber.

Viscose, rayon, modal, and lyocell are related by their cellulose basis but are not identical products or manufacturing processes. In crochet, a fluid hand and growth under load may be relevant. A yarn that falls beautifully across your hand can also lengthen when an entire garment hangs from the shoulders. Test a larger swatch and include a supported hanging observation for a long garment.

“Natural,” “plant-based,” and “eco” are not complete descriptions of environmental impact or performance. The manufacturing process, dyeing, transportation, use, care, and lifespan all contribute. Record a specific claim when it is documented; do not infer a broad environmental ranking from a botanical name.

## Synthetic fibers

Acrylic, polyester, nylon or polyamide, and elastane are examples of synthetic fibers that may appear on a yarn label. They can be used alone or in blends. Acrylic is common in easy-care yarns, polyester in many plush constructions, nylon in blends intended to improve durability, and elastane where stretch is engineered into the yarn. Those are common uses, not guarantees that every yarn in the category performs the same way.

Heat can permanently change synthetic yarns. Steam blocking, direct ironing, and a hot dryer are not interchangeable treatments. Do not place an iron directly on an unknown crochet fabric to flatten it. Try any permitted finishing treatment on a sample and preserve an untreated comparison.

A yarn’s softness does not establish its suitability for contact with high heat. Kitchen projects need an appropriate material and construction, and they should never be assumed to provide tested thermal protection. A decorative potholder pattern is not a certified oven mitt. Do not use an untested crochet object as a safety barrier.

## Blends and competing priorities

A blend combines fibers, but its behavior is not necessarily a simple weighted average. A small amount of one fiber can change surface friction, elasticity, or strength significantly. Construction can amplify or reduce those effects. A wool-cotton blend may be more resilient than one cotton yarn and less resilient than another wool yarn; the percentage alone cannot predict the finished fit.

When comparing candidates, rank the project’s needs. For a frequently worn cardigan, you might prioritize acceptable skin feel, manageable weight, recovery, laundering, and sufficient yardage. For lace, you might prioritize drape, blocked definition, and stability of open spaces. A yarn that meets four needs and fails the fifth may still be the wrong choice.

| Project need | What to investigate | What a label cannot prove alone |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic recovery | Stretch a washed sample and let it rest | Whether a cuff will stay fitted |
| Drape | Suspend or fold a realistic sample | How a full garment will hang |
| Washability | Follow the exact care instructions | Whether your chosen seam and trim survive |
| Crisp texture | Compare stitch definition after washing | Whether raised stitches remain distinct |
| Wear resistance | Rub and use a sample realistically | Long-term pilling or abrasion performance |
| Skin comfort | Try against intended wearer’s skin | Individual comfort from fiber name |
| Low weight | Compare sample mass for equal area | Final garment mass from ball softness |

## A useful fiber comparison laboratory

Choose two or three yarns of broadly similar thickness but different fiber composition. Make equal-count single crochet samples with a suitable hook for each. Record the hook because forcing every yarn onto the same hook may produce an unfair comparison of fabric quality. Record both stitch count and dimensions so you can distinguish “same count” from “same area.”

Measure each dry sample without stretching it. Weigh it if your scale can resolve the mass usefully. Wash each according to its own label, then dry as directed. Measure again only when fully dry. Compare width, length, surface, and flexibility.

For a stretch observation, mark two points a known distance apart. Extend the sample gently to a recorded length, release it, and let it rest. Measure the distance again. This is a home comparison, not a standardized laboratory test. Do not present the result as an accredited elasticity rating. Its value is that you used the same procedure and kept notes.

For garment drape, support the sample across its full upper edge rather than hanging it from one stitch. Measure before hanging, after a stated period, and after resting flat. The sample’s weight may not reproduce a full garment’s load. If the project is long or heavy, make a larger sample or an actual panel before committing.

## Read a blend in context

Imagine a pattern written for a resilient 100 percent wool yarn. You prefer a smooth cotton-viscose blend. Both can produce 16 stitches over 4 inches after washing. That matching width does not establish equivalent garment behavior. The substitute may have different row gauge, greater mass per area, less recovery, or a different response at seams.

You could still use the substitute, but first make a sample in the actual stitch pattern. Test a seam and border. Check the intended ease. Consider whether the neckline needs reinforcement or whether the body length should account for observed growth. If those changes become extensive, treat the work as a redesign rather than a simple yarn swap.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> A yarn can feel soft in a loose ball and feel much firmer when crocheted densely. Test the fabric density the project needs before buying the entire quantity.
>
> Save the washed sample with the label, not just the unused yarn. The washed sample records the material you will live with; the ball records the material you bought.
>
> When a yarn grows during washing, do not automatically subtract a guessed percentage from every dimension. Width and length may change by different amounts, and a heavy garment may behave differently from a tiny sample.

## Check your understanding

Two yarns have the same fiber content and nominal weight category. Are they automatically substitutes? No. Compare construction, washed gauge, drape, mass, and care. A yarn says bamboo on the front and viscose from bamboo in its fiber declaration. Which identifies the finished fiber more precisely? The fiber declaration. A wool garment is machine washable. Is high dryer heat therefore acceptable? Only if the actual care instructions permit that treatment.

## Fiber quick-choice table

| Project | Good fibers | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Baby blanket | Machine-washable acrylic, cotton, superwash wool | Long fuzzy fibers that shed; hand-wash-only yarn |
| Dishcloth | Cotton, linen blends | Acrylic does not absorb water well |
| Winter hat | Wool, wool blends, acrylic | 100% cotton is heavy and less elastic |
| Summer top | Cotton, linen, bamboo blends | Drapey fibers can grow when wet |
| Amigurumi | Cotton, acrylic | Fuzzy yarns hide stitches |
| Pet items | Machine-washable acrylic or blends | Loose loops and long fibers that can be chewed |
| Felted bag | 100% wool or other animal fiber not labeled superwash | Superwash wool is treated so it will not felt |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Superwash wool will not felt
> Superwash wool is treated to resist felting so it can be machine washed. That is great for socks and baby items, and useless for felting projects.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Cotton has no bounce
> Cotton stitches do not spring back like wool. Ribbing in cotton stays where you stretch it, so use fewer stitches or a smaller hook for snug cuffs.

## The burn test, with caution

Some crafters identify unknown fibers with a burn test: a short snippet is burned in a safe, fireproof dish to see whether it smells like hair (animal fiber), paper (plant fiber) or melts into a hard bead (synthetic). It is only a rough guide, blends confuse it, and it is a fire risk. Never do it near the yarn stash, near children, or without water at hand. When in doubt, treat an unknown yarn as delicate.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Keep one ball band from every yarn you love. In ten years you will be glad to know what it was.


> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Count chains from the front
> Count a foundation chain on the front, where each chain is a neat V. The slip knot and the loop on the hook are not counted.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — The bump on the back
> Working into the back bumps of a foundation chain gives a neat edge that looks like the top of a row.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> If the first row is a struggle, work it with a hook one size larger, then change back. Nobody will ever know.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Buy one ball of a new yarn and swatch it before buying a sweater's worth.
