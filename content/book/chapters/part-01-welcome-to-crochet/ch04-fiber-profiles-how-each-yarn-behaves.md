These profiles describe how each common fiber tends to behave in crochet. They are general tendencies. Spinning, twist, blends and finishing change how any yarn behaves, so the ball band and a washed swatch always have the final word (Chapters 3 and 65).

> [!STATUS]
> General fiber guidance for planning. It is not a laboratory comparison, and the care advice is not a substitute for the label.

## Animal fibers

### Wool

Elastic and springy, so it holds its shape well and recovers after stretching. It is warm, even when slightly damp. Wool that is not treated (non-superwash) felts with heat, moisture and agitation. **Crochet notes:** forgiving for beginners, blocks beautifully, and good for hats and garments. Some people find wool itchy next to the skin; finer wools such as merino are often softer.

### Merino

A fine wool, soft enough for many people to wear next to the skin. It is often sold as superwash. **Crochet notes:** excellent stitch definition. Superwash merino can relax and grow when wet, so swatch-wash it.

### Alpaca

Very warm and soft, with a gentle halo. It has less elasticity than wool, so heavy garments can lengthen. **Crochet notes:** use lighter stitches for garments. Blends with wool add bounce.

### Mohair

From the Angora goat. It has a long, fluffy halo and a light, warm fabric. **Crochet notes:** it hides stitch detail and is hard to frog. Use larger hooks and open stitches.

### Cashmere

Very soft and warm, and usually expensive. It can pill with wear. **Crochet notes:** best for small luxury accessories; often blended.

### Silk

Smooth, lustrous and strong, with little elasticity. **Crochet notes:** slippery, so weave ends in further. Silk drapes well in lace and shawls.

## Plant fibers

### Cotton

Absorbent, cool and crisp, with excellent stitch definition. It has little elasticity. **Crochet notes:** ideal for dishcloths, amigurumi and summer wear. Ribbing in cotton does not spring back much. Mercerized cotton is smoother and shinier.

### Linen

Strong and cool. It softens with washing and can feel stiff at first. **Crochet notes:** great for market bags and summer tops. It drapes more after laundering.

### Hemp

Strong and durable, and it softens with use. **Crochet notes:** good for bags and home items. It can feel rough on the hands at first.

## Regenerated and synthetic fibers

### Bamboo and other viscose (rayon)

Regenerated cellulose fibers. They are smooth and drapey with a sheen, and have little elasticity. **Crochet notes:** they can grow in length, especially when wet. Choose light stitches and swatch-wash.

### Acrylic

Affordable, light and widely available in many colors. Usually machine washable (check the label). Little natural elasticity. **Crochet notes:** a great practice yarn and a common choice for blankets and toys. Heat can permanently relax ("kill") acrylic, so test steam on a swatch.

### Nylon (polyamide)

Very strong and durable. It is often blended with wool for sock yarn. **Crochet notes:** it adds wear resistance to blends.

### Polyester

Durable and colorfast. It is used in many chenilles and novelty yarns. **Crochet notes:** chenilles can "worm" (loops pull out). Keep stitches compact.

## Choosing at a glance

| Need | Look for | Avoid or test |
|---|---|---|
| Stretch and recovery | Wool, wool blends | 100% cotton, viscose |
| Absorbency | Cotton, linen | Acrylic |
| Machine washable | Acrylic, superwash wool, cotton (check label) | Non-superwash wool |
| Drape | Silk, viscose, alpaca, linen | Stiff, tightly twisted cottons |
| Felting | Non-superwash wool and other animal fibers | Superwash, synthetics |
| Budget practice yarn | Acrylic | Fuzzy or textured yarns |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Read the blend percentages
> "Wool blend" can mean anything from 10% to 90% wool. The percentages on the label tell you how the yarn will behave.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Touch the yarn to your neck in the shop. If it itches there, it will itch in a scarf.
