Yarn weight usually describes a thickness category, not the mass of the ball. A 100 g ball can contain a short length of bulky yarn or a long length of fine yarn. To understand what you are buying, read the thickness category, total mass, total length, fiber content, and yarn structure together.

## The category system is a starting map

The Craft Yarn Council system uses categories 0 through 7. These describe broad groups rather than exact diameters. A category can contain yarns that make noticeably different fabrics. The names lace, super fine, fine, light, medium, bulky, super bulky, and jumbo are useful shopping language, but they do not replace a gauge sample.

![yarn_label (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/yarn_label.png)

| Category | General name | Typical names you may encounter |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Lace | Lace, some fine crochet threads |
| 1 | Super fine | Sock, fingering, baby |
| 2 | Fine | Sport, baby |
| 3 | Light | DK, light worsted |
| 4 | Medium | Worsted, aran |
| 5 | Bulky | Chunky, craft, rug |
| 6 | Super bulky | Super chunky, some roving-style yarns |
| 7 | Jumbo | Jumbo, very large specialty yarns |

Names in the last column are approximate commercial language. “Baby” describes a marketing use as well as appearing in thickness terminology. “Aran” and “worsted” are not perfectly interchangeable across every manufacturer. “Roving” may describe a yarn’s construction rather than a single thickness. Confirm the actual label and sample.

This book does not reproduce the Council’s official category symbols or its full recommended hook-and- gauge chart. The table is a text cross-reference. ## Ply has more than one meaning

A plied yarn consists of component strands twisted together. You may be able to separate the end of a scrap and count those strands. A four-strand yarn is not automatically thicker than a two-strand yarn; the component strands may be much finer.

Regional labels such as 4-ply can also function as conventional thickness descriptions. Do not assume that a regional “8-ply” label means eight visibly separable strands or maps exactly onto a particular US category. Read the gauge and length-to-mass information. A translation chart can narrow the search but should never be the final substitution test.

Do not untwist or cut a working project to investigate its structure. Use a short scrap. If the yarn is a chainette tube, a blown structure, or chenille, searching for ordinary plies may be the wrong question entirely.

## Twist changes handling

Twist helps hold fibers and component strands together. A cohesive yarn often gives a cleaner entry point for the hook. A loosely twisted yarn can spread apart as the hook enters. Whether that becomes a problem depends partly on hook shape, working angle, and the direction in which the yarn is handled.

![yarn_anatomy (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/yarn_anatomy.png)

More twist does not automatically mean better yarn. A firm twist can support definition and resilience, while a softer construction may give a fabric the designer wants. Judge the intended use. A delicate halo yarn and a crisp cable yarn are solving different design problems.

If the yarn repeatedly splits, pause with the hook at the insertion point. Open the stitch with the supporting hand. Insert through the intended opening rather than forcing the head against a strand. Try a different hook profile on a small sample. When several plies have already been trapped, undo the split stitch; do not pull harder until the damage becomes permanent.

## Common constructions

A singles yarn is not plied in the ordinary sense. It can show attractive color and softness, but some lightly spun singles are less tolerant of abrasion or repeated unravelling. A multi-ply yarn combines component strands. A chainette yarn has a knitted or chained tubular construction rather than a simple bundle of twisted plies. Blown yarns can hold fibers within a supporting structure. Ribbon and tape yarns are flatter and can twist visibly as you work.

Bouclé creates loops or curls on its surface. Eyelash yarn has projecting strands. Brushed yarns have a halo. These surfaces can obscure stitch tops and trap neighboring stitches during unravelling. Carrying a smooth companion strand can sometimes help the fabric’s visibility or stability, but it also changes gauge, hand, and material consumption. Swatch the combined yarn rather than calling it the same material with a helpful extra thread.

Chenille has pile held by a core structure. Pulling or repeatedly dragging a tail can strip pile or damage the core in some products. The soft visible thickness may not equal the strength of the underlying core. Inspect a scrap and follow the manufacturer’s care and handling advice.

## Work with chenille without relying on brute force

First make a small chain and a few rows in the chosen yarn. Test whether a gentle pull causes shedding or exposes the core. Identify stitch tops with markers as you work rather than trying to count a large fuzzy piece later. If you cannot see the opening, feel it with a finger and guide the hook deliberately; stabbing through the pile can catch the wrong strand.

An adjustable ring can be difficult to close when pile creates friction. A proposed workaround is to form the ring with a compatible smooth strand and crochet the chunky yarn around it. That creates a second material in the center. It must be tested for strength, compatibility, laundering, and retention of the tail. It is a candidate technique, not a verified universal solution.

Do not choose the assisting strand solely because it is thin. A weak sewing thread can cut into or fail against a heavier construction. A contrasting strand can show through after closure. If a test ring does not close without damaging the materials, use a chain start or another construction that avoids the problem. Chapter 51 contains the dedicated test protocol.

Chenille may also develop loose surface loops, often called worming. Corrective treatment depends on the yarn and construction. Do not assume one tighter hook or one finishing knot prevents every instance. Make a sample, handle and launder it, and inspect whether loops migrate before investing in a large item.

## Compare length per unit mass

Divide length by mass to describe a useful purchasing comparison. A yarn with 200 m in 100 g has 2 m per gram. Another with 150 m in 100 g has 1.5 m per gram. That difference may reflect thickness, fiber density, construction, or a combination. It does not prove the yarns belong to different categories or will make different gauge, but it warns you to investigate.

![wpi (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/wpi.png)

Keep the units consistent. Do not divide yards by grams for one yarn and meters by grams for another, then compare the numerical answers as though they share a unit. You may use either unit system as long as both candidates use the same one.

For a rough comparison of unknown yarn, wraps per inch can be helpful. Wrap the yarn side by side around a ruler over a measured span without compressing or stretching it. Divide the number of wraps by the span in inches. Fuzzy and compressible yarns give particularly variable results. Use WPI as a clue for a sample, not proof of interchangeability.

## Holding strands together

Two strands held together behave as a combined yarn, but their thickness categories do not add as ordinary numbers. Two category-2 yarns do not become category 4 by arithmetic. Two equal strands add cross- sectional material, while their arrangement, compression, and fiber density affect apparent thickness and fabric.

If two identical strands are carried throughout, each meter of crocheting feed consumes approximately one meter from each supply. A project requiring 400 m of the combined feed therefore uses approximately 400 m from each strand, or 800 m total, before extra allowances. If the strands come from different yarns, calculate each supply separately.

Keep two balls feeding freely. If one catches, it can form short floats or tighten inside the fabric while the other stays loose. Check that the hook catches both strands at every stitch. A missed strand can leave a visible loop even when the stitch count is correct.

## Texture comparison exercise

Choose a smooth yarn and a textured yarn of broadly comparable intended use. Make ten single crochets for four rows in each. Mark every stitch in the first row if necessary. Compare how readily you can identify the top V, count rows, and undo one stitch without snagging.

Write down which tasks were easy and which were difficult. If you want to make amigurumi, can you inspect for gaps? If you want lace, can the eye distinguish openings from surface fuzz? If you want to reuse the yarn, does the sample tolerate unravelling? These questions connect the yarn’s structure to the project instead of treating texture as decoration alone.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Ball size is a terrible yardage calculator. A large airy ball can contain less length than a compact smaller one. Read the length.
>
> When carrying two strands, put the hook down occasionally and look for an isolated strand loop. It is easier to repair that loop while it is one row old.
>
> With fluffy yarn, markers are a visibility tool. Placing them now saves a future counting session conducted mostly by squinting and negotiation.

## Check your understanding

A 50 g ball contains 125 m. Its length-to-mass ratio is 2.5 m/g. A 100 g ball containing 250 m has the same ratio. That does not establish matching fiber behavior or gauge. Two strands held together for 300 m require approximately 300 m of each strand, not 150 m of each.

## Construction and what it means for crochet

| Construction | How it behaves | Tips |
|---|---|---|
| Plied (2 or more strands twisted) | Even, round, good stitch definition | The easiest yarn to learn with |
| Single-ply | Soft, can pill or split | Use a smooth hook; avoid over-twisting by turning work |
| Chainette (knitted tube) | Light, bouncy, can snag | Insert the hook carefully |
| Chenille | Velvety, can "worm" (loops pull out) | Keep stitches compact; check care label |
| Bouclé / textured | Hides stitches; hard to frog | Mark stitches often; not for beginners |
| Tape or ribbon | Flat; may twist | Let the ball dangle to untwist |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Crochet adds or removes twist
> Depending on how you wrap the yarn, crochet can add twist or remove it. If your yarn starts kinking or splitting, let the ball or project dangle to untwist from time to time.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Two strands held together is a new yarn
> Holding two strands together makes a thicker yarn, but not always twice as thick. Swatch the doubled yarn to find its real gauge.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> If you cannot see your stitches, you cannot count them. Save the fancy textured yarns for projects with few stitches and simple shapes.
