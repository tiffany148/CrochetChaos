Every experienced crocheter carries a pocketful of small habits that save hours, yarn and tears. None of them is complicated. Most were learned the hard way, at the end of a long evening, while staring at a blanket that had somehow grown three inches wider at one end. This chapter collects them in one place, before you need them, and points you to the chapters where each idea is explained in full.

## Before you start any project

**Read the whole pattern first.** Skim from the materials list to the final edging before you make a slip knot. Notice where the pattern changes direction, divides for armholes, changes color, or asks for a stitch you have not tried. Five minutes of reading prevents the classic surprise of discovering on page three that the first row was meant to be worked into the back bumps of the chain.

**Make the gauge swatch, then wash it.** A swatch is not a punishment. It is the cheapest test you will ever run on a project. Make it larger than the gauge window, treat it the way the finished piece will be treated, let it dry, and only then measure (Chapter 16).

**Check the dye lot on every ball.** Skeins of the same color name can come from different dye baths. The difference may be invisible in the store and obvious in daylight across a finished sweater. Buy enough of one lot at the start, and keep a label.

**Gather the small tools.** Locking stitch markers, a blunt tapestry needle with an eye that fits the yarn, small scissors, and a flexible tape measure. If the project is worked in the round, have at least one marker in a color that stands out from the yarn.

## Counting without losing your mind

**Count at the end of every row or round until you trust the pattern.** Most patterns print the expected count in parentheses at the end of a line, such as "(36 sts)". That number is a checkpoint, not decoration. If your count disagrees, stop and find out why before working the next row; one stray stitch becomes a slanted edge very quickly.

**Count in groups.** Place a marker every 10 or 20 stitches in a long foundation chain or first row. Recounting 10 is easy; recounting 180 is how evenings disappear.

**Know whether the turning chain counts.** In many double crochet patterns, "ch 3 (counts as dc)" means the chain is the first stitch of the row and the last stitch of the following row is worked into the top of that chain. In others, the chain does not count and you work the first stitch into the very first stitch. Both are correct; mixing them up is what makes edges grow or shrink. Write the convention on a sticky note beside the pattern.

**Mark the first and last stitch of the row.** The edge stitches are the ones most often missed or doubled. A marker in each tells you exactly where the row begins and ends, even when the turning chain hides the first stitch.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
> When the count is off by one, the culprit is almost always at an edge: the last stitch of a row was skipped (it hides under the turning chain), or a stitch was worked into the turning-chain space by mistake. Check the edges before you rip out the middle.
>
> A locking marker clipped through the live loop is the best pause button in crochet. Put one there every time you put your work down, especially if a cat lives in your house.
>
> Use a contrasting scrap of smooth yarn as a "running marker": lay it between the last stitch of one round and the first of the next, flip it forward and back as you pass, and you will have a visible spine of round boundaries you can count later.

## Tension, comfort and speed

**Relax the hand that holds the yarn.** Tension comes from a steady, light drag on the working yarn, not from a death grip. If your fabric is stiff and your forearm aches, loosen the yarn-hand first and let the hook size do its job.

**Form loops on the shaft, not the throat.** Each new loop should settle on the full-diameter part of the hook before you start the next stitch. A loop pulled down onto the narrow throat will be too small, and the next insertion will be a fight (Chapter 2).

**Your first and last rows can differ.** Many beginners crochet tighter at the start of a project and looser once relaxed. A swatch made on the first evening may not match the work three weeks later. Measure the project itself as you go.

**Change hands, not just hooks.** If you take breaks, stretch your fingers and shoulders. Persistent pain, tingling or numbness is a signal to stop and get advice, not something to crochet through.

## Yarn tricks that feel like magic

**Pull from the center.** Most center-pull balls and cakes have a tail hidden inside. Feed from the center and the ball stays put instead of rolling under the sofa. If you cannot find the inner tail, reach in and pull out a small handful of yarn (sometimes called "yarn barf"); the tail is in there.

**Leave long tails where you will sew.** If a piece will be seamed, leave a beginning or ending tail long enough to sew that seam. You save a join and a pair of ends to weave in.

**Weave ends in three directions.** Weave a tail in one direction for an inch or so, turn and go back a short way, then change direction again before trimming. Changing direction locks the end into the fabric better than a single straight pass, especially with slippery fibers.

**Split-ply weaving for slippery yarn.** With cotton, bamboo or silk, pass the needle through the plies of the stitches on the wrong side, not just between stitches. The friction holds the end.

**Keep a "yarn passport".** Tape a short snip of yarn and the ball band to a card for each project. When you need to repair, reorder or wash the piece, every answer is on one card.

> [!GRANNY] Granny’s Wisdom
> I never trusted my memory with a project. I trusted a pencil. Every time I changed hooks, skipped a row, or added a stitch to make something fit, I wrote it down in the margin of the pattern. Twenty years later I could still make a matching sleeve. The notes in the margin are the real pattern; the printed one is just where you started.

## Working in rounds

**Mark the first stitch of every round.** Whether you join or spiral, move the marker up each round. In a spiral, the beginning of the round drifts slightly, and the marker is the only reliable boundary (Chapter 10).

**Joined rounds leave a seam; spirals leave a jog.** Joined rounds with a turning chain produce a visible diagonal or vertical line where each round begins. Spirals avoid that line but create a small step at the color change. Choose the one your design can hide.

**Stagger increases.** In a flat circle of single crochet, placing the increase in the same spot every round makes the circle grow into a hexagon. Shift the increase position from round to round (for example, by working half of the plain stitches before the first increase on alternate rounds) to keep the outline round.

**Hats have a magic number of their own.** A hat crown is finished when the flat circle's diameter is about the hat's finished circumference divided by π (about 3.14). After that, stop increasing and work even. Chapter 43 gives the full method and tables.

## Color, joins and ends

**Change color on the last yarn over.** Work the last stitch in the old color until one yarn over remains, then complete it with the new color. The loop on the hook is now the new color and the next stitch starts clean (Chapter 11).

**Carry or cut?** For stripes two rows deep, you can usually carry the unused color up the side edge. For wider stripes, cut it; long carried strands snag and pull.

**Crochet over tails.** When you join a new color, lay both tails along the top of the row and work the next several stitches over them. Fewer ends to weave later, and they are anchored.

**Plan granny squares on paper first.** Lay out squares on a grid, by hand or using the layouts in Chapter 28, and photograph the layout before you join. Then join in the order the photo shows.

## Finishing like you mean it

**Block before you seam.** Pieces that have been blocked to their measurements line up better, and seams stay flatter (Chapter 63).

**Use the right seam for the job.** Mattress-style seams for flat, nearly invisible joins on row edges; slip-stitch seams for firm structure; single crochet seams when you want a decorative ridge (Chapter 62).

**Edge with an odd but deliberate count.** Around corners, three stitches in the corner stitch (or a corner chain space) keep a single crochet border flat. Along row ends, try a short test section and measure: too many stitches ruffle, too few pull in.

**Give it a label.** Sew or tie a care label to gifts. "Hand wash cool, dry flat" saves a lovingly made sweater from the dryer.

## Twenty-five quick tips that save real time

1. Keep a spare hook one size up and one size down next to you when swatching.
2. Store hooks by millimeter size, not by color or letter; letters vary by brand.
3. Photograph your work at the end of each session. It is a free progress record.
4. Highlight your size in a multi-size pattern before you start.
5. Copy the pattern for personal use and mark it up; keep the original clean.
6. Put a marker in the last stitch of the row before turning if you tend to miss it.
7. Rewind frogged yarn loosely around your hand or a cardboard tube; do not wind it tight.
8. Let crinkled frogged yarn relax: a gentle steam or a damp skein dried under light tension (where the fiber allows) straightens it.
9. Keep a magnet or a small dish for metal hooks and needles during work.
10. Work the first row of a big blanket, then measure it before going further.
11. Use a lifeline before risky lace: a smooth contrasting thread drawn through the tops of a good row gives you a safe place to rip back to.
12. Measure lengths with the piece lying flat, not hanging.
13. Weave ends in before blocking so the blocking settles them.
14. Count chains on the smooth front side, where each chain is a clear V.
15. Make the foundation chain with a hook one size larger if your first row always pulls in.
16. Or use a foundation stitch (such as foundation single crochet) for a stretchier start.
17. For amigurumi, use a hook smaller than the yarn label suggests so stuffing cannot show through.
18. Before you stuff, turn any amigurumi piece so the intended right side is out.
19. Keep a running list of abbreviations a new pattern uses; check its key, not memory.
20. When a pattern is ambiguous, look for the stitch count at the end of the row; it usually reveals the intent.
21. Work in good light; dark yarn needs more of it than you think.
22. Use a removable marker on the right side of the fabric as soon as you know which side that is.
23. Write the date and hook size on a tag pinned to every swatch.
24. Never cut the yarn until the piece is measured and approved.
25. Finish one project in a while. The joy of completion is fuel for the next one.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
> The yarn label is a suggestion. The hook that gives you the right fabric and the right gauge is the right hook, even if it is two sizes away from the label.
>
> A pattern’s "approximately 450 yards" assumes the designer’s gauge. If your fabric is denser, buy an extra ball of the same dye lot; leftovers are cheaper than a mismatched sleeve.
>
> If your rows are slanting, you may be working into the turning chain at one edge and skipping it at the other. Pick one convention and mark it.

> [!HINT]
> When you cannot see your stitches, change the conditions, not your patience: lighter yarn, smooth fiber, a larger hook, better light, and a plain background on your lap.


> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Same hook all project
> Finish a project with the same hook you started with. Even two hooks of the same size from different brands can change gauge.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Rest your hands
> Stop and stretch your fingers, wrists and shoulders every half hour or so. If crocheting causes pain that does not ease with rest, ask a health professional.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Practice joins on a swatch
> Try the slip-stitch join, the standing stitch and the invisible join on one practice swatch. Then you can choose the right one in a real project.

> [!HINT] Russian join for no ends
> A Russian join threads each yarn end back into itself to join two balls without a knot. It works best with wool and other grippy yarns; test it on slippery yarn first.
