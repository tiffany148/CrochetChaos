Feeling nervous is normal. Every crocheter you admire once held a hook like a chopstick and wondered which end of the yarn to use. This chapter is the friendly first hour: what to buy, how to hold everything, the handful of words you will see on every page, and a tiny first project you can finish this week. Read it once, then keep it open beside you while you practice the photo how-tos in Chapter 9.

## What to buy first (and what to skip for now)

You need very little. Buy these, and nothing else until you have finished your first small project.

| Buy this | Why | What to look for on the label |
|---|---|---|
| One ball of medium-weight yarn | Thick enough to see every stitch, thin enough to handle easily | The number **4** in a little yarn-ball symbol ("medium", "worsted" or "aran"); smooth, not fuzzy |
| A light, solid color | Dark, speckled and fuzzy yarns hide your stitches, so you can't count them | Pale pink, mint, yellow, light blue, cream |
| Acrylic or cotton | Acrylic is soft, cheap and forgiving; cotton makes useful dishcloths but is a little less stretchy | "100% acrylic" or "100% cotton" |
| A 5.5 mm crochet hook (US I-9) | The size usually suggested for medium yarn; the yarn label will name a size, so use that if it differs | The metric size in mm is printed on the hook |
| Small scissors | For cutting the yarn | Any sharp pair |
| A blunt tapestry (yarn) needle | For weaving in ends | A large eye and a rounded tip |
| Four locking stitch markers | To mark the first and last stitch of every row | Plastic clip-on markers; a safety pin or a scrap of yarn also works |

**Skip for now:** black yarn, eyelash and novelty yarns, chenille, very thin thread, tiny steel hooks, and kits with 40 hooks. You can collect those later. If your hands get sore, an ergonomic (soft-grip) handle is worth the extra money.

> [!GRANNY] Granny’s Wisdom
> Buy the cheap, cheerful yarn for learning. You are going to pull it out and redo it, maybe ten times, and that is exactly how learning works. Save the cashmere for when your hands know what they are doing.

## Holding the hook

Pick up the hook in your dominant hand. There are two common holds, and both are correct:

- **Pencil hold:** hold the hook like a pencil, between thumb and index finger, resting on the middle finger.
- **Knife hold:** hold it like a dinner knife, with your hand over the handle and your index finger along the top.

Hold it on the flat thumb rest (the flattened part in the middle), not at the very end. The hook's head should face you or point slightly down. Try both holds for a few minutes and keep the one that feels easier. See the figure of hook holds in Chapter 9.

## Holding the yarn

Your other hand feeds the yarn and holds the work. Here is one simple, popular way to wrap it. There are many others; any wrap that lets the yarn slide smoothly is right.

1. Hold your hand palm up. Lay the yarn coming from the ball across your palm.
2. Wrap it once loosely around your little finger, so it can still slide.
3. Bring it across the palm and up over the top of your index finger.
4. Pinch your work (the slip knot, then your chain) between your thumb and middle finger, just below the hook.
5. Lift your index finger a little to make the yarn a bit taut between the finger and the hook. That taut stretch is where the hook grabs the yarn.

If the yarn will not slide, unwrap the little finger. If it slides away uncontrolled, add the wrap back or wrap twice. Your tension will settle down after a few practice rows; nobody's is even on the first day.

## Seven words you will see on every page

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Working yarn** | The yarn that leads to the ball. This is the one you crochet with. |
| **Tail** | The short loose end at the start. You do not crochet with it; you weave it in at the end. |
| **Loop on the hook** | The loop that always sits on your hook. It is **never** counted as a stitch. |
| **Yarn over (yo)** | Wrap the working yarn over the hook from back to front. |
| **Stitch** | One finished unit, such as one single crochet. Each stitch has a little **V** on top. |
| **Row / round** | One trip across (row) or once around (round). |
| **Turning chain** | The chain or chains you make at the start of a row to reach the height of the new stitches. |

## How to count your stitches

Counting is the most important beginner skill, and it is easy once you know what to look for.

1. Lay your work flat with the hook at the top right (left-handers: top left).
2. Look along the top edge. Each stitch has a sideways **V**, like a little chain lying on its side.
3. Count the Vs, starting from the stitch nearest the hook. **Do not count the loop on the hook.**
4. Do not count the turning chain unless your pattern says it counts (see the table below).
5. Put a stitch marker in the first and last stitch of every row. When you reach the marker, that is the last stitch.

Count at the end of every row while you are learning. If the number is wrong, it is far easier to fix one row than ten.

## Turning chains: how many, and do they count?

| Stitch you are about to work | Usual turning chain | Does it count as a stitch? |
|---|---|---|
| Single crochet (sc) | Chain 1 | Usually **no**. Work your first sc into the very first stitch. |
| Half double crochet (hdc) | Chain 2 | It depends; your pattern will tell you. In this book it does not unless stated. |
| Double crochet (dc) | Chain 3 | Often **yes**. Skip the first stitch and work into the top of the turning chain at the end of the next row. |
| Treble crochet (tr) | Chain 4 | Often **yes**. |

These are the usual numbers, not laws. A pattern always wins: if it says "ch 2 (does not count as a st)", do exactly that. The most common beginner mystery is fabric that gets wider or narrower, and it is nearly always the turning chain counted the wrong way, or a missed first or last stitch.

## The front and back of a stitch, and the right side of your work

- **Front loop and back loop.** Look at the top V of a stitch. The strand nearer to you is the **front loop**; the one farther away is the **back loop**. Unless a pattern says otherwise, insert your hook under **both** loops.
- **Front and back of a stitch.** In single crochet, the front of a stitch shows a little V on its face; the back shows a horizontal bump. When you work back and forth in rows, the fabric shows fronts on one row and backs on the next, which gives plain single crochet its look.
- **Right side (RS) and wrong side (WS).** The right side is the side that will show. In rounds, the side facing you as you work is usually the right side. In rows, a pattern will tell you which row is the right side; put a marker or a scrap of yarn on that side so you don't lose track.

## Your first project: a practice square that becomes a dishcloth

Make the First Rectangle practice piece in Chapter 9, then keep going. If you are using cotton yarn, keep working rows of 12 single crochets until the piece is a square (about as tall as it is wide). That is a real, usable dishcloth or coaster, and it is a perfect first make. Fasten off and weave in the ends as described in Chapter 11.

A relaxed first-week plan:

| Day | Practice | You are done when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Slip knot and chains | You can make 20 even chains and count them |
| 2 | First row of single crochet | You have a row of 12 sc and can count the Vs |
| 3 | Turning and Row 2 | Row 2 also has 12 sc |
| 4 | Rows 3 to 8 | Every row has 12; your edges are fairly straight |
| 5 | Keep going to a square | The piece is square |
| 6 | Fasten off and weave in ends | No tails are showing |
| 7 | Start a second square, or try half double crochet | You smile at the first one |

## What is normal at the beginning (so don't worry)

- Wobbly edges and a slightly wavy first row.
- A tight first chain that is hard to work into. (Chain more loosely, or use a hook one size bigger just for the chain.)
- Sore hands after twenty minutes. Take breaks, stretch, and loosen your grip.
- Pulling your work out and starting again. Crocheters call this **frogging** ("rip it, rip it"). It is part of the craft, not a failure.
- Your second piece looking twice as good as your first.

> [!FIX] Fix It · I lost count, or my piece is getting wider
> Stop at the end of the row and count the Vs. If the number is right, carry on. If not, pull out the row (gently pull the working yarn until you are back at the start of that row), put the loop back on your hook, and work the row again with a marker in the first and last stitch. Check that you did not crochet into the turning chain, and that you did not skip the last stitch.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That! · Put the loop on a marker
> When you put your crochet down, slip a stitch marker (or a safety pin) through the loop on the hook. Your work cannot unravel, the cat cannot pull it apart, and you can pick it up again exactly where you left off.
