Your first goal is repeatable movement. Speed arrives later, usually when you stop trying to make it arrive. Begin with a short piece that you can count easily. A crooked practice square is useful if you can explain why its edges changed.

This chapter uses US terminology. US single crochet is UK double crochet. A chain is a chain in both systems. The practice pattern’s turning chain does not count as a stitch. Every row contains 12 actual single crochets.

## Find a hold that leaves room to move

Hold the hook in your dominant hand if that feels natural. A pencil hold rests the handle much like a writing tool. A knife hold places more of the hand over the handle. Hybrids are common. Choose a hold that lets the head rotate to catch yarn without forcing your fingers to perform every movement separately.

![holds (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/holds.png)

The other hand manages the working yarn and supports the fabric near the stitch you are making. Rest the strand over or around fingers so it feeds with light resistance. Do not wrap it so tightly that the fingers are constricted. Slide your supporting fingers along the fabric as the row grows. Holding the work several inches below the hook makes it harder to guide the head into the next stitch.

Tension is the controlled resistance that keeps new loops from becoming wildly different sizes. It is not the same as pulling tightly. A loose but consistent crocheter can match a gauge with an appropriate hook. A tight, inconsistent crocheter still has inconsistent fabric. Aim for a loop that slides along the shaft without being squeezed into the throat.

## Make an adjustable starting loop

Leave a beginning tail long enough to weave in later; about 6 inches or 15 cm is a practical starting choice for a small smooth-yarn sample, with more needed for bulky or slippery yarn and particular finishing methods. Form a loop in the yarn. Pull a bend of the working strand through that loop. Place the new loop on the hook and gently tighten the knot below it.

**Step by step: Slip knot**

The slip knot puts the first loop on your hook. It is not a stitch, so never count it. The "ball yarn" is the yarn that leads to the ball; the "tail" is the short loose end. Same name in UK terms.

Check that the loop on the hook can move. It should neither choke the shaft nor hang several inches below it. Pull the working yarn to see which strand adjusts the loop. If your slip knot was formed in the opposite orientation, it may adjust differently; what matters here is that you can control the starting loop without turning it into a fixed knot on the hook.

The slip knot anchors the beginning but does not count as a chain. Keep the tail separate from the working yarn. If the first few stitches shorten the tail rapidly, stop: you may be crocheting with the wrong strand.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom — The two-finger slip knot
> Here's a quicker way to make a slip knot. Wrap the yarn around two fingers, twice. With your other hand, grab the back loop (the one closest to your hand) and pull it over the front loop, three times. Pull the loop off your fingers, then tighten the yarn down until the loop is the size you need.

## Yarn over and draw through

With one loop on the hook, wrap the yarn over the hook from back to front. This is a yarn over (yo). Turn the hook head slightly down so it catches the yarn, then pull the yarn through the loop on the hook. You have made one chain (ch): a new link hangs below the hook, and one loop is still on the hook.

Let the new loop reach the full shaft before making the next chain. If you tighten it around the narrow throat, the following draw-through may be difficult. If the hook catches only some plies, undo that chain now and remake it. Splitting becomes more difficult to diagnose after several rows have been added.

Repeat slowly until you have 13 completed chain links. Count from the hook end, excluding the live loop. Place a marker in the thirteenth chain if that helps distinguish it from the starting knot. Lay the chain down without stretching it. A chain that resembles a rigid cord is probably too tight for an easy first row.

## Work your first single crochet

Count to the second chain from the hook (do not count the loop on the hook). Push the hook into that chain. For your first try, go under the top strand of the chain. Going into the bump on the back of the chain is also fine, but use the same place for every stitch in this practice piece.

Wrap the yarn over the hook and pull it back through the chain. Check: two loops on the hook. Wrap the yarn over again and pull it through both loops. Check: one loop on the hook. You have made one US single crochet (sc).

Move to the next chain toward the tail. Make another single crochet. Continue until every remaining chain has been used. Because you began with 13 chains and skipped one, you should have 12 single crochets. The skipped chain gives height at the edge; it is not counted as a single crochet in this exercise.

Place a marker through the top V of the first single crochet and another through the top V of the twelfth. Count the completed Vs between them, including both marked stitches. Do not include the live loop, the starting knot, or the small edge chain.

## Turn and begin Row 2

Make 1 chain (ch 1). Then turn your work over like the page of a book, so you can crochet back along the row. This chain is the turning chain. It lifts the yarn up to the height of the next row, and in single crochet it does not count as a stitch.

![turning_rows (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/turning_rows.png)

![Decorative generated crochet art](../../assets/art/fill/p01-weight-lineup.jpg)

**Step by step: Turning chain and turning your work**

At the end of each row you add a turning chain to lift the yarn up to the height of the next row, then turn the work so you can crochet back the other way. For single crochet the turning chain is 1 chain, and it is not counted as a stitch. Your first stitch of the new row goes into the last stitch you made. Same terms in UK patterns.

Look at the top of the row. Each stitch has a sideways V on top. Find the V right under the turning chain: it belongs to the last single crochet you made. Push the hook under both strands of that V and make one single crochet. Move your edge marker into this new stitch. This is the first stitch of Row 2.

Make one single crochet in every remaining actual stitch. The last one goes into the stitch marked at the far edge. Move that marker up after making it. Count 12 single crochets. Do not add a stitch into the turning chain after the marked final stitch.

Repeat the same procedure for eight rows in total. You can count rows along the side, but early fabric is easier to track with a written checklist. Write the row number when you finish it, rather than relying on the number you intended to finish before someone asked where the scissors went.

## First Rectangle practice pattern

Status: original instructional exercise; stitch totals checked by arithmetic; not physically sample-tested for this edition. There is no promised finished size or yardage. It is a learning sample rather than a fitted object.

Materials: a small quantity of smooth yarn, a compatible hook, two locking markers, scissors, and a blunt yarn needle. Gauge is not prescribed. Use a relaxed fabric that lets the hook enter without force.

Foundation: ch 13.

Row 1: sc in second ch from hook and in each remaining ch; 12 sc. Mark first and last sc.

Rows 2–8: ch 1, turn; sc in each of 12 sc. Move edge markers to first and last new sc. The ch-1 never counts as a stitch.

Finish: cut the working yarn leaving a tail suitable for weaving. Draw the cut end completely through the remaining live loop and snug it. Thread the tail onto a blunt yarn needle. Pass it through the body of several nearby stitches without pulling the edge out of shape. Reverse direction through a different path to improve retention, taking care not to create a hard visible lump. Trim only after checking that the tail is secure and the fabric lies comfortably.

Do not interpret this finishing example as a universal number of inches or stitches that guarantees security for every yarn. Slippery fibers, heavy objects, and frequently washed items need testing of their actual finishing method.

## Diagnose what happened

| What you see | First check | Useful correction |
|---|---|---|
| Sample widens every row | Extra stitch in turning chain or at row start | Return to last 12-stitch row and follow markers |
| Sample narrows every row | Missed first or last actual stitch | Identify and mark both edge Vs |
| Bottom edge draws inward | Foundation chain much tighter than fabric | Remake chain more loosely or try a larger chain hook |
| Hook catches tiny fibers | Split plies or hook angle | Withdraw, locate opening, reinsert cleanly |
| Every draw-through is difficult | Loops tightened on throat | Let loops settle on shaft; relax yarn feed |
| Loose vertical gaps appear | Uneven pull-up loop height | Support work closer to hook and compare movements |
| Correct count but slanted sides | Placement or tension variation | Inspect exact entry points; do not add stitches blindly |

A different count at the end of a row does not mean the whole sample must be discarded. Undo the current row until you reach the mistake, reinsert the hook in the live loop, and work forward again. If you cannot locate the error, unravel to the last row with a confirmed count. A marker and a note at that row make the decision much easier.

## Tension experiments that teach something specific

Make three separate chains of 20 using the same yarn and hook. For the first, pull firmly after every chain. For the second, allow a relaxed loop on the shaft. For the third, deliberately make very large loops. Compare the length and ease of insertion. The middle chain is often more manageable, but the lesson is the relationship between loop size and fabric rather than a single ideal length.

Next, work four single crochet rows of 12 stitches while consciously holding the fabric close to the hook. Make a second sample while letting the work dangle. Compare control and fatigue. Support the larger project the way you supported the more comfortable sample.

If your gauge changes as you relax, that is useful information. Work a few warm-up rows before measuring a serious garment swatch. Your first tense row and your twentieth relaxed row should not be treated as interchangeable evidence.

## Right-handed and left-handed practice

Use the same stitch sequence with either hand. The hook enters the next available stitch in your working direction. A left-handed learner does not need to reverse the arithmetic or invent a new stitch name. When watching a demonstration, a matched-handed version can reduce the burden of mental mirroring.

Count independently of the apparent direction. Mark first and last stitches based on the order you worked them. If a written instruction refers to a garment’s left front, that usually means the wearer’s left, which is a different issue from which hand holds the hook.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Use the marker as a destination. When you reach it, make that stitch, move the marker, and stop. The little edge chain beyond it is not an invitation to create a bonus stitch.
>
> A larger hook for the foundation can help a tight lower edge, but it cannot correct a habit of tightening each loop until it squeaks. Address the movement as well as the tool.
>
> Do not trim tails immediately beside the finishing knot. The knot stops the live loop; woven tails help the end resist movement in the fabric. They perform related but different jobs.

## Check your understanding

The practice rectangle begins with 13 chains and has 12 single crochets in each row. After eight rows it contains 96 worked single crochet stitches, excluding the foundation and turning chains. That arithmetic checks the instructions; it does not prove that your particular sample contains 96 well-formed stitches. Count the actual work and inspect its edges.

You are ready for Chapter 10 when you can complete two successive 12-stitch rows, identify their first and last stitches, and explain whether the turning chain belongs in the stitch total. Even tension will continue improving with practice.

## Troubleshooting first stitches

| What you see | Likely cause | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation chain too tight to work into | Chains pulled tight on the throat of the hook | Chain with a hook one size larger, or slide each chain onto the wider shaft |
| Rows get wider | Extra stitch at the start or end | Count every row; check the first and last stitch |
| Rows get narrower | Missing the last stitch | Mark the last stitch of each row |
| Holes at row ends | Turning chain too tall or too loose | Try a shorter turning chain, or chain tighter |
| Wobbly, uneven stitches | Inconsistent yarn hold | Wrap the yarn the same way each time; practice a few rows |
| Hand cramps | Gripping the hook too hard | Relax the grip; try a knife hold or a pencil hold |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Pull the loop onto the shaft
> Tight stitches usually happen because each loop is made on the narrow throat of the hook. Slide the loop up onto the shaft before the next yarn-over, and your stitches will all be the shaft's size.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Tension is the yarn hand's job
> Your hook hand moves the hook; your other hand controls the yarn. If your stitches vary, look at the yarn hand first.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Practice with smooth, light-colored yarn
> A smooth worsted yarn in a light, solid color shows every loop. Dark, fuzzy or variegated yarn hides the stitches you are trying to learn.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Your first rectangle is not supposed to be pretty. It is supposed to teach your hands. Keep it; you will be proud of how far you have come.

## Left-handed notes

Left-handed crocheters work the same stitches as a mirror image: the hook in the left hand, stitches worked from left to right along the row. Charts are read in the mirror direction too. Many left-handed crocheters find it easiest to flip a right-handed photo or diagram, or to watch a video in mirror view. Written patterns do not change.


> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Read the notes first
> Pattern notes often hide key rules: whether the turning chain counts, which side is the right side, and where stitch counts change.

> [!HINT] Use a highlighter tape
> Removable highlighter tape on a printed pattern marks your row without damaging the page.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> When a pattern seems wrong, it usually isn't. Read the line again, slowly, out loud. Then check the designer's errata page.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Errata exist
> Many designers publish corrections online. Search for the pattern name plus 'errata' before starting a big project.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Swatch in the round for round projects
> Gauge worked in rows can differ from gauge in rounds. For a hat, swatch in rounds.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Note your changes on the pattern
> Write any change you make on the pattern itself, in pen. Next time you will make the same item, not a slightly different one.

> [!HINT] Count at the end of each row
> Count stitches at the end of every row for the first few rows of any new pattern.
