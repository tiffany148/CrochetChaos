Rows and rounds use the same basic stitches but organize them differently. In a row, you travel across an edge and usually turn. In a round, you travel around a center or tube. The place where one round ends and the next begins may be obvious, nearly invisible, or deliberately disguised. Marking that boundary is more reliable than trying to recognize it after the fact.

## Keep a row’s boundaries visible

For a flat single crochet rectangle, mark the top V of the first and last actual stitch. If the beginning chain does not count, work into the first actual stitch immediately below it. At the far edge, work the marked final actual stitch and stop. If the pattern says the beginning chain counts as a stitch, the row’s accounting changes: the chain represents one stitch, and the end of the following row usually includes its designated top.

Neither convention is universally correct. Problems arise when the crocheter begins under one rule and ends under another. Write the convention beside your row count. A double crochet sample may count a chain-3, whereas another double crochet design may use a non-counting chain-2 or an alternative turning stitch. Read the specific pattern rather than transferring a rule from a previous project.

Turning exposes the opposite face of the fabric. A stitch worked through the near loop on one row does not necessarily align with the same physical face on the next. If the design requires a ridge to remain on one public face, record the RS and follow the placement instructions for that row.

## Three ways to start circular work

A chain ring begins with several chains joined into a circle. Stitches are then worked into the central opening. This leaves a center whose initial size is set by the chain, although weaving the tail can sometimes reduce it. A short chain start may place the first round into one selected chain instead of an open ring.

An adjustable ring, often called a magic ring, forms the first round over a loop that can be tightened afterward. Its main advantage is adjustable center closure. Its main risk is assuming that tightening alone permanently secures the tail. It still needs an appropriate finishing method. Chapter 51 treats the ring, its alternatives, and the special problems of fragile chenille.

For this chapter’s round exercises, use a chain-2 start: make 2 chains, then work all first-round stitches into the second chain from the hook. That method gives a compact center without requiring the adjustable ring movement. The starting chain may remain visible. The exercise is about round tracking, not achieving a photo-perfect center.

## Joined rounds

A joined round finishes with a slip stitch into a specified stitch or beginning chain. A new beginning chain then establishes height for the next round. Whether you turn between rounds is a separate choice. A joined round does not automatically mean turned work, and a non-turned round does not automatically mean a spiral.

In the following joined single crochet exercise, the chain-1 does not count and the joining slip stitch does not count. Mark the first actual single crochet. At the end of the round, slip stitch into that marked single crochet. Do not work the next round into the joining slip stitch or the beginning chain.

Joined practice disk, original exercise; arithmetic checked, not physically sample-tested. Foundation: ch 2. Round 1: 6 sc in second ch from hook, mark first sc; sl st to first sc to join; 6 sc. Round 2: ch 1, 2 sc in each sc around, moving marker to first new sc; sl st to first sc; 12 sc. Round 3: ch 1, repeat [sc in next sc, 2 sc in next sc] six times; join to first sc; 18 sc. Round 4: ch 1, repeat [sc in each of next 2 sc, 2 sc in next sc] six times; join; 24 sc. Do not turn. Fasten off and secure both tails.

The counts are 6, 12, 18, and 24. The slip-stitch join may be visible as a seam. This is expected for the exercise. If the disk ruffles or cups, first verify counts and placement. Correct counts can still produce a non-flat disk with a particular yarn, tension, or stitch proportion; do not describe the recipe as physically verified for every combination.

## Continuous spirals

In a spiral, you do not join at the end of each round. After the last stitch of one round, make the next stitch directly into the first stitch of the previous round. The work advances as one continuous helix. A marker labels an accounting boundary inside that continuous path.

Use a locking marker in the first stitch of each round. When you reach it again, remove it, work the next round’s first stitch into that stitch, and place the marker through the top of the new first stitch. If the first instruction is an increase, put the marker in the first of the two new stitches, not between them.

Spiral practice disk, original exercise; arithmetic checked, not physically sample-tested. Ch 2. Round 1: 6 sc in second ch from hook; mark first sc; do not join. Round 2: 2 sc in each of 6 sc; 12 sc. Round 3: [sc in next sc, 2 sc in next sc] six times; 18 sc. Round 4: [sc in each of next 2 sc, 2 sc in next sc] six times; 24 sc. Move the first-stitch marker every round. Stop after the twenty-fourth stitch of Round 4. Fasten off; the edge may show a step because a spiral ends at a different height from where its accounting round began.

The same totals appear in both exercises, but the round boundaries differ. Do not insert a joining slip stitch into the spiral because the joined sample used one. Do not add an extra chain-1 at each spiral boundary. Those additions would change the construction.

## From a disk to a tube

If you continue adding enough increases around a disk, it expands. If you stop increasing and work one stitch into each stitch, the fabric begins to form sides. Several even rounds after the 24-stitch disk create a small cup or tube, depending on proportions. A single even round may not reveal the final shape immediately.

A tube begun from a foundation chain has a different construction. Chain the intended circumference, check that the chain is not twisted, and join according to the pattern. The first round is worked around that chain. A permanently twisted foundation produces a twisted tube; this can be deliberate in some designs but is a mistake when a straight cuff or body is intended.

To check for a twist, lay the chain on a table with its Vs oriented consistently before joining. For a long chain, a pattern may instead work the first row flat and join afterward, leaving a small seam to close. Use that alternative only when the instructions account for the edge and row height.

## Marker systems that remain understandable

One marker can identify the first stitch. Additional markers can separate repeat groups, designate corners, or label increases. Do not use the same color for every purpose if the piece will become complicated. Make a small key: teal marks round start, pink marks corners, yellow marks a fit checkpoint.

A running marker uses a contrasting scrap strand carried loosely between rounds at the boundary. It can preserve a visible history of round starts rather than only the current one. The strand must not be worked into the stitches or pulled tight. Remove it when it is no longer needed. It is a tracking aid, not a structural lifeline that automatically prevents unravelling.

For long rows, place a marker every 10 or 20 stitches. Decide whether the marker sits in the tenth stitch or after a group and record that rule. A marker inside stitch 20 and a marker in the space after stitch 20 are not identical counting conventions.

## Read counts as an audit trail

| Round | Input stitches used | New stitches made | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Center opening | 6 | Six stitches placed at center |
| 2 | 6 | 12 | Two new stitches in each old stitch |
| 3 | 12 | 18 | Six repeats consume 2 and produce 3 |
| 4 | 18 | 24 | Six repeats consume 3 and produce 4 |

The input and output columns help catch a subtle error: a repeat can have the correct final total yet be placed unevenly. If you accidentally work two increases side by side and omit an increase elsewhere, the count may still be 24. The disk’s shape and the positions of increases give additional evidence.

Counting in groups is especially useful in amigurumi. Before stuffing closes access to the inside, verify totals and inspect for split stitches or loose loops. A correct number does not prove correct construction; it is one check among several.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Mark the first actual stitch before it disappears into a busy round. “I’ll remember where it is” has probably caused more accidental spirals than any advanced technique ever has.
>
> Separate joining, turning, and beginning-chain rules in your notes. They answer three different questions: how the round closes, whether the fabric flips, and whether the height chain counts.
>
> If your joined tube gains one stitch every round, inspect the slip-stitch join. Its top can look like an inviting extra V. A marker in the first actual stitch helps you walk past the invitation.

## Check your understanding

In Round 4, each repeat consumes three old stitches and produces four new ones. Six repeats therefore consume 18 and produce 24. In the joined sample, neither the chain-1 nor the joining slip stitch adds to 24. In the spiral sample, there is no join to count at all.

Before moving on, work both samples far enough to recognize their boundaries. Record their counts, the presence or absence of a seam, whether you turned, and which chain convention you used. Those four observations belong beside any circular pattern you design later.

## Rows or rounds: which to use

| Shape | Best method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Rectangle, scarf, blanket | Rows | Easy to keep straight edges |
| Circle, coaster, crown of a hat | Joined rounds or spiral | Increases spread evenly from the center |
| Tube, hat body, sleeve | Rounds | No side seam |
| Amigurumi | Spiral rounds | No visible join line |
| Granny square | Joined rounds | Corners stay crisp |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Mark the first stitch, not the slip stitch
> In joined rounds, put the marker in the first real stitch of the round. The slip stitch that joins the round is not a stitch and is easy to work into by mistake.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — A spiral needs a jog fix
> Spirals end with a small step. To smooth it at the end, work a slip stitch into the next stitch or two, then fasten off and weave the tail along the edge.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Seams can twist in joined rounds
> Joined rounds worked in the same direction every time tend to make the join line drift to one side. Turning at the end of every round, or starting each round with a standing stitch, keeps it straighter.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> One marker for the start, a second color for anything else. A marker that means two things means nothing.

## Counting rounds in a spiral

Lay a strand of contrast yarn between stitches at the start of round 1, and flip it front to back at the same point each round. When you need to count, count the strand's crossings—each one is a round.
