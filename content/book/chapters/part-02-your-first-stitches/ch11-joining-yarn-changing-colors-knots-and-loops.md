Sooner or later every ball runs out, every stripe needs a new color, and every piece needs to be fastened off. How you handle those moments decides whether a finished project looks tidy or scrappy. This chapter gathers the joins, color changes, knots and loops that experienced crocheters use without thinking, and explains when each one is the right choice.

![joining_yarn (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/joining_yarn.png)

## Where to join a new ball

The best place to join new yarn is where the join will disappear. At the end of a row is easiest: the tails sit along an edge that will be seamed or bordered, and they are simple to weave. In the middle of a row, a join is fine if you work over the tails, but avoid placing it in lace, at a highly visible center front, or in a spot that will take strain, such as a strap or a handle.

Before you start a row, check whether the ball has enough yarn to finish it. A practical rule of thumb: a row of a given stitch uses roughly the same length every time, so measure one row's yarn on your swatch (unravel it and measure, or weigh the ball before and after on a scale that reads to a gram or better). If the remaining yarn is less than about one and a half rows' worth, join the new ball at the start of the row instead of gambling.

## Joining methods compared

| Method | How it works | Best for | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Join at row end | Complete the last stitch’s final yarn over with the new yarn | Stripes, flat pieces, seamed edges | Leaves two tails at the edge to weave |
| Work over tails | Lay both tails along the stitch tops and crochet over them | Solid fabric, sc, hdc, dc | Can show through lace or very fine fabric |
| Russian join | Thread each tail back through its own strand, forming interlocked loops | Smooth, plied yarn; no ends to weave | Adds a short double-thickness section; takes a needle and a minute |
| Spit (felted) splice | Overlap fraying ends, moisten, rub until they felt together | Non-superwash animal fibers that felt (e.g. many pure wools) | Will not work on superwash wool, cotton, acrylic or most blends |
| Magic knot | Two sliding knots pulled together, ends trimmed (step by step below) | Speed with grippy, wool-type or acrylic yarn; joining mid-row with no ends to weave | Leaves a small knot; can slip on silk, bamboo and some cottons; not for lace or reversible work |
| Square/overhand knot | Tied ends | Temporary joins you will undo | Lumps, can slip; generally avoided in finished work |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
> Never join a new ball with a knot in the middle of a baby blanket and trim it close. Knots migrate, little fingers find them, and a trimmed knot can open in the wash. Weave or splice instead.
>
> For the Russian join, use a needle with an eye just large enough for the yarn and thread back about 2 inches (5 cm) through the center of the plies. Too short and it can pull out; too long and the double section shows.
>
> A felted splice needs friction and moisture, not saliva specifically: a drop of water and brisk rubbing between your palms works. Test on a scrap first; if the yarn says "superwash," it will not felt.

## Joining yarn with a magic knot

The magic knot joins a new ball to the old one with two small knots that slide together and lock. There are no ends to weave in afterward, and you can join anywhere, even in the middle of a row. It is quick, and many crocheters love it for blankets, amigurumi and stash-busting stripes. It does leave a small knot in the yarn, so it is not the right join for every project.

**What you need:** the end of your old yarn, the start of your new yarn, and sharp scissors. Leave yourself plenty of tail: at least 6 inches (15 cm) of each yarn overlapping.

### Magic knot, step by step

1. Lay the old yarn (the strand coming from your work) and the new yarn side by side, overlapping by about 6 inches (15 cm), with the two tails pointing in opposite directions.
2. Take the tail of the **new** yarn. Wrap it around the **old** yarn and tie an ordinary overhand knot, so the knot hugs the old strand. Do not pull it tight yet; it should still slide.
3. Take the tail of the **old** yarn. Wrap it around the **new** yarn and tie an overhand knot around it in the same way.
4. Check: you now have two separate knots. Each knot is tied around the *other* yarn, never around its own strand.
5. Hold the two long strands (the old yarn leading back to your work and the new yarn leading to the new ball) and pull them firmly apart. The two knots slide toward each other.
6. Keep pulling until the knots meet and lock into one small, hard knot. Give it a strong tug, harder than you would ever pull while crocheting. If it moves, see "When a magic knot slips" below.
7. Trim both tails close to the knot, leaving about 1/8 inch (3 mm). On yarn that feels a little slick, leave closer to 1/4 inch (6 mm).
8. Crochet on as usual. If the knot arrives at the hook in the middle of a stitch, nudge it to the back (wrong side) of the fabric with your finger.

### When to use a magic knot

- Grippy yarns that hold a knot well: most wool, wool blends, acrylic and other "sticky" or slightly fuzzy yarns.
- Solid, dense fabric such as single crochet, half double and double crochet, where a tiny knot disappears inside the stitches.
- Amigurumi, where the inside of the toy is never seen.
- Blankets, scrap projects and stripes where you would otherwise have dozens of ends to weave.

### When NOT to use a magic knot

- **Slippery yarns.** Silk, bamboo and other viscose (rayon), and some smooth or mercerized cottons can let the knots slide apart, even when they felt tight. If you must use one, test-tug a sample knot first and leave slightly longer tails.
- **Lace and openwork.** In a mesh or lacy stitch there is nowhere for the knot to hide, and it will show.
- **Reversible pieces.** Scarves, shawls and blankets meant to look good on both sides may show the knot on one side.
- **Items that take strain or hard wear.** Straps, handles and bag bases rub and pull against the join. Choose a Russian join or a weave-in join instead.
- **When you are not sure.** Make a test knot in your project yarn, tug it hard, then work a few stitches over it. If it moves or pokes through, use another join.

### Trimming

Trim only after the tug test. Cut each tail close to the knot, leaving 1/8 inch (3 mm), or 1/4 inch (6 mm) on smoother yarn. Use sharp scissors so the tails do not fray, and never cut flush against the knot itself, or you may nick the strand that holds everything together.

> [!FIX] Fix It · When a magic knot slips
> **If the knots will not slide together,** one knot was tied around its own strand. Untie it and tie it again around the other yarn.
>
> **If the knot slides apart when you tug it,** cut the knot out and start again with longer tails, about 1 inch (2.5 cm) beyond each knot. On slippery yarn, wrap the tail around twice before you tighten it (a double overhand knot), which grips better.
>
> **If it still slips,** the yarn is too smooth for a magic knot. Switch to a Russian join or join at the end of the row and weave in the ends.
>
> **If the knot pokes through to the right side,** push it to the wrong side with your fingertip or the tip of your hook. Crocheters often call this "popping" the knot through.

| Join | Knot? | Ends to weave | Best for | Not ideal for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Magic knot | Small knot | None | Grippy yarns, dense stitches, amigurumi, scrappy stripes | Silk, bamboo, some cottons; lace; reversible items |
| Russian join | No knot | None | Smooth plied yarns; joins that must be invisible from both sides | Very fine, loosely spun or novelty yarns; takes a needle and a minute |
| Felted (spit) splice | No knot | None | Non-superwash animal fibers that felt, such as many pure wools | Superwash wool, cotton, acrylic, most blends |
| Join at the end of a row | No knot | Two tails at the edge | Stripes, flat pieces, edges that will be seamed or bordered | Spirals and seamless pieces with no edge |
| Work over the tails, then weave in | No knot | Short tails to finish | Any yarn, including slippery ones; the most secure everyday join | Very open lace where the tails show |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That! · Tug before you trust it
> Before you trim a magic knot, pull on it like you mean it. A knot that gives even a little now is very likely to work loose later, in the wash or with wear. A knot that holds a hard tug is a good sign, though no knot is guaranteed on every yarn, so keep test-tugging and trim with a little tail to spare. And if you are joining silk or bamboo, skip the magic knot and reach for a Russian join from the start.

> [!GRANNY] Granny’s Wisdom
> I keep one rule for knots: if I would be sad to see the inside of it, I don’t knot it. Toys, stash blankets and dishcloths get the magic knot and I never think twice. Christening shawls and anything lacy get a Russian join or a careful weave, because those are the pieces that get passed down.

## Changing colors in rows

The clean color change happens one step early. Work the last stitch in the old color until only the final yarn over remains. Drop the old color, yarn over with the new color and draw it through to complete the stitch. The live loop on your hook is now the new color, so the next stitch, including any turning chain, is entirely in the new color.

![Decorative generated crochet art](../../assets/art/fill/p00-cozy-corner.jpg)

![color_change (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/color_change.png)

For single crochet that means stopping with two loops on the hook. For double crochet, stop when two loops remain after the first "yarn over, draw through two," and complete the final draw-through with the new color. The same principle applies to every stitch height: introduce the new color on the last yarn over of the previous stitch.

**Carrying colors up the side.** For stripes of two rows (or any even number), the old color waits at the same edge where you will need it next. Leave it hanging and pick it up again, keeping the carried strand relaxed along the edge. For stripes three or more rows deep, cut and rejoin, or twist the waiting yarn with the working yarn every second row so it does not form a long loop. A border will usually hide carried strands.

## Changing colors in rounds

**Joined rounds.** Complete the round in the old color. Join with a slip stitch to the first stitch using the new color: insert the hook, yarn over with the new color and pull through the stitch and the loop on the hook. Chain up in the new color and continue.

**Continuous spirals.** Because a spiral has no join, each color change leaves a small "step" or jog where the new round begins above the old. To soften it, work the last stitch of the old color, change color as usual, then work the first one or two stitches of the new round as a slip stitch and a half double crochet (or similar gradually taller stitches) before returning to the main stitch. Many crocheters simply place the jog at the back of a hat or the underside of a toy.

**Standing stitches.** A standing stitch starts a new color directly as a stitch, without a slip knot and chain. For a standing single crochet: place a slip knot of the new color on the hook, insert into the stitch, yarn over and draw up a loop, yarn over and draw through both loops. Standing stitches give cleaner round starts in granny squares and motifs.

## Fastening off

**Ordinary fasten off.** Cut the yarn, leaving a tail of at least 6 inches (15 cm), more if you will sew. Yarn over with the tail and pull it all the way through the loop on the hook. Tug gently to close.

The pictures below show the same finish done with the hook out: pull the loop tall, cut the yarn, then pull the cut end through the loop. Both ways give the same result.

**Invisible join (needle join) for rounds.** Instead of a slip stitch, cut the yarn after the last stitch and pull the tail up through the top of that stitch. Thread a tapestry needle. Skip the first stitch of the round (or the turning chain), pass the needle under both loops of the next stitch from front to back, then insert it back down into the center of the last stitch you made and pull until the new "stitch" matches the others in size. Weave the tail on the wrong side. The round now looks continuous, with no bump.

**Securing the beginning tail of a ring.** After pulling a magic ring closed, weave the tail around the center in one direction, then back in the other. A ring tail that is merely trimmed can slowly loosen and reopen the center (Chapter 51).

## Knots and loops every crocheter meets

| Name | What it is | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|
| Slip knot | An adjustable loop that starts most projects; not counted as a stitch | Start of a foundation chain |
| Live (active) loop | The loop on the hook; secure it with a marker during breaks | Always |
| Front loop / back loop | The near and far strands of a stitch’s top V | BLO/FLO texture, ribbing, invisible decreases |
| Third loop (back bar) | The horizontal bar below the top V on the back of hdc | "Third loop" or "camel stitch" ribbing |
| Back bump | The bump on the back of each foundation chain | Neat foundation edges |
| Magic (adjustable) ring | A loop of yarn worked into, then closed by pulling the tail | Amigurumi, hats, motifs |
| Picot | A small loop of chains closed with a slip stitch | Decorative edges |
| Turning chain | Chains made at the start of a row to reach the new stitch height | Flat rows |

> [!GRANNY] Granny’s Wisdom
> Every end you weave today is one less thing that can unravel in somebody’s washing machine. I used to save them all for the end of a blanket and it took me a whole weekend. Now I weave as I go, a few every evening, and the finishing is a pleasure instead of a chore.

## Weaving ends that stay put

1. Thread the tail onto a blunt tapestry needle.
2. On the wrong side, run the needle through the stitch posts or along the back of the stitches for about 1 to 1½ inches (2.5 to 4 cm).
3. Change direction and run back a short way, skipping over one strand of yarn to anchor the turn.
4. Change direction once more for slippery fibers.
5. Gently stretch the fabric so the tail settles, then trim close without cutting the fabric.

For colorwork, weave each tail into an area of the same color, so it cannot peek through to the right side.

> [!HINT]
> If a tail keeps working loose, it is usually because it was woven in a single straight line between stitches. Weave it through the yarn itself (split-ply) on the back, and change direction at least twice.


> [!HINT] Photograph your swatch with a ruler
> A photo of your swatch beside a ruler is a permanent gauge record when the swatch itself gets frogged.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Tie off a project neatly and it will look finished for years. Rush the last inch and it shows forever.
