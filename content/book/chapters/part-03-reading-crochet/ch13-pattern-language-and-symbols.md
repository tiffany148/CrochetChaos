A crochet pattern is a set of instructions with several layers. The materials describe what the designer used. Gauge connects stitch counts to physical size. Notes establish conventions. Special-stitch definitions tell you how named units are constructed. Row instructions then tell you where and how often to use them. Read those layers before treating Row 1 as a complete explanation of the project.

## Read once for structure

Before crocheting, identify the object’s construction in plain language. Does the sweater begin at the neck or hem? Is the bag a rectangle folded and seamed, or a tube with an attached base? Are the ears made separately from the amigurumi head? This first pass prevents a later instruction from appearing to demand an impossible turn simply because you pictured a different construction.

![pattern_anatomy (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/pattern_anatomy.png)

Find the terminology statement. Confirm the size notation and highlight only your chosen size. Read the finishing section early: it may explain why an edge is left unworked or why a tail is deliberately long. Check for a current errata notice from the pattern publisher when one exists.

## Abbreviations you will meet often

| Abbreviation | Meaning in this book | What to confirm in another pattern |
|---|---|---|
| beg | Beginning | Whether it refers to row, round, or repeat |
| rem | Remaining | Which stitches have already been consumed |
| rep | Repeat | The exact boundaries and total repetitions |
| sk | Skip | Number and type of destinations omitted |
| sp | Space | Whether formed by chains or between groups |
| ch-2 sp | Chain-2 space | Work into opening or individual chains as directed |
| inc | Increase | Exact stitch and number added |
| dec | Decrease | Exact method and destinations consumed |
| pm | Place marker | In stitch, space, or boundary |
| sm | Slip or move marker as defined | Do not assume it means slip stitch |
| RS and WS | Right and wrong side | Intended public face and row orientation |
| MC and CC | Main and contrasting color | Color assignment and carrying method |
| BLO and FLO | Back loop only and front loop only | Defined as the work faces you |

Special abbreviations always need local definitions. A designer may define a shell as five double crochets or as (2 dc, ch 2, 2 dc). You cannot choose the version you prefer and expect the same count and shape.

## Parentheses, brackets, and asterisks

Punctuation groups actions. Different publishers assign slightly different roles to parentheses and brackets, so consult the pattern notes. In this book, parentheses commonly group stitches placed in the same location, while square brackets identify a repeat sequence. Size alternatives are introduced explicitly as size alternatives.

“(Sc, ch 2, sc) in next st” puts all three actions at one destination. It makes two single crochet tops and a chain-2 space. “Sc in each of next 3 sts” uses three different destinations. The preposition “in” and its object tell you where the group belongs.

“[Sc in next 2 sts, 2 sc in next st] 4 times” consumes three old stitches per repeat and produces four new stitches. Four repeats consume 12 and produce 16. The bracket includes both the two ordinary single crochets and the increase.

An asterisk can mark the beginning of a repeated section, but the instruction after it must tell you where repetition stops. “Repeat from * twice more” normally means the initial execution plus two additional executions, three total. “Work the bracketed sequence 3 times total” is clearer when writing your own pattern.

## Separate row numbers from repetitions

“Repeat Row 2 four times” usually adds four executions of that row after its first occurrence, but a poorly edited pattern may leave uncertainty. Calculate the expected total and use the context. If the instructions explicitly say Rows 3–6 repeat Row 2, the number of additional rows is four and the total through Row 6 is unambiguous.

For your own writing, prefer explicit ranges. “Rows 3–10: repeat Row 2” produces eight rows in that range. “Repeat Rows 2–3 four more times” adds eight rows after the first two-row cycle. Write out the intended final row number to catch an off-by-one error.

“Work even” means continue without the shaping increases or decreases that would change the relevant count. It does not mean “make every stitch identical” if the established pattern contains a mix of stitch types. “Work evenly along edge” is a different instruction about distribution along an edge.

## Understand stitch totals

A total at the end of a row is a check on what the row produces. It may include counted beginning chains and may separately list chain spaces. Read the convention. A row labeled “24 dc, 8 ch-1 spaces” is not necessarily a 32-stitch row in every later instruction.

When a total does not match your work, audit the consumed destinations before inventing a compensating increase. Count the old row. Count how many old stitches each repeat uses. Check whether a turning chain or join has been counted improperly. A different final number can result from a misread repeat rather than a missing stitch in the fabric.

## Read multiple sizes without mixing them

A pattern might list five sizes as A (B, C, D, E). Establish the position of your size and mark that position in every relevant instruction. If you choose size C, use the third value consistently unless you deliberately make a documented adjustment.

Zero is often an instruction to omit an action for that size. A dash may indicate not applicable, but the pattern should define it. Do not replace a zero with a neighboring value simply because “do nothing” feels odd. Conversely, a missing value is an editing problem, not permission to guess.

Garment left and right generally refer to the wearer. Lay out pieces in wearing orientation before attaching them. A left front held in your hand can appear on your right when you face the garment on a table.

## Translate a row into a checklist

Consider this original instruction: “Ch 1, turn; sc in first 2 sts, [2 sc in next st, sc in next 3 sts] 3 times, sc in last 2 sts; 19 sc.” It needs an input row of 16 stitches. The edges consume four and produce four. Each repeat consumes four and produces five. Three repeats consume 12 and produce 15. Together, the instruction consumes 16 and produces 19.

Write a working checklist: two edge sc; increase; three sc; increase; three sc; increase; three sc; two edge sc. You now have a readable expansion that can be checked against the compact line. Do not expand from memory after beginning; transcribe and audit it before work.

## What to do when wording is ambiguous

Stop at the smallest unresolved point. Identify exactly what is missing: destination, repeat boundary, chain convention, count, or face. Check the notes, special stitches, chart, schematic, and publisher errata. If those disagree, preserve the discrepancy and seek clarification rather than claiming the pattern has been corrected by a guess.

When adapting your own project, you can choose a method and test it. When explaining another designer’s instructions, distinguish their statement from your interpretation. “I worked the chain as a non- counting height chain in this sample” is evidence of your experiment, not proof of the designer’s original intention.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Read the sentence after the repeat before starting the repeat. “Until two stitches remain” changes where you stop.
>
> Highlight the size position, not just one number. A highlighted starting chain does not protect you from accidentally taking the neighboring sleeve count later.
>
> If a row becomes confusing, rewrite it as destinations consumed and stitches produced. Arithmetic cannot verify beauty, but it can expose an impossible instruction.

## Decode a pattern line, step by step

Take this sample line: *Rnd 4: Ch 3 (counts as dc), dc in same st, \*dc in next 2 sts, 2 dc in next st; rep from \* 4 more times, dc in next 2 sts, join with sl st to top of beg ch-3. (24 dc)* The round below had 18 stitches.

1. **Start:** chain 3; it counts as the first dc.
2. **Same stitch:** one more dc in the stitch at the base of the chain (an increase).
3. **Repeat section:** dc in each of the next 2 stitches, then 2 dc in the next stitch. Work it once, then 4 more times: 5 times in all, using 15 stitches of the round below and making 20.
4. **Finish:** dc in the last 2 stitches, then join. Stitches used below: 1 + 15 + 2 = 18. Stitches made: 2 + 20 + 2 = 24.
5. **Check the count** against the number in brackets at the end.

Reading "rep from \* 4 more times" correctly matters: "4 more times" means 5 times in total. If you read it as 4 in total, the round comes out 4 stitches short and 3 stitches of the round below are left unworked.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — "More times" adds one
> "Repeat 5 more times" means you work the section 6 times in all. "Repeat 5 times" usually means 5 times in all. Check the stitch count to be sure.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Circle your size first
> In multi-size patterns, highlight every number for your size before you begin. It is very easy to read the wrong number in a parenthesis halfway through.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Read the whole pattern once with a cup of tea before you pick up a hook. The surprises are always on page three.
