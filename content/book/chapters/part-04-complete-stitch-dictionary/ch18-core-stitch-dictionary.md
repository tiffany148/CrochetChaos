This dictionary separates the movement that makes a stitch from the place where it is worked. Unless an entry says otherwise, insert under both top loops of the indicated stitch. Each completed ordinary stitch ends with one live loop on the hook. Work with the working yarn rather than the starting tail.

The same abbreviation can mean a different stitch in a UK pattern. Confirm the terminology system before beginning. A US double crochet is a UK treble, even when the pattern is printed by a company in a country you associate with the other terminology system. Location is not an adequate substitute for a terminology statement.

## Chain

Start with one live loop. Yarn over and draw through that loop. One new chain link is complete, and one live loop remains. Let the loop reach the shaft before making another chain. Chains can form a foundation, give height at a row edge, bridge a skipped stitch, or create an open space.

**Step by step: Chain (ch)**

A chain is a yarn over pulled through the loop on your hook. A row of chains is the starting edge for your first row. Same name in UK terms (ch).

Count completed links below the hook. Do not count the live loop or slip knot. When working into a chain, identify whether the pattern calls for an individual link or the opening under an arch. Those are different destinations.

Common error: a tight chain foundation pulls inward beneath a wider fabric. Try a more relaxed chain or a larger hook for the chain, then check the sample. A larger chain hook does not automatically solve every edge problem.

## Slip stitch

Insert the hook into the indicated stitch or space. Yarn over and draw the strand through the insertion point and through the live loop on the hook. One live loop remains. This creates a short stitch useful for joins, surface movement, edges, and slip-stitch fabrics.

**Step by step: Slip stitch (sl st)**

The shortest stitch, used for joining and for neat edges. When the hook is in a stitch, the two top strands of that stitch lie over the hook. They are not loops on the hook, so do not count them. UK: slip stitch (ss).

Some crocheters make the draw-through in two visible movements. That is still a slip stitch if the strand passes through the fabric and live loop without a separate second yarn over. Adding another yarn over before pulling through two loops would instead follow the single crochet structure.

Slip stitches often tighten easily. If the next row must be worked into them, keep their top loops accessible. Do not pull a decorative edge so tight that it gathers the fabric unintentionally. A joining slip stitch often does not count in a round total, but that rule must be stated by the pattern.

## Single crochet

Insert into the indicated location. Yarn over and draw up a loop; two loops are on the hook. Yarn over and draw through both. One loop remains. US single crochet is UK double crochet.

Single crochet makes a relatively short stitch. Dense single crochet can suit stuffed shapes, while a looser fabric can be softer and more flexible. The stitch name alone does not determine opacity or firmness. Hook, yarn, tension, and finishing all contribute.

Common error: pulling the first loop through the existing live loop immediately produces a slip stitch. To make single crochet, keep both loops on the hook until the second yarn over.

## Half double crochet

Yarn over once before insertion. Insert, yarn over, and draw up a loop; three loops are on the hook. Yarn over and draw through all three together. One loop remains. US half double crochet is UK half treble.

**Step by step: Half double crochet (hdc)**

Like single crochet, but you wrap the yarn over the hook BEFORE you go into the stitch, and you finish by pulling through all three loops at once. In picture 2 the arrow shows where the hook goes in. US half double crochet (hdc) = UK half treble (htr).

The initial yarn over contributes an additional strand near the stitch top, often called a third loop. Its exact position is easiest to see by tilting the fabric. A third-loop instruction must specify which strand and face the designer intends; do not assume every visible horizontal bar is interchangeable.

Common error: forgetting the initial yarn over and making single crochet instead. Another error is pulling through two and then two, which produces the double crochet structure rather than half double.

## Double crochet

Yarn over once. Insert, yarn over, and draw up a loop; three loops are on the hook. Yarn over and draw through two; two loops remain. Yarn over and draw through the final two. One loop remains. US double crochet is UK treble.

The two successive reductions are part of the stitch. If two loops remain when you move to the next stitch, you have left the double crochet unfinished. Unfinished double crochets are useful in clusters and decreases, but they are intentional there.

A pattern may use a counted chain-3 at the beginning of a row or a non-counted height chain. Read that convention separately from this stitch definition. Stitch construction does not impose one universal turning-chain rule.

## Treble crochet

Yarn over twice. Insert, yarn over, and draw up a loop; four loops are on the hook. Yarn over and draw through two, leaving three. Yarn over and draw through two, leaving two. Yarn over and draw through two, leaving one. US treble is UK double treble.

![loop_tr (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/loop_tr.png)

**Step by step: Treble crochet (tr)**

A tall stitch. You wrap the yarn over twice before you go in, then work off the loops two at a time. Each "wrap, then pull" step below is one smooth move. Count your loops at every check. US treble crochet (tr) = UK double treble (dtr). Some US patterns call it "triple crochet."

Keep the initial wraps controlled without cinching them tightly. A long first draw-up loop can create an elongated opening at the base. That may be a deliberate effect in some patterns but should not happen unpredictably in a uniform sample.

## Double treble crochet

Yarn over three times. Insert, yarn over, and draw up a loop; five loops are on the hook. Work yarn over and draw through two four times. One loop remains. US double treble is UK triple treble.

![loop_dtr (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/loop_dtr.png)

![st_double_treble_crochet (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_double_treble_crochet.png)

Taller stitches can be extended further with additional initial wraps and corresponding two-loop reductions, but long columns can become open, unstable, or uneven. A decorative label such as “extra tall stitch” is not enough for a reproducible pattern; state the wraps and the completion sequence.

## Loop-count checkpoint

| Stitch | Wraps before insertion | Loops after drawing through fabric | Completion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slip stitch | 0 | Draw-through continues through live loop | One continuous draw- through |
| Single crochet | 0 | 2 | Draw through 2 |
| Half double crochet | 1 | 3 | Draw through all 3 |
| Double crochet | 1 | 3 | Draw through 2, then 2 |
| Treble crochet | 2 | 4 | Draw through 2 three times |
| Double treble crochet | 3 | 5 | Draw through 2 four times |

The strand lying across the hook during a yarn over counts as a loop for these working checkpoints. Do not count strands in the fabric below the hook as additional loops on it.

## Extended single crochet

Insert, yarn over, and draw up a loop; two loops are on the hook. Yarn over and draw through only one loop; two loops remain because you replaced the loop you drew through with a new one. Yarn over and draw through both. One live loop remains.

![st_extended_single_crochet (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_extended_single_crochet.png)

The extra one-loop draw-through adds an extension near the base. It is not the same construction as a half double crochet, even if their heights happen to be similar in your yarn. Define the stitch as esc or another stated abbreviation; spell it out wherever a publisher’s notation differs.

## Extended double crochet

Yarn over, insert, yarn over, and draw up a loop; three loops are on the hook. Yarn over and draw through one; three loops remain. Yarn over and draw through two; two remain. Yarn over and draw through two; one remains.

![st_extended_double_crochet (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_extended_double_crochet.png)

Keep the extension consistent. An extension made by pulling up a very long loose loop is structurally different from the extra one-loop draw-through described here. The two may look similar from a distance but respond differently in a repeated fabric.

## Single crochet two together

Insert into the first indicated stitch, yarn over, and draw up a loop; two loops. Insert into the next indicated stitch, yarn over, and draw up a loop; three loops. Yarn over and draw through all three. Two old stitches have produced one new stitch, reducing the total by one.

![inc_dec (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/inc_dec.png)

![st_single_crochet_two_together (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_single_crochet_two_together.png)

Do not yarn over before the second insertion for this version. That extra wrap changes the loop count. Work through the stated loops of both stitches unless the pattern defines an invisible or other specialized decrease.

## Invisible single crochet decrease

One common amigurumi version works through the front loops of two adjacent stitches. Insert under the front loop of the first, then under the front loop of the next. Yarn over and draw through both front loops. Yarn over and draw through the two remaining loops on the hook.

![st_invisible_decrease (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_invisible_decrease.png)

This places the unused back loops inside the fabric when worked in the intended orientation. Its appearance depends on the yarn and which face is outward. “Invisible” is a conventional name, not a guarantee that the decrease cannot be seen. Do not replace every ordinary decrease with it when the design depends on both-loop structure or a visible decorative line.

## Double crochet two together

Yarn over, insert into the first indicated stitch, yarn over and draw up a loop. Yarn over and draw through two, leaving two loops. Yarn over, insert into the next indicated stitch, yarn over and draw up a loop; four loops are now on the hook. Yarn over and draw through two, leaving three. Yarn over and draw through all three.

![st_double_crochet_two_together (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_double_crochet_two_together.png)

Two old stitches produce one new top. The first two-loop stage of each partial double crochet remains, while their final closures are combined. Check the input locations: two partial double crochets in the same old stitch make a cluster-like unit rather than a decrease across two stitches.

## Foundation single crochet

This method makes a chain-like base and a single crochet top together. It can provide a flexible start, but it still needs sampling for the intended edge. Begin ch 2. Insert into the second chain from hook, yarn over and draw up a loop. Yarn over and draw through one loop to make the base chain. Yarn over and draw through two to complete the first single crochet.

![st_foundation_single_crochet (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_foundation_single_crochet.png)

Identify the base chain just created beneath that stitch. Insert under its two appropriate strands, yarn over and draw up a loop. Yarn over and draw through one to create the next base chain; yarn over and draw through two to complete the next single crochet. Repeat by entering the newest base chain, not the top V of the single crochet.

The base-chain entry can be difficult to identify in text alone. Use a smooth-yarn sample and mark the base chain as it is created. The publication illustration for this entry must be produced from a verified physical sample. Count completed single crochet tops to determine the foundation stitch total.

## Standing single crochet

When joining a new yarn, place a slip knot on the hook if the pattern’s method calls for one. Insert into the destination, yarn over and draw up a loop, then yarn over and draw through both loops. The resulting stitch begins without a separate height chain. Secure the tail appropriately.

![st_standing_single_crochet (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_standing_single_crochet.png)

A standing stitch and a chainless turning stitch solve different situations. Standing stitches introduce a new yarn or a detached yarn end at a destination. A chainless turn continues the attached yarn at a row edge using a defined construction. Do not substitute the terms without explaining the method.

## Reverse single crochet

Often called crab stitch, reverse single crochet travels in the opposite direction from ordinary single crochet while retaining its basic draw-up-and-close structure. Without turning the work, insert into the adjacent stitch in the reverse direction, yarn over and draw up a loop, then yarn over and draw through two. Continue around or along the edge as directed.

![st_reverse_single_crochet (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_reverse_single_crochet.png)

This creates a twisted-looking corded edge. The motion can feel awkward because the hand travels against its usual sequence. Work loosely enough to enter the next stitch. The edge may be decorative and firm; it is not automatically suitable where later rows must be added.

## Build a labeled stitch library

Make separate 12-stitch samples in sc, hdc, dc, and tr. Use one yarn and hook so the comparison focuses on stitch construction. For sc, chain 13 and work from the second chain. For hdc, chain 14 and work from the third. For dc, chain 14 and work from the third using a non-counting chain-2 height convention. For tr, chain 15 and work from the fourth using a non-counting chain-3 convention. Each first row contains 12 actual worked stitches.

On subsequent rows use ch 1 for sc, ch 2 for hdc and dc, and ch 3 for tr; none counts as a stitch in this exercise. Turn and work 12 actual stitches. Make at least four rows of each. These height-chain choices are exercise conventions, not universal stitch rules. If an edge chain is conspicuously loose or tight, record that observation rather than silently changing the convention midway.

## Turning chain reference

These are common conventions. Patterns vary, so the pattern's own instruction always wins.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — The stacked-stitch alternative
> A ch-3 turning chain often leaves a gap at the edge of dc rows. Many crocheters replace it with a "stacked single crochet" (a sc worked into the top of a sc) or a chainless starting dc. Check whether the pattern counts the turning chain before you replace it.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Count the yarn-overs to name a stitch
> No yarn-over before inserting: sc. One: hdc or dc. Two: tr. Three: dtr. Then the way you finish (all at once or two at a time) tells you hdc versus dc.

> Say "yarn over, pull through two" out loud for your first rows of dc. Your hands learn the rhythm from your voice.
