You can change a familiar stitch dramatically without changing its basic yarn-over sequence. Move the insertion point and you change which strands remain visible, which rows connect directly, and how the fabric bends. Placement is a design choice with consequences for thickness, stretch, and row gauge.

## Both loops, front loop, and back loop

Both-loop work passes under the two strands of the top V. Front-loop-only work passes under the nearer strand as the stitch faces you. Back-loop-only work passes under the farther strand. The unused strand remains visible and can become a ridge or a later attachment point.

![st_back_loop_only (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_back_loop_only.png)

To practice BLO single crochet, make a row of ordinary single crochet. Chain 1 and turn. Identify the top V of the first actual stitch and slide the hook under only its far strand. Complete a single crochet. Repeat in each stitch. On the following row, turn again and select the back loop as it now faces you.

Repeated turned BLO rows often form a ridged fabric that stretches readily across the ridges. The amount of useful recovery depends on the yarn and gauge. Do not assume it functions like elastic or that it can replace a snug cuff without a fit sample. The direction of the ridges also matters: a cuff worked in short rows around the wrist has a different orientation from a cuff worked in rounds up the arm.

FLO leaves the other top strand unused. Compare it directly with BLO rather than assuming it produces the same effect with its name reversed. In turned rows the visible ridges and flexibility can differ from non-turned rounds.

## Third-loop half double crochet

A half double crochet has an additional horizontal strand associated with its initial yarn over. In a sample, tilt the row and identify that strand relative to the top V. Its position appears different when viewed from the front or back and when the work is turned. A pattern should specify its intended third-loop location clearly.

![st_third_loop_half_double_crochet (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_third_loop_half_double_crochet.png)

Work under the designated third loop rather than the top V, then complete the half double crochet normally. The top V can roll outward as a visible line. If you accidentally alternate between the third loop and a strand of the top V, the ridge may become irregular even with a correct stitch count.

Use a marker or a blunt needle to identify several third loops before working them. Do not force an illustration-free guess into a large garment. This entry needs a verified close-up in the illustration phase, including both faces and an identified top V.

## Front post and back post stitches

For front post double crochet, yarn over and insert the hook from the front of the fabric to the back on one side of the designated post, then bring it back to the front on the other side. The post lies across the hook. Yarn over and draw up a loop around the post, then complete the double crochet as usual.

![post_stitches (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/post_stitches.png)

![st_front_post_double_crochet (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_front_post_double_crochet.png)

![st_back_post_double_crochet (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_back_post_double_crochet.png)

For back post double crochet, yarn over and insert from the back to the front on one side of the post, then return to the back on the other side. Draw up the loop and complete the double crochet. The directional names describe how the hook surrounds the post, not a different number of yarn overs.

When a post stitch replaces a stitch in an ordinary row, do not also work into the top V belonging to that same designated stitch unless the pattern expressly calls for it. Doing both can create an accidental increase. Track the relationship between a post and its top, especially in dense cables.

Post stitches can shorten a fabric’s effective row height because the new stitch anchors around an existing post below its top. Measure gauge in the actual post pattern. Plain double crochet gauge cannot reliably predict the dimensions of a heavily textured panel.

## Keep a raised column on the public face

Suppose the RS row uses front post double crochets around selected posts. After turning, those same columns are viewed from the reverse. Back post stitches on the WS can maintain the raised effect on the original RS. Repeating front post stitches on every turned row produces a different arrangement.

Mark the RS and label the columns. Work a small sample before planning a garment panel. An instruction such as “continue ribbing” is too vague for your own reproducible design unless the setup row and face- dependent repeat have been defined.

## Spike stitches

A spike stitch reaches into a lower row rather than the immediately preceding stitch top. Insert at the specified lower destination, draw the loop up to the working row’s height, and complete the stated stitch. The drawn-up loop must be long enough to avoid pulling the fabric upward.

![st_spike_stitch (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_spike_stitch.png)

The spike commonly replaces a stitch at the current row. Skip the corresponding top stitch if the pattern says so; otherwise you may add stitches unintentionally. Do not insert into a vague “somewhere below” opening. State the row and column relationship, such as one row below directly beneath the next stitch.

Longer spikes can distort or snag more readily. A dense fabric may be difficult to penetrate cleanly. Test the chosen placement, yarn, and length rather than relying on a decorative name alone.

## Crossed stitches

Crossing changes the order in which destinations are worked. A simple two-stitch cross can skip the next stitch, work a double crochet in the following stitch, then work back into the skipped stitch. Whether the second stitch passes in front of or behind the first changes the crossing direction.

![Decorative generated crochet art](../../assets/art/fill/p05-color-wheel.jpg)

![st_crossed_double_crochet (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_crossed_double_crochet.png)

The two destinations still need two stitches in total. Do not work the skipped stitch again as you resume the row. For a more complex cable, identify which stitches are temporarily skipped, how many are crossed, and whether the return stitches pass in front or behind. A cable instruction without that information is incomplete.

Crossed stitches may lean or narrow a panel. A four-stitch cable repeat does not necessarily occupy the width of four ordinary double crochets. Make and wash the complete cable repeat before calculating a garment width.

## Surface slip stitch

Surface crochet adds a line to existing fabric. Place the yarn supply behind the fabric and the hook in front. Draw a loop to the front through the starting location, then insert into the next planned location, catch yarn from behind, and draw it through the fabric and the loop on the hook. Continue along the marked path.

![Decorative generated crochet art](../../assets/art/fill/p05-granny-stack.jpg)

![st_surface_slip_stitch (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_surface_slip_stitch.png)

Keep the line relaxed enough that it does not gather the base. A line that crosses every stitch top can have a different length from one running diagonally across rows. Test the spacing and maintain the base fabric’s intended dimensions. Secure both ends without leaving loose loops on the reverse.

Surface decoration can obscure a structural flaw but does not repair it. Mend a hole or weak seam first. Avoid loose decorative strands where they could snag or be chewed.

For a separate post sample, make a plain double crochet base row with a clearly stated turning convention. Choose four internal posts and work front post stitches around them on the RS, back post stitches around the corresponding columns on the WS, and ordinary stitches elsewhere. Keep the total unchanged. This is a placement investigation rather than a finished-item pattern; obtain a verified sample before publishing a chart for it.

## Diagnose a texture that is not behaving

| Symptom | Possible cause | Inspection |
|---|---|---|
| Rib suddenly smooths out | Both loops used instead of BLO | Compare unused strands row by row |
| Raised column swaps faces | Wrong post direction after turn | Check RS marker and FP/BP sequence |
| Count increases in post row | Top V and post both used | Pair each designated post with its replacement |
| Spike row pulls upward | Drawn loop too short | Compare loop length before completing stitch |
| Cable panel narrows | Crossed-stitch gauge differs | Measure the complete washed repeat |
| Surface line puckers base | Decoration too tight | Remove a short section and compare |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Photograph both faces of a texture sample. The “back” of one sample may become the design feature of your next project.
>
> When testing a new placement, leave the sample unfinished until you understand it. You may want to undo one stitch slowly and watch which strand it was attached to.
>
> If the count is correct but the texture is wrong, stop counting and start tracing. The insertion path can be wrong without changing the total.

## Texture by placement: quick guide

| Where you insert the hook | Result |
|---|---|
| Under both loops | Standard, flat fabric |
| Back loop only | Horizontal ridges; stretchy rib when turned sideways |
| Front loop only | Softer ridges on the back; free loops for later |
| Third loop (back bar) of hdc | Knit-look rib on the front |
| Around the post (front) | Raised column toward you |
| Around the post (back) | Column pushed away |
| Into a lower row (spike) | Long stitch that crosses rows |
| Between stitches | Offset texture, often looser |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — BLO in rounds ridges only one side
> In rows, BLO puts ridges on both faces. In unturned rounds, every stitch is worked from the same side, so the ridges show only on one face.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Post stitches need extra width
> Rows of post stitches pull in. If your ribbing makes a cuff too narrow, add stitches or go up a hook size for the ribbed section.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> One stitch, four placements, four fabrics. Learn placement before you learn a new stitch, and your stitch vocabulary multiplies.


> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Count corners, too
> Before joining, count stitches between corners on every motif. A single extra stitch makes a wave in the join.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Same hook for the border
> Use the same hook for the border as for the motifs unless the pattern says otherwise; a smaller hook can pull the edges in.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Corners eat more yarn
> Corners use more stitches than sides. When estimating a border, count corner stitches separately.
