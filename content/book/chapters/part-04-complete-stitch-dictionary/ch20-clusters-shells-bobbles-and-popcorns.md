Raised and grouped stitches are easy to confuse because publishers do not use every decorative name identically. Read the definition, not just the label. Ask four questions: where do the component stitches begin, are they completed separately, how do they close, and how is the group counted in the next row?

## A group is not always an increase

Five double crochets in one stitch create five separate tops. If nothing compensates for them, they increase the stitch count. The same five-double-crochet group placed into a lace space may form a shell within a repeat whose skipped stitches maintain the overall width. Count both the group’s output and the old stitches it consumes.

A cluster combines unfinished stitches with a shared closure. It may place all components into one destination or distribute them across several destinations. A decrease such as dc2tog is a particular shared- closure structure across separate stitches. A bobble can use a similar shared closure in one location but create a raised texture. The mechanics overlap; the design purpose and definition supply the difference.

## Five-double-crochet shell

In the indicated stitch or space, make five complete double crochets. Each begins and ends with one live loop. The group has five separate top Vs. The center double crochet is the third. If the next row says to work in the center of a five-stitch shell, count deliberately rather than choosing the most prominent- looking post.

![Decorative generated crochet art](../../assets/art/fill/p05-hexagons.jpg)

![st_shell (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_shell.png)

Shells can contain a different number or height of stitches, and some include chains. Define those differences. “Shell” alone is not a complete special-stitch definition for a pattern you publish.

A crowded shell may spread differently in a chain space than in a tight single crochet. Do not swap destinations without resampling. The entry point influences the group’s fan and the stability of the base.

## V stitch

For the version used here, work (dc, ch 1, dc) in the indicated stitch or space. This creates two double crochet tops separated by a chain-1 opening. A later row can work into that opening to stack V groups.

![st_v_stitch (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_v_stitch.png)

Other patterns use a chain-2 center or different stitch heights. The abbreviation V-st is therefore defined locally. If a pattern counts the center space separately from the double crochets, record that distinction. Two double crochets plus one chain do not become three double crochets merely because the repeat occupies three visible positions.

## Three-double-crochet cluster in one location

Yarn over, insert into the designated location, yarn over and draw up a loop, yarn over and draw through two. Two loops remain. Repeat that partial double crochet into the same location twice more. Four loops now remain: the original live loop plus one retained loop for each of three partial double crochets. Yarn over and draw through all four.

![loop_cl3 (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/loop_cl3.png)

![st_cluster (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_cluster.png)

The three partial components share one top. Unless the pattern defines an additional locking chain as part of the unit, do not add one automatically. If a locking chain is included, state whether the next row works into it or the cluster top and whether it counts separately.

For a cluster across three stitches, place one partial double crochet in each of three destinations before the shared closure. That version consumes three old stitches instead of one. The same closure count does not imply the same shaping.

## Five-double-crochet bobble

For this book’s defined version, work five partial double crochets in the same destination, leaving each incomplete after its first two-loop draw-through. Six loops remain. Yarn over and draw through all six. The bobble has one shared top and no extra chain in this definition.

![st_bobble (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_bobble.png)

Shorter surrounding stitches can encourage the group to protrude. The bump often appears more strongly on one face, and the worked face is not automatically the intended public face. Push or arrange the bump gently if the design calls for it; do not pull its component loops individually.

A bobble may be defined with four components, a chain closure, or another stitch height elsewhere. Restate the version in every original pattern. The name “bobble” does not override that pattern’s specific instructions.

## Three-draw-up puff

Begin with one live loop. Repeat [yarn over, insert into the same destination, yarn over and draw up a loop to a consistent height] three times. Seven loops are on the hook: the starting loop plus six from the three draw-up actions. Yarn over and draw through all seven. For this defined puff, ch 1 to close. Count the puff as one unit; the closing chain is not a separate unit for the exercises here.

![loop_puff (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/loop_puff.png)

![st_puff_stitch (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_puff_stitch.png)

That counting convention must be carried into the following row. Work into the defined puff top or closing-chain location as specified by a pattern. Do not count both and accidentally double the row. This chapter explains the unit, but a production pattern must name the exact insertion point in its next-row instructions.

Even draw-up loop height matters. If one loop is short, it can pull the group inward or make the final draw-through difficult. Rotate the hook head and ease the loops along the shaft instead of yanking through a crowded throat.

## Five-double-crochet popcorn

Make five complete double crochets in one destination. Enlarge the live loop slightly and remove the hook. Insert the hook through the top of the first of those five double crochets in the direction specified for the intended face. Catch the dropped live loop and draw it through that first stitch top. The five separate columns are gathered into a rounded group.

![st_popcorn (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_popcorn.png)

For the ordinary front-facing version, insert through the first double crochet from the front before retrieving the dropped loop. A reverse-facing version changes the retrieval direction. Keep the live loop controlled so it does not unravel while the hook is out.

Some popcorn definitions add a chain afterward. The definition here does not. When designing, specify whether a chain is added, whether it counts, and where the next row enters. The gathered group is treated as one popcorn unit, not five ordinary available stitches unless the design deliberately opens it again.

## Compare the mechanics

| Unit defined in this chapter | Components | Closure | Tops treated as units |
|---|---|---|---|
| Five-dc shell | Five finished dc | Each closes separately | Five stitch tops |
| V stitch | Two finished dc and ch-1 | Separate dc with center space | Two dc plus a space |
| Three-dc cluster | Three partial dc | Shared draw through four loops | One cluster |
| Five-dc bobble | Five partial dc | Shared draw through six loops | One bobble |
| Three-draw-up puff | Three yo-and-draw-up actions | Draw through seven, then ch 1 | One defined puff unit |
| Five-dc popcorn | Five finished dc | Retrieve live loop through first dc | One popcorn unit |

These are this chapter’s definitions. They are not an attempt to impose a universal naming standard on every publisher.

## A counted shell sample

Status: original practice sample; input and output counts checked; not physically sample-tested. Use smooth yarn and a suitable hook. Ch 20. Row 1: sc in second ch from hook and in every remaining chain; 19 sc.

Row 2: ch 1, turn; sc in first sc; repeat [skip 2 sc, 5 dc in next sc, skip 2 sc, sc in next sc] three times. The repeat consumes six old stitches and creates six new stitches. Including the initial single crochet, the row contains 19 actual stitches: 4 sc and 15 dc.

Row 3: ch 1, turn; sc in each actual stitch across; 19 sc. Do not work into the spaces between the shell stitches. Fasten off and secure ends. This sample isolates one shell row and its return to an ordinary counted row; it does not claim to be a repeating shell-fabric pattern.

If the shell row pulls, ruffles, or has a pronounced scalloped shape, record that physical result. Correct arithmetic establishes that the row preserves the count, not that every yarn combination produces a flat rectangle.

## A raised-unit comparison sample

Make four separate 11-stitch single crochet bases. On the next row of each, work five single crochets, one defined raised unit into the sixth stitch, and five single crochets. Use the cluster, bobble, puff, and popcorn definitions above, one per sample. For the puff, treat its closure according to the defined unit convention and mark that unit.

Compare the face on which the bump appears, the thickness, ease of closure, and yarn consumed. This is a technique experiment, not a calibrated consumption chart. Do not publish a claim that one unit always uses a particular multiple of ordinary single crochet without measuring actual samples across the relevant materials.

## Troubleshoot the group

If the hook cannot draw through a puff, check whether loops are tightened on the throat or vary in height. If the bobble count is wrong, count retained loops after each partial component. If the popcorn unravels, secure the dropped live loop and verify that all five double crochets were complete before retrieval.

If the next row gains stitches, inspect whether you counted a closing chain in addition to its group. If a cluster fails to decrease the row as expected, check whether all partial stitches were accidentally placed into one destination instead of separate stitches.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Write the special-stitch definition beside the pattern, even if you know a stitch with the same name. Your familiar bobble may be somebody else’s cluster.
>
> Mark the top of the first unfamiliar group immediately. The next row becomes much easier when you know which loop the designer intends you to enter.
>
> For a textured garment, swatch the plain area and the raised area together. Matching gauge in the plain area alone may hide a texture panel that narrows or shortens.

## Group stitches compared

| Group | Made of | Closed how | Looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shell | Complete stitches in one place | Not closed | A fan |
| V-stitch | Two stitches with a chain between | Not closed | A V |
| Cluster | Partial stitches | Together at the top | A gathered point |
| Bobble | Partial stitches in one stitch | Together at the top, popping out | A round bump |
| Puff | Long loops without finishing | All at once | A soft oval |
| Popcorn | Complete stitches | Drawn together through the first | A crisp cup |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Bobbles pop to the back
> A bobble pushes out on the side facing away from you. That is why bobbles are usually worked on wrong-side rows—so they pop out on the right side.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Close puffs with a chain
> Many patterns close a puff with ch 1. That chain keeps the puff from slanting and gives the next row a clear stitch to work into—check whether it counts as a stitch.

> [!HINT] Yarn for groups
> Clusters, bobbles and puffs use a lot of yarn. Expect a textured blanket to need noticeably more yarn than a plain one of the same size; swatch and weigh to estimate (Chapter 68).
