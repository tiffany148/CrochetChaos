Construction determines the path you follow through an object. You can make a bag as a rectangle folded and seamed, as a tube with a separate base, or as a continuous shape grown from its center. Those objects may look similar while offering different opportunities for fitting, repair, color placement, and adjustment.

## Choose a path before calculating counts

Describe the construction in one sentence, then list its pieces in working order. For a simple panel garment: make back, make fronts, make sleeves, join shoulders, attach sleeves, close side and sleeve seams, add bands. For a top-down tube: make the starting opening, establish shaping, divide openings, continue the body, return to sleeves, finish edges.

The sequence matters because an early dimension may control later work. A panel garment gives access to flat pieces for blocking and measuring. A continuous garment can reduce seams and allow intermediate fitting, but mistakes near the beginning may be harder to remove independently. Neither is automatically more advanced or better.

## Flat pieces

Flat work commonly turns at the end of each row, but a design may work repeated RS rows using new yarn or a specialty method. State whether turns occur. Rectangles are easy to measure and seam, yet their orientation still matters: the foundation edge may stretch differently from the final row, and row ends differ from stitch tops.

Mark RS, bottom, and major joining edges before setting a piece aside. Two identical-looking panels can be mirrored intentionally or accidentally. If a texture has a directional slope, turning one panel upside down during assembly can change the appearance even when its dimensions fit.

For matched pieces, use the same gauge and treatment. Make both sleeves before permanently attaching either if comparison is important. Record any row added for fit on one piece so the corresponding piece receives the intended change.

## Tubes

A tube can begin with a chain joined into a ring, a flat row later joined, or a center that expands into sides. The starting method affects the edge and any seam. Check the foundation for an unintended twist before continuing.

The approximate flat width of a straight, unstrained tube is half its circumference. Its depth depends on row or round gauge. A tube’s spiral boundary may drift visually because the stitches form a helix; a joined tube may show a seam. Choose the method with its appearance and color changes in mind.

When you divide a tube for openings, identify which stitches remain active, which are skipped or held, and whether new chains bridge gaps. The new counts must include those bridge chains according to the next round’s instructions. A sleeve separation cannot be audited from the body count alone.

## Motifs

Motifs are repeated units made separately or joined during their final rounds. They can support portable work and color planning. Their assembly introduces additional edges, joins, tails, and measurements. A nominal square size before joining is not necessarily the final contribution of each square in a blanket.

Make and finish several motifs before deciding the final layout. Measure them without stretching. Join a representative pair or group using the intended method. Measure the assembled unit. A raised joining seam may add width; an overlapping seam may use some width. Do not assume every join has zero effect.

For a four-by-five layout of nominal 5-inch squares, the unjoined arithmetic suggests 20 by 25 inches. If each internal join adds a measured quarter inch of separation, the width gains three quarters of an inch across three internal joins, and the length gains one inch across four. Borders add further dimensions. Those additions are hypothetical until established by the actual join sample.

## Continuous and join-as-you-go construction

Join-as-you-go connects a motif while its last round is being made. A chain or stitch in the final round is replaced or supplemented by a joining action at a specified point on a neighboring motif. The exact substitutions must preserve the intended count and corner relationships.

![join_order (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/join_order.png)

Plan the order of assembly. A motif joined to one neighbor needs different joining opportunities from an internal motif touching several neighbors. Mark corners and identify whether a junction connects two, three, or four units. A vague instruction to “join at all corners” can create excess bulk or a hole if the junction method is undefined.

Continuous construction can reduce detached pieces but makes local replacement more complex. Keep a construction map so a damaged motif can be traced to its attachments later. Less seaming at the end does not mean no finishing or no structural decisions.

## Orientation and grain-like behavior

Crochet is not woven fabric, but it can behave differently along rows, across rows, and diagonally. A ridged cuff may stretch strongly in one direction. A heavy panel may lengthen along its stitch columns. Rotating a rectangular panel 90 degrees changes how those properties align with the body.

Test the orientation that the object will actually use. A swatch hanging from its foundation edge may not represent a panel that will be supported by its side edge. If the design combines rotated motifs, test the assembled arrangement rather than assuming all directions behave alike.

## A construction comparison exercise

Make two 12-stitch by 12-row single crochet rectangles using the non-counting ch-1 convention. Seam one rectangle’s two row-end edges together to create a short tube whose circumference is based on its stitch width. Compare it with a 12-stitch tube worked directly in rounds for a similar depth.

Use a measured seam sample and a stated round method. Compare the seam, inside surface, stretch, and actual circumference. The two tubes need not match exactly because seaming and flat-versus-round gauge can differ. This is an original comparison exercise; it has no promised finished size and has not been physically sample-tested for this edition.

## Join-as-you-go basics

Join-as-you-go (JAYGO) joins motifs on the last round instead of sewing them afterward.

1. Finish the first motif completely.
2. Work the second motif up to its last round.
3. On the last round, along the side that touches the first motif, replace each chain of a chain-space with a sl st (or sc) into the matching space of the first motif, then continue.
4. At corners, join to the corner space of the neighbor; where four motifs meet, join to the same corner point or to the previous join, following your pattern.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Join with the right sides up
> Keep every motif right side up as you join. One flipped motif is easy to miss until the blanket is finished.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Pick a corner rule and keep it
> Where four motifs meet, decide whether every new corner joins to the first motif's corner or to the last join made. Mixing both makes lumpy corners.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Lay out the whole blanket on the floor before you join. Take a photo. Then follow the photo, not your memory.

See Chapter 28 for planning layouts and join order.


> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Lifelines before lace
> Before a tricky lace section, thread a lifeline through the last good row. Frogging then stops exactly where you want.

> [!HINT] Walk away for five minutes
> When a mistake looks impossible, put the work down. Most fixes are obvious after a short break.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Fix it on the next row only if it doesn't show
> A missed stitch can sometimes be fixed by adding an increase on the next row, but only if it does not change the pattern or show on the right side.
