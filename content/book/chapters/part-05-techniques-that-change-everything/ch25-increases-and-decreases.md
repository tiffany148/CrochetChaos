Shaping changes a piece by changing how many new stitches replace the old ones. An increase makes more output stitches than the destinations it consumes. A decrease makes fewer. The amount, placement, and spacing determine the shape. Two rows with the same final count can have very different outlines if the shaping occurs in different places.

## Track inputs and outputs

Two single crochets in one stitch consume one destination and produce two stitches, a net gain of one. Three in one consume one and produce three, a net gain of two. Sc2tog consumes two and produces one, a net loss of one. A cluster across three stitches that produces one top has a net loss of two if the design counts that top as one stitch.

Skipping stitches also reduces destinations used, but the resulting shape differs from closing them together. A chain bridge over skipped stitches can maintain a planned open repeat rather than simply narrowing the fabric. Always account for all produced chains, spaces, and stitch units under the pattern’s counting convention.

## Increase at the edge or within the row

An edge increase changes the outline directly. A centered increase can expand a panel around a landmark. Distributed increases enlarge a circumference. Keep markers in important boundaries so the shaping remains aligned as counts change.

For a simple flat row that gains one stitch at each end, work two single crochets in the first stitch, one in each internal stitch, and two in the last. An input of 20 produces 22. On the following row, whether you increase again depends on the intended slope; automatic increasing every row can make a much steeper edge than planned.

An ordinary decrease near the edge can leave a different appearance from one placed one or two stitches inward. State the location. “Decrease at each end” is less reproducible than “sc in first st, sc2tog, work to last 3 sts, sc2tog, sc in last st.” The latter preserves one edge stitch on each side.

## Distribute increases evenly

To increase from 40 to 48 stitches with ordinary two-in-one increases, add eight stitches. Eight increase events are needed. If placed across eight equal input groups, each group consumes five old stitches. Work [sc in next 4 sts, 2 sc in next st] eight times. Each group produces six; eight groups produce 48.

That simple recipe is possible because 40 divides evenly by eight. For 42 to 50, eight increases still add eight, but the input spacing cannot all be five. Six groups can consume five and two can consume six, since 6 × 5 + 2 × 6 = 42. In each group, place one increase at the designated position. Spread the two longer groups rather than placing them together unless the design wants that distribution.

This creates groups producing six or seven stitches, respectively. Total output is 6 × 6 + 2 × 7 = 50. Write the actual sequence before crocheting. “About every fifth stitch” is a planning thought, not a final pattern instruction.

## Distribute decreases evenly

To decrease from 48 to 40 with sc2tog, make eight decreases. Divide the 48 input stitches into eight groups of six. Work [sc in next 4 sts, sc2tog] eight times. Each repeat consumes six and produces five. The row ends with 40.

Do not use the increase interval formula without checking what the decrease consumes. Sc2tog uses two destinations. A sequence that places the decrease “every sixth stitch” can be interpreted in several ways and should be expanded into consumed groups.

If you preserve fixed edge stitches, subtract them before distributing the internal shaping, then add them back afterward. For example, retaining one stitch at each edge of a 50-stitch row leaves 48 internal stitches. Eight internal sc2tog decreases reduce those 48 to 40, producing a total of 42 including the fixed edges.

## Schedule shaping over rows

Suppose a sleeve begins with 40 stitches and must reach 60. Each shaping row adds one at each end, gaining two. You need ten shaping rows. If the section contains 40 rows, one possible schedule shapes on Rows 4, 8, 12, and so on through Row 40. The intermediate rows are worked without changing the count.

That schedule includes a shaping event on the final row. If you need plain rows before a seam or want shaping to begin earlier, redistribute deliberately. Distinguish the number of events from the number of gaps between them. Ten events have nine gaps from first event to last, plus any leading and trailing rows.

At 5 rows per inch, a four-row interval is 0.8 inch. If your actual gauge is 6 rows per inch, the same schedule spans a different length. Recalculate the interval when row gauge changes.

## Model a flat circle

A familiar single crochet model begins with six stitches and adds six per round. Round n then has 6n stitches. A repeat for Round 5 can be [sc in next 3 sts, 2 sc in next st] six times: it consumes 24 and produces 30.

![circle_sc (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/circle_sc.png)

The recipe’s arithmetic is exact, but its flatness is conditional. Stitch proportions, yarn compression, and tension determine how much circumference the fabric needs at each radius. If the disk cups with correct counts, it may need a different distribution or rate. If it ruffles, it may have more circumference than it can lie flat with. Diagnose the sample rather than calling every non-flat result a counting error.

Stacking increases at identical angular positions can make visible corners. Staggering their positions can soften that appearance, while keeping the same total additions. The revised sequence must still consume the entire old round correctly and preserve any intended landmarks.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Write “events” above a shaping plan and “rows” above the actual schedule. They are related numbers, not the same number.
>
> Count the old stitches a repeat consumes before counting its new output. A repeat that cannot fit the input row cannot be repaired by a convincing-looking final total.
>
> An increase has a location as well as a quantity. Put markers at design landmarks so the shape grows where you intended.

## Flat circle increase schedules

Common schedules for flat circles worked in joined rounds or spirals. These follow from how much each stitch height needs to grow; they are standard starting points, and your yarn and tension may need small changes.

| Stitch | Round 1 | Increase per round | Stitches in round n |
|---|---|---|---|
| sc | 6 sc | 6 | 6n |
| hdc | 8 hdc | 8 | 8n |
| dc | 12 dc (or ch 3 + 11 dc) | 12 | 12n |

If a circle ruffles, it has too many stitches: skip an increase round. If it cups, it has too few: add an increase round or use more increases.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Stagger the increases
> If you always place increases at the same point, a sc circle turns into a hexagon. Shift the starting point each round (for example, start round 4 with half the repeat) to keep it round.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Even decreases: divide and round
> To spread 7 decreases across 60 stitches, divide 60 by 7 ≈ 8.6. Work 7 groups, each ending in a decrease: 3 groups of 8 stitches and 4 groups of 9 (3 × 8 + 4 × 9 = 60), mixed evenly around. The round ends with 53 stitches.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Lay your circle flat on the table every few rounds. The table never lies.
