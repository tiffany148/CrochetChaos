Finishing makes an object usable. It secures yarn ends, organizes edges, and provides the opening or fastening the design needs. A border can emphasize shape or restrain it. A closure can support a garment or pull it out of balance. Plan these features before the last row rather than treating them as decorative rescue operations.

## Secure ends with the material in mind

After fastening off the live loop, weave the tail through suitable fabric paths. Avoid pulling it so tight that the edge gathers. Change direction where appropriate to improve retention, and distribute the tail without forming a hard lump. The actual path depends on stitch structure, yarn friction, and whether the fabric is reversible.

A smooth slippery yarn may need a different strategy from a fuzzy wool. Chenille requires care to avoid stripping pile or damaging its core. Test the finishing method with realistic handling and laundering. Do not publish a single fixed tail length as a universal guarantee.

Joining yarn by a knot does not remove the need to evaluate that knot’s bulk and security. Some fibers can be spliced or joined by specialized methods, while others cannot. Use a method appropriate to the exact yarn and clearly distinguish a new-yarn join from securing the final end.

## Begin an edging on an understandable edge

Along stitch tops, one single crochet per actual stitch is a common starting point for a simple border. Along row ends, the correct distribution depends on physical row height relative to stitch width. One stitch per row end may draw in a tall-stitch edge; too many can ruffle it.

A useful planning rate is stitches per unit length along the edging divided by rows per unit length along the side. If the border’s sample gauge is 4 sc per inch and the side edge has 5 rows per inch, the initial estimate is 0.8 edging stitches per row end, or four stitches over five row ends. This is a starting distribution to sample, not a universal prescription.

Mark equal sections along the side and distribute the planned number within them. Inspect the edge after a short distance. If it flares, check whether there are too many stitches or an overly loose border. If it pulls inward, check too few stitches or overly tight tension. Also verify the base piece itself is not distorted.

## Corners

A border needs enough material to turn an outside corner without pulling, but the required number depends on stitch height, angle, and fabric. A familiar single crochet corner may use multiple stitches in one corner destination. A taller or more open border may use stitches separated by chains.

Mark the actual corner position and use the same defined treatment at corresponding corners. If you add three stitches at one corner and two at another unintentionally, the later rounds can drift. Count each side and define whether corner stitches or spaces belong to the side totals.

Inside corners require a different response from outside corners. A neckline V may need a reduction where a blanket corner needs expansion. Do not copy the same three-stitch treatment into both.

## Picots and corded edges

A picot is a small projection formed by chains secured back into a specified earlier point. For one defined ch-3 picot, ch 3 and slip stitch into the first chain of those three. Its attachment and spacing must be stated in the border pattern. Some versions close into the base stitch instead; the name alone does not identify the method.

Reverse single crochet, described in Chapter 18, produces a corded-looking edge. Slip-stitch edging can make a slim line but may tighten the perimeter. Sample the actual edge and use an appropriate hook and tension. Decorative firmness is useful only when it supports the object’s intended movement.

## Button bands

A button band needs enough structure to support the closure without excessive stretch or curling. Make the band in the actual stitch, yarn, and orientation. A band crocheted along row ends can behave differently from one made separately in short rows and sewn on.

Plan the closed garment configuration. Overlapping bands do not contribute their full combined width to the finished circumference. Place the buttons and buttonholes so the garment hangs as intended, then check for gaping between them during movement.

## Horizontal buttonhole sample

Have the actual button available. Make a sample band using the proposed yarn, hook, stitch, and width. A simple horizontal opening can chain across and skip a group of stitches, then work into those chains on the next row. Equal chain and skipped-stitch counts preserve nominal positions, but the opening’s physical size still needs testing.

Original count-based sample: ch 13. Rows 1–3: work 12 sc per row, beginning Row 1 in second chain and using a non-counting ch-1 on later rows. Row 4: ch 1, turn; sc in first 5 sts, ch 2, skip 2 sts, sc in last 5 sts. Row 5: ch 1, turn; sc in first 5 sc, sc in each of two chains, sc in last 5 sc; 12 sc. Rows 6–7: ch 1, turn; sc in each st; 12 sc. Fasten off and secure ends.

Status: original prototype; counts checked, physical opening size and retention pending. Test the intended button repeatedly through the washed opening. It should pass without damaging the fabric and remain secured during intended use. If it fails, revise the opening or band; do not force the button until the yarn stretches permanently and call that sizing.

## Sew on buttons securely

Use thread or yarn compatible with the button holes, fabric, and care. Place the button on the reinforced area and attach through sound fabric strands rather than one weak surface loop. Provide any needed clearance for the thickness of the overlapping band. A sewn thread shank or a shanked button may be appropriate, but its size must suit the actual layers.

Inspect attachment after handling and washing. Small detachable parts are unsuitable where they can be swallowed or chewed. For children’s and pet items, choose the closure and construction according to their use and applicable product requirements rather than assuming a tightly sewn button removes every risk.

## Zippers, snaps, and hook-and-loop tape

A zipper needs a stable attachment line and enough opening length. Pin or baste it in place without stretching the crochet, then assess its hang before permanent sewing. A wavy zipper can result from unequal feed or an unstable edge. Use a compatible reinforcement or construction when the sample shows it is needed.

Snaps concentrate force at their attachment points. Reinforce appropriately and test opening without tearing or distorting the fabric. Hook-and-loop tape can catch yarn, collect lint, or irritate skin if exposed. Orient and cover it according to the design, and sample how it behaves with the chosen yarn.

Do not promise a closure’s strength or suitability solely from its name. The hardware, attachment, fabric, care, and intended movement all contribute.

## Final inspection

Lay the object flat and inspect every edge. Check that corners match, bands lie comfortably, openings remain usable, seams are secure, and no pins or temporary markers remain. Try closures in the actual closed configuration. Review both faces for tails, weak strands, or loose loops.

For a garment, perform a final fit check after the intended finishing treatment. For a sample pattern, record the actual finished measurements and consumption. If these differ from the plan, update the pattern rather than leaving the attractive prediction beside a contradictory sample.

> Buy or choose the button before sizing its hole. Finding a button to fit an accidental opening is a very specific kind of treasure hunt.

## Weaving in ends securely

- Leave tails at least 6 in long.
- Weave on the wrong side, through the backs of stitches, in at least two directions (for example, along a row and then back, or in a zigzag).
- For slippery yarns (cotton, bamboo, silk), weave a little further and split the plies when you turn direction.
- Trim close only after blocking, so the tail is not pulled back out.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Crochet over the tails
> When starting a new row or color, lay the tail along the top of the row and crochet over it for several stitches. You will have far fewer ends to weave in later.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Three stitches into a corner
> On a sc border, 3 sc in each corner stitch keeps a square corner flat. On a dc border, many patterns use (2 dc, ch 2, 2 dc) or (3 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) in the corner space.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> A border is a frame. Even a beginner's blanket looks finished with a calm, even edge.


> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Wash a test swatch with the real soap
> Wool wash, detergent and fabric softener can each change how a fabric feels. Test with the same products you will use.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Darn with matching fiber
> Repair with the same fiber where you can. Cotton patches on wool can shrink at a different rate.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Photograph before frogging
> Take a photo before ripping back a big section. It reminds you what you changed and why.
