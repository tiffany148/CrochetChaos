A motif is a self-contained unit with its own count, shape, and joining edge. Repeating motifs turns small pieces into a larger fabric, but the assembly has its own gauge. Make and finish several units, then join a sample before estimating the size of a blanket or garment.

The traditional granny group contains three complete double crochets worked together in one destination. It is not a shared-top cluster. Its three separate stitch tops and the spaces between groups make the characteristic fabric. Many other squares are called granny squares informally; read their actual construction rather than assuming the name defines it.

## Three-round corner-space square

This is an original instructional rendition of a traditional construction, not a claim to have invented the granny square. Status: counts audited, physical sample pending. Use smooth yarn, a suitable hook, and markers. US terms. The beginning ch-3 counts as one dc. Joining slip stitches do not count. Work into spaces, not individual chains, when a space is named. Do not turn.

![granny_chart (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/granny_chart.png)

Foundation: ch 4 and sl st to first chain to form a ring.

Round 1: ch 3, 2 dc in ring; [ch 2, 3 dc in ring] three times; ch 2, sl st to top of beginning ch-3. There are four 3-dc groups, 12 dc including the beginning chain, and four corner ch-2 spaces. Fasten off.

Round 2: join yarn with a non-counting slip stitch in any corner ch-2 space. Ch 3, then (2 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) in that same space to complete the first corner. Repeat [ch 1, (3 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) in next corner space] three times. Ch 1 and join to top of beginning ch-3. There are 24 dc, four corner ch-2 spaces, and four side ch-1 spaces. Fasten off.

Round 3: join in any corner space. Ch 3, then (2 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) in the same space. Repeat [ch 1, 3 dc in next side ch-1 space, ch 1, (3 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) in next corner] three times. Ch 1, 3 dc in the final side ch-1 space, ch 1, and join to the beginning ch-3. There are 36 dc, four corner ch-2 spaces, and eight side ch-1 spaces. Fasten off and secure all ends.

The fresh join at each round avoids needing a travel sequence to reach a corner. It creates additional ends. A continuous single-color version can use another documented start method, but its travel stitches and counts must be specified rather than silently inserted.

## Extend the square

In each later round, work a full corner of (3 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) in each corner space. Work 3 dc in every side ch-1 space, with ch 1 between neighboring groups along each side. Begin and finish with the same counted-chain and joining conventions as above.

At Round n, this version has 4n granny groups, or 12n dc, plus four corner spaces and 4(n − 1) side spaces. Round 4 therefore has 48 dc and 12 side ch-1 spaces. Count the units separately; do not add the space count to the double crochet count and label the result dc.

If the square twists or bows, verify counts, corner placement, tension, and round orientation. Alternating turning directions is a possible design variant, but it changes the sample method. Record and test the variant rather than treating it as a guaranteed cure for every yarn.

## Original five-petal motif exercise

Status: original count-based flower study, physically untested. Make 10 sc in an adjustable ring, with a non-counting ch-1; mark the first sc and close the ring gently. Join with a slip stitch to the first sc. Round 2: repeat [sl st in next sc, (hdc, 3 dc, hdc) in next sc] five times. Join to the first actual slip stitch of the petal round; that final join does not count. Fasten off and secure ends.

Each repeat consumes two base stitches and makes one slip stitch plus five taller stitches. The petal round contains 5 worked slip stitches, 10 hdc, and 15 dc, or 30 actual stitch units excluding the final join. The petal shape and flatness require sampling. Do not promise a particular diameter or rigid outline without measuring it.

## Centers, sides, and corners are different regions

A circular center can be enclosed in a square only when later rounds provide suitable corner expansion and side spacing. Adding four arbitrary chain loops does not establish that the circle is squared correctly. Check the transition count, the resulting outline, and whether the fabric lies as intended.

Likewise, a six-sided motif is not made by adding two corners to a square’s existing increase rate. Corner number and expansion interact. Test each polygon’s full schedule rather than deriving every motif from its name alone.

## Join a motif grid

Lay out the whole arrangement before permanent joining. Mark orientation and color order. Check that each motif has the intended round count and treatment. If some units differ in size, investigate gauge before forcing them together.

For a square grid, identify corners and distribute side-edge attachments between them. Decide whether corner spaces are joined once at a junction or treated separately along crossing seams. The intersection of four motifs needs an explicit plan so it does not become a bulky knot or an unintended hole.

Join-as-you-go requires replacement instructions for particular final-round chains or stitches. A complete design should state the first motif, edge motifs, and interior junctions separately. The small square above is finished independently; it does not include an undefined join-as-you-go substitution.

## Measure assembled contribution

If a finished motif measures 5 inches across, four unjoined motifs suggest 20 inches, but seams can add or consume width. Join two, measure their combined width, and record the difference from 10 inches. Use a representative larger junction sample when the method changes at crossings.

For color planning, label which rounds use each yarn and estimate their consumption separately. Outer rounds usually contain more stitches than inner rounds, so equal numbers of colored rounds do not imply equal quantities of each color.

## Motif audit exercise

Make three squares from the same instructions and one flower. Record counts, dimensions before and after care, and end-finishing method. Join two squares with one seam method and compare the third as an unjoined reference. Record the actual seam contribution and corner behavior.

This exercise creates evidence for the chosen materials. It does not establish a universal size for a three- round square or a universal joining allowance. Save the samples so the later pattern portfolio can report tested observations rather than remembered estimates.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Label the round count before assembling a pile of squares. One missing round can hide surprisingly well in a colorful stack.
>
> Count groups and corners first. They are often easier to audit than a long perimeter of individual stitches.
>
> Join a few units before making the full set. The seam can change both the look and the final size of the project.

## Motif shapes and their corners

| Motif | Corners | Typical corner group (dc motifs) | Joins well with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Square | 4 | (3 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) | Squares, rectangles |
| Hexagon | 6 | (3 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) or (2 dc, ch 2, 2 dc) | Hexagons, half-hexagons at edges |
| Triangle | 3 | (3 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) or a larger group | Triangles, hexagons |
| Octagon | 8 | Smaller groups | Octagons with small squares in the gaps |
| Circle in a square | 4 (added last) | Taller stitches at corners | Squares |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Turn each round to stop the twist
> Granny squares worked without turning can twist slightly, because each round leans the same way. Turning the work every round (or blocking firmly) helps keep the square straight.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Start rounds in a different corner
> Starting each round in the same corner leaves a visible seam. Start in a different corner or side space, or use a standing stitch, to hide it.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Make every square the same size before you join. Block them as a stack and they will agree.

> [!HINT] Keep a repair kit
> A small tin with a tapestry needle, a crochet hook, locking markers and scraps of each project yarn makes mending easy.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Mending is part of making. A darned elbow tells a story.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Find the row before you frog
> Mark the row with the mistake with a locking marker before pulling anything. Frogging is easier when you know where to stop.
