A granny-square blanket is really two projects. The first is making the squares. The second, which is where most of the magic happens, is deciding where each square goes. A thoughtful layout turns a pile of leftovers into a design; a hurried one can leave two identical squares kissing in the middle of the blanket where everyone will notice. This chapter turns placement into a plan you can count, check and photograph before a single seam is sewn.

## Step 1: Measure one finished square

Make and block one square in the yarn and hook you will use. Measure it flat, side to side through the center, and diagonally if your blanket will be joined on point. Write down the measurement after blocking, because blocking often adds a little size and squares up the corners.

Then decide how you will join. Joins add width. A slip-stitch or whipstitch join adds very little; a single crochet ridge or a join-as-you-go round adds a measurable amount; a decorative joining round such as a flat braid adds more. Measure a joined pair: lay two joined squares flat and measure across both. Subtract twice the single-square width to find the net join width.

## Step 2: Do the size arithmetic

For a grid of *C* columns and *R* rows of squares of finished width *s*, with net join width *j* between neighbors:

- Width before border = *C* × *s* + (*C* − 1) × *j*
- Length before border = *R* × *s* + (*R* − 1) × *j*
- Total squares = *C* × *R*

A worked example: 6-inch squares, a join that adds 0.125 inch, 8 columns and 10 rows. Width = 8 × 6 + 7 × 0.125 = 48.875 inches. Length = 10 × 6 + 9 × 0.125 = 61.125 inches. Total squares = 80. A border adds its own depth twice to each dimension (once per side).

| Blanket use (planning examples only) | Approximate finished size, inches | 6-in squares, before join and border |
|---|---|---|
| Stroller or car-seat blanket | 30 × 35 | 5 × 6 = 30 squares (30 × 36 in) |
| Baby / receiving blanket | 36 × 36 | 6 × 6 = 36 squares |
| Lap throw | 48 × 60 | 8 × 10 = 80 squares |
| Twin bed top (no drop) | 39 × 75 | 6 × 12 = 72 squares (36 × 72 in) |

> [!STATUS]
> The blanket sizes in this table are common planning targets used by many designers, not a published standard. The square counts are exact arithmetic for 6-inch squares; your real size depends on your measured square, your join and your border.

## Step 3: Choose a placement system

Six reliable systems cover almost every granny blanket. Each figure below was generated by a formula or a seeded rule, so the counts in the legend are exact for that grid. Letters stand for complete colorways (a whole square's color recipe), not individual rounds.

### Checkerboard

The simplest two-colorway plan. Colorway = (row + column) mod 2. On an even number of squares the two colorways split exactly in half.

![lay_checker (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/lay_checker.png)

### Diagonal bands

Colorway = (row + column) mod *k* for *k* colorways. Bands of color run corner to corner and wrap around. With 6 colorways on an 8 × 10 grid the counts are 13, 14, 14, 14, 13 and 12, so you need slightly different numbers of each square; the legend tells you exactly how many.

![lay_diagonal (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/lay_diagonal.png)

### No square touches its twin

The four-colorway formula colorway = (row + 2 × column) mod 4 guarantees that no square shares a colorway with any of its eight neighbors, including the diagonal ones. That property was checked for the full grid by computer. On an 8 × 8 grid each colorway appears exactly 16 times.

![lay_noneighbor (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/lay_noneighbor.png)

### Frames

Rings of color move inward from the border: colorway = (distance from the nearest edge) mod 3. Frames look especially striking with a dark outer ring that ties into a dark border.

![Decorative generated crochet art](../../assets/art/fill/p05-hexagons.jpg)

![lay_frame (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/lay_frame.png)

### Stripes

Every two rows of squares share a colorway. Stripes are the easiest layout to assemble by rows and the easiest to extend later.

![Decorative generated crochet art](../../assets/art/fill/p00-coasters.jpg)

![lay_stripes (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/lay_stripes.png)

### Planned scrappy

A "random" look that is not random at all. Squares were placed one at a time, left to right and top to bottom, and each was given a colorway not used by any already-placed neighbor (left, upper-left, above, upper-right). A fixed seed makes the layout reproducible. Counts are uneven by nature, which is part of the charm.

![lay_scrappy (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/lay_scrappy.png)

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
> Lay the squares out on a bed or floor, stand on a chair, and take a photo. Then convert the photo to black and white on your phone. Value (lightness and darkness) jumps out in grayscale, and you will immediately see the two dark squares that clump together.
>
> Number the back of each square with a safety pin or a paper tag in row-and-column order (R1C1, R1C2 …) before you pick them up. Stack each row in order. Assembly becomes paint-by-numbers.
>
> If you are making squares from leftovers, keep a tally of how many of each center color you have made. It is much easier to make two more of the color you are short of than to redesign the layout at the end.

## Step 4: Plan the join order

![join_order (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/join_order.png)

**Sewn or crocheted after.** Join squares into rows first, then join the rows. Line up corners with locking markers before you begin each seam so the rows do not drift.

**Join-as-you-go (JAYGO).** Each new square is joined on its last round to squares already in place. Work along the first row, then begin the second row at the same side. Each square then has at most two finished neighbors (left and above) waiting for it, and the four corners meet cleanly when every square joins into the same corner space.

**Corners.** Where four squares meet, small holes can form. Join each corner into the same corner space, or work a slip stitch into the shared joining point, so all four meet at one spot.

## Step 5: Estimate yarn per colorway

Weigh one blocked finished square (use a scale that reads in grams; a single square is light). For a square of several colors, weigh the yarn for each round as you make it, or unravel a test square and measure each color's length.

Yarn for a colorway = (weight per square) × (number of squares of that colorway) × a safety allowance. Many crocheters allow an extra 10 to 15 percent for joining and the occasional redo; that allowance is a planning choice, not a measured result. Add the border and join yarn separately, based on a measured sample length per inch of border.

> [!GRANNY] Granny’s Wisdom
> I always made two extra squares, one in the most-used colorway and one in the least. If a square was stained or puckered, I had a spare. If not, the spares became a matching pillow. Nothing was wasted, and I never had to match a dye lot five years later.

## The classic granny, round by round

The traditional granny square is the foundation for most placement plans. This version uses 3-dc clusters, ch-2 corners and ch-1 side spaces (one widely used convention; some patterns use ch-1 or ch-3 corners, which changes the shape slightly).

![granny_chart (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/granny_chart.png)

> [!PATTERN] Classic Granny Square (counts checked by calculation)
> **Rnd 1:** Ch 4, sl st to first ch to form a ring (or use a magic ring). Ch 3 (counts as dc), 2 dc in ring, ch 2, [3 dc in ring, ch 2] 3 times; join with sl st to top of beginning ch-3. (4 clusters, 12 dc, 4 ch-2 corner sps)
>
> **Rnd 2:** Sl st in next 2 dc and into the ch-2 sp. Ch 3 (counts as dc), (2 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) in same sp, ch 1, *(3 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) in next ch-2 sp, ch 1; rep from * 2 more times; join. (8 clusters, 24 dc)
>
> **Rnd 3:** Sl st to the corner sp; ch 3, (2 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) in same sp, ch 1, 3 dc in next ch-1 sp, ch 1, *(3 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) in corner sp, ch 1, 3 dc in next ch-1 sp, ch 1; rep from * 2 more times; join. (12 clusters, 36 dc)
>
> **Every later round:** Work a corner group in each corner sp and one 3-dc cluster in each ch-1 sp along the sides, with ch 1 between groups. Round *n* has 4*n* clusters and 12*n* dc.
>
> **Status:** standard construction; counts verified arithmetically. Not physically re-tested for this edition.

> [!HINT]
> When a granny square twists into a spiral instead of lying square, check that you are turning (or not turning) consistently and starting each round in a corner. Many crocheters turn the square over at the end of each round to counter the natural lean of right-handed stitches.

## Borders that pull a layout together

A border frames the layout like a mat around a picture. Choose a border color from a colorway that appears around the outer edge, or a neutral that appears in every square. Work at least one round of single crochet or half double crochet first, with three stitches in each corner, to give the border a stable base; then add granny clusters, shells or a picot edge. Measure the border on two sides after the first round: if the edge ruffles, reduce stitches; if it cups, add a few.
