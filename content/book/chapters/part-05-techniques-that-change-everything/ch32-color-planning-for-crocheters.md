Color is the first thing anyone notices about a piece of crochet, and it is the part many crocheters find hardest to plan. The good news is that a few practical ideas, mostly about lightness and darkness, do most of the work. This chapter covers how to choose colors that read clearly, how to plan stripes and granny layouts, and how to test a palette before you buy a blanket's worth of yarn.

> [!STATUS]
> Color preference is personal. The guidance here describes common design practice and simple tests; none of it is a rule you must follow.

## Value matters more than hue

**Hue** is the name of a color: red, blue, green. **Value** is how light or dark it is. In colorwork, value does most of the work of making a pattern readable. Two very different hues of the same value (a mid red and a mid green, for example) can blur together from a distance, while two shades of the same blue, one light and one dark, will show a tapestry motif clearly.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Take a black-and-white photo
> Lay your yarn balls side by side and photograph them in grayscale (most phone cameras have a mono filter). Colors that look the same gray will not separate in tapestry, mosaic or C2C. This one test prevents most colorwork disappointments.

## Contrast for each technique

| Technique | How much value contrast it needs | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Tapestry crochet | High | The motif is made of single stitches; low contrast blurs the edges |
| Overlay and inset mosaic | High | The pattern is made of long stitches over a background; it disappears without contrast |
| C2C pictures | Medium to high | Tiles are larger than stitches, so medium contrast can still read |
| Filet crochet | One color; relies on light behind the work | The picture is made of holes and blocks |
| Stripes | Any | Low contrast gives a gentle blend; high contrast gives bold bands |
| Granny squares | Any; plan the centers and the joining color | Joining color frames every square |

## Building a palette

Three approaches crocheters use often:

- **One color family plus a neutral.** Several values of one hue with cream, gray or charcoal. Calm and easy to get right.
- **Neighbors on the color wheel.** Colors that sit next to each other (for example blue, teal and green). Harmonious; add one darker value for definition.
- **Opposites for accents.** Colors across the wheel from each other (for example orange with blue). Use one as the main color and the other sparingly, so they do not fight.

A common workable proportion is one dominant color, one or two secondary colors, and a small amount of accent. Treat it as a starting point, not a formula.

> [!HINT] The neutral does the heavy lifting
> In granny-square blankets, the joining and border color appears around every square. Choose it first, then pick the square colors to look good against it.

## Planning stripes

Stripes are the easiest way to use several colors, and they repay a little planning.

- **Regular stripes.** The same number of rows in each color, repeated. Calm and orderly.
- **Graduated stripes.** Widths that grow or shrink, such as 1, 2, 3, 4 rows. Movement in one direction.
- **Fibonacci stripes.** Widths from the sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 (each number is the sum of the two before). A popular way to get irregular-looking stripes that still feel balanced.
- **Random stripes.** Truly random choices often clump. Many crocheters instead write out a sequence in advance and check that no color appears twice in a row.

> [!HINT] Even stripes and color carrying
> In rows, stripes of an even number of rows end at the same edge they started, so the unused color can be carried up the side instead of cut. Stripes of an odd number of rows end at the opposite edge.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Write the stripe sequence down
> A planned sequence written in your notes, with row numbers, saves you from unpicking a stripe you repeated by mistake. Tick it off as you go in your own notebook.

## Planning granny-square color

For blankets made of many squares, decide two things separately:

1. **The color order inside each square** (which color for Round 1, Round 2, and so on).
2. **Where each square goes** in the layout (see Chapter 28).

Common strategies inside a square: keep the last round the same for every square, so the blanket is unified; or keep the center the same and vary the outer rounds.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Lay every finished square on the floor before you join a single one. Take a photograph, walk away, and look at the photograph later. The square that bothers you will jump out.

## Variegated, self-striping and speckled yarns

These yarns change color along their length, so the result depends on stitch count and stitch height.

| Yarn type | What tends to happen | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Variegated (short color changes) | Colors can pool into blotches | Moss stitch, smaller pieces, or planned pooling |
| Self-striping (long color changes) | Wide rows give thin stripes; narrow rows give wide stripes | Choose width to suit the stripe length |
| Speckled | Flecks spread evenly in most stitches | Plain stitches show speckles best |
| Gradient (one long fade) | One slow color shift across the piece | Plan where the fade will sit; work from one end |

## Testing a palette before you buy

- Wrap a few turns of each candidate yarn around a strip of card in the proportions you plan to use. The card shows how much of each color the eye will see.
- Make one small motif or a short stripe sample in each candidate combination.
- Look at samples in daylight and in the light where the item will live.
- Do the grayscale photo test for any colorwork.

## Reading color in charts

Charts use symbols or colored squares. A chart's colors are for clarity; they do not have to match your yarn. Before starting, write a key that pairs each chart color with your actual yarn, and keep it with the chart.

## Quick reference

| Goal | Try |
|---|---|
| Make a motif stand out | Increase value contrast |
| Make a blanket calmer | Use a single neutral for joins and border |
| Use up many leftovers | One unifying joining color |
| Make stripes look less regular | Fibonacci or a written random sequence |
| Avoid pooling in variegated yarn | Change stitch or piece width |
| Check colorwork before stitching | Grayscale photo |
