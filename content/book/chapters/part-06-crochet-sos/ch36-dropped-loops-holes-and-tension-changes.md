Different faults can create similar-looking openings. A live loop that slipped off the hook, a missed stitch, a broken strand, and a deliberately chained space require different responses. Identify which yarn path failed before deciding how to repair the appearance.

## Recover a dropped active loop

If the hook slips out and no yarn has been pulled, find the enlarged live loop and insert a locking marker through it. Reinsert the hook in the orientation that lets it continue without twisting the loop. Gently adjust its size on the shaft.

If one or more stitches have opened, trace back to the last completed stitch and identify the surviving live loop. Rework the lost stitches from the instructions. Ordinary crochet does not necessarily create the same vertical ladder as a dropped knitted stitch, so do not apply a knitting ladder-repair sequence without verifying the actual structure.

## A missed stitch with intact yarn

If the current row skips an unintended destination and later rows preserve the narrower count, the intact fabric may remain connected but differ in width or pattern. The most direct correction from an accessible live edge is to unravel to the missed stitch and rework.

A decorative surface stitch can hide a visual gap but does not necessarily restore the missing column or the original fit. Decide whether the issue is appearance, count, or structural geometry. Do not label a cosmetic cover as a full reconstruction.

## A pulled loop or snag

A snag can draw yarn from neighboring parts of a stitch or row. Do not cut the protruding loop: it may be the same continuous yarn supporting the fabric. Inspect both faces and trace which nearby strands became tightened.

Using a blunt tool, ease a small amount of slack gradually back along the identified path, comparing neighboring stitch sizes. Work over a supported surface and avoid splitting the yarn. If the strand is abraded or broken, redistributing it does not restore strength; a mend may still be needed.

Try the technique first on a sample with a deliberately enlarged loop. This is controlled retensioning, not a guarantee that every snag will disappear. Heavily felted, fragile, or complex yarns may not tolerate movement.

## A broken strand

Support the damaged region and secure visible free loops or ends temporarily with removable markers. Inspect how far the weakness extends. A single visible break can be surrounded by abrasion that makes a tiny local repair insufficient.

Do not trim the ends before planning the repair. Their length may be needed for reconstruction or finishing. For an internal stitch reconstruction, the replacement yarn path must be identified from an undamaged comparison or duplicate sample. If that path is uncertain, choose a supported patch, component replacement, or specialist repair.

## An original sewn-patch practice repair

This exercise covers a planned opening without cutting any fabric. Status: original structural practice, physically untested. It demonstrates attachment and does not claim an invisible repair or a load rating.

Base: ch 21. Rows 1–4: work 20 sc per row with a non-counting ch-1. Row 5: ch 1, turn; sc in first 8 sts, ch 4, skip 4 sts, sc in last 8 sts. Row 6: ch 1, turn; sc in first 8 sc, sc in each of four chains, sc in final 8 sc; 20 sc. Rows 7–10: ch 1, turn, sc in each stitch; 20 sc. Fasten off and secure ends.

Patch: ch 11 and work six rows of 10 sc with the same non-counting ch-1. Finish the patch using the same permitted care procedure as the base. At matching gauge, its intended attachment footprint covers a 10- position-wide and six-row-high region centered around the opening. Check the actual overlap before sewing; counts alone do not establish physical coverage.

Place the patch on the intended inside face and baste it over the opening so sound fabric surrounds it on all sides. Sew around its perimeter through sound base strands, using small, distributed stitches that do not gather either piece. Secure the seam ends. Remove basting and inspect both faces for exposed loops, gaps, and distortion.

This repair leaves a visible backing behind the slit and adds a second fabric layer. It does not reconstruct the original missing stitches. The distinction matters when selecting it for a garment, a decorative item, or a region that must stretch.

## Tension changes without broken yarn

Measure across several sections with the same stitch count. A progressively narrowing fabric can result from tightened loops or a changing yarn feed. A single tight carried strand can narrow colorwork while the visible stitches look normal.

If the piece is still in progress, return to the last compatible gauge region and remake the affected work when necessary. A minor finishing adjustment may even out small variations, but do not use severe blocking to disguise a persistent construction mismatch.

## A repair decision table

| Observation | Initial action | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Live loop simply off hook | Secure and reinsert hook | That no earlier stitches opened |
| Intact but missed stitch | Audit count and accessible rollback | That a surface cover restores width |
| Pulled unbroken loop | Trace and redistribute cautiously | That abraded yarn regained strength |
| Broken yarn | Retain ends and secure region | That surrounding fabric is sound |
| Large worn area | Assess patch or component replacement | That a tiny local stitch is sufficient |
| Gauge drift | Measure and investigate method | That blocking can make it permanent |

## Practice and record

Keep the patched sample beside an unpatched base made with the same opening. Compare flexibility and thickness before and after the intended care treatment. Record attachment paths and any failure. For a real repair, select the method according to the object’s use rather than which sample looks most invisible in a photograph.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Do not cut a snag to make it look tidy. First find out whether it is holding the rest of the fabric together.
>
> Keep damaged yarn ends as long as possible until the repair is planned. Short ends reduce your finishing options.
>
> A visible patch can be a successful repair. Calling it invisible does not make its extra thickness disappear.

## Tension change map

| Situation | Typical effect | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Relaxed evening crochet | Looser stitches | Re-check gauge mid-project |
| Stressful day | Tighter stitches | Slow down; take breaks |
| New hook brand | Gauge change | Swatch with the new hook |
| Long break from the project | Different tension | Work a few practice stitches on a scrap |
| Different chair or posture | Tension can change | Crochet in the same place for the same project |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Your tension changes with your mood
> It sounds silly, but many crocheters stitch tighter when stressed. If a project's rows suddenly change width, check the date—you may have worked them during a busy week.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Repair a broken strand with a new tail
> If yarn breaks in the fabric, undo back to a point where you have 6 in tails, join new yarn, and weave both tails in. Knots in the middle of a fabric can work loose and show.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Crochet is patient. If your hands are tight today, put it down and come back tomorrow.


> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Hats stretch more in width than in length
> Many hat fabrics stretch more around than up. Check height on the head, not just circumference.

> [!HINT] Use waste yarn to mark length
> Thread a strand of contrasting waste yarn at the target length on a garment piece; it is easier to see than a marker.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> When in doubt, make it a little longer. A sweater too short is never comfortable.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Weigh one sleeve
> Weigh the first sleeve when finished. You will know whether you have enough yarn for the second before you start it.
