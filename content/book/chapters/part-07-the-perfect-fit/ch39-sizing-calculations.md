Sizing begins with a measured goal and a measured fabric. Keep units attached to every number. Distinguish the body, the finished object, and the unfinished pieces. A mathematically correct multiplication can still produce the wrong garment if it used the wrong type of measurement.

## Body, ease, and finished size

Finished circumference equals body circumference plus intended ease. Positive ease adds room. Negative ease makes the relaxed object smaller than the body and depends on suitable stretch, recovery, and comfort. Negative ease should never be chosen solely because a fabric can be pulled far enough once.

For a 38-inch chest and a chosen 4 inches of positive ease, the target finished circumference is 42 inches. At 4 stitches per inch, that suggests 168 stitches around. If two equal flat panels form the circumference, each has a nominal 21-inch finished contribution before accounting for seams, bands, or asymmetric details.

Do not add the entire circumference’s ease to each panel. Four inches of total ease distributed across two equal panels is two inches per panel. Doubling the allowance doubles the intended ease accidentally.

## Counts from measurements

For a straight section, stitch count = target width × stitches per unit. Row count = target length × rows per unit. The result must then be reconciled with whole stitches, repeat multiples, edge conventions, and construction.

If a target width is 13.5 inches at 3.6 stitches per inch, the raw count is 48.6 stitches. You cannot work six- tenths of an ordinary stitch. A plain fabric might use 49 and accept the corresponding width of approximately 13.61 inches. A multiple-of-6-plus-1 pattern might also permit 49. Another repeat might require a different nearby count.

Always calculate backward after rounding. Actual planned width = chosen count ÷ gauge. That step reveals the physical effect of the rounding instead of hiding it in a count that looks convenient.

## Repeat compatibility

Suppose a fabric needs 8n + 2 stitches, including fixed edges. The nearest compatible totals to a raw target of 61 are 58 and 66. At 4 stitches per inch, their widths are 14.5 and 16.5 inches. The difference is substantial. You might choose a different stitch pattern, adjust the panel layout, or accept a documented different width.

Do not add a random partial repeat at the edge unless you know how it connects. Lace, cables, and color motifs can require special partial-repeat instructions. A plain side panel can sometimes absorb a remainder, but that is an intentional design decision with its own gauge.

## Seams and bands

A seam can consume width or create a ridge, depending on its method and placement. Rather than assuming a universal seam allowance in stitches, make a representative seam sample. Measure the joined fabric and compare it with the two unjoined panels.

A front band adds width to an open cardigan, but overlap changes how that width contributes when fastened. A button band and buttonhole band may overlap rather than simply adding both full widths to the wearing circumference. Draw and measure the closed configuration you intend.

For a border around a rectangle, a border depth b adds approximately 2b to each outside dimension if it lies flat and grows equally at both opposing sides. A 10-by-14-inch center with a 1-inch border becomes approximately 12 by 16 inches. Corner construction and blocking can alter the actual result, so verify the sample.

## Percentage ease

If ease is expressed as a percentage of body measurement, finished circumference = body × (1 + ease fraction). For 40 inches and 10 percent positive ease, 40 × 1.10 = 44 inches. For 10 percent negative ease, 40 × 0.90 = 36 inches.

Percentages must identify their base. A 4-inch difference is 10 percent of a 40-inch body, but approximately 9.09 percent of a 44-inch garment. Use the body as the base when following the formulas here. Do not mix percentage ease and stretch percentage without defining each.

## Circles and cylinders

For an ideal geometric circle, circumference = pi × diameter. A target 20-inch circumference corresponds to a diameter of about 6.37 inches. That can help plan a hat crown, but crochet fabric has thickness, stretch, and a transition into sides. The geometric diameter is a starting model, not a verified hat-fitting rule.

For a straight tube at 4 stitches per inch and a target circumference of 20 inches, the nominal count is 80. Its flat width is approximately 10 inches when laid without stretching. Do not multiply that flat width by pi; doubling a laid-flat tube width is the relevant approximate circumference relationship here.

## Convert units consistently

One inch equals exactly 2.54 cm. Convert a body measurement once, then keep the calculation in that system. If gauge is 1.5 stitches per cm and target circumference is 90 cm, the raw count is 135. Do not multiply 90 cm by a stitches-per-inch gauge without converting one quantity.

| Quantity | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Finished circumference | Body + ease | 38 + 4 = 42 in |
| Raw stitch count | Width × stitch gauge | 42 × 4 = 168 sts |
| Planned width | Chosen count ÷ stitch gauge | 168 ÷ 4 = 42 in |
| Raw row count | Length × row gauge | 18 × 5 = 90 rows |
| Planned length | Chosen rows ÷ row gauge | 90 ÷ 5 = 18 in |
| Percent change | (New − old) ÷ old × 100 | (44 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = 10% |

## A complete panel calculation

You want a finished seamed rectangular panel contribution 12 inches wide by 16 inches long. Your washed fabric has 4 stitches and 5 rows per inch. A seam sample indicates that the chosen assembly uses the equivalent of one stitch-width at each side of the panel. The desired visible width is 48 stitch-widths; add two for the measured seam behavior to plan 50 stitches.

The length target is 80 rows if the top and bottom have no additional measured take-up. If a later border contributes 1 inch at each end and the 16-inch target includes it, the center should instead contribute 14 inches, or 70 rows. Record which dimensions include the border and seam.

The seam allowance in this scenario is observed for its hypothetical sample. It is not a general instruction to add two stitches to every crochet garment. Your own seam sample must establish your allowance.

## Sizing exercise

At 4.5 stitches and 6 rows per inch, calculate a plain rectangle 10 inches wide by 12 inches long. The raw counts are 45 stitches and 72 rows. If a repeat requires 4n + 1 stitches, 45 is compatible. If a four-row repeat must end at a complete cycle, 72 is also compatible.

Now change the width to 10.5 inches. The raw count is 47.25. Nearby 4n + 1 totals are 45 and 49, producing 10 and approximately 10.89 inches. State which result you choose and why, or change the design. There is no invisible rounding method that makes both counts equal 10.5 inches.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Write the backward calculation beside every rounded count. It makes the size consequence visible before the yarn is committed.
>
> A body measurement is not a garment measurement with the label changed. Ease, seam behavior, and construction connect them.
>
> If two pieces must join, check their joining lengths in physical units as well as stitch totals. Equal counts in different fabrics can mean unequal lengths.

## Sizing formulas in one place

All of these are arithmetic only. Gauge comes from your own blocked swatch.

| To find | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Stitches for a width | width × stitches per inch | 20 in × 3.5 = 70 sts |
| Rows for a length | length × rows per inch | 12 in × 4 = 48 rows |
| Finished width from a count | stitches ÷ stitches per inch | 70 ÷ 3.5 = 20 in |
| Finished size with ease | body measurement + ease | 36 in + 4 in = 40 in |
| Percentage ease | body × (1 + ease %) | 36 × 1.10 = 39.6 in |
| Diameter of a circle | circumference ÷ π | 21 ÷ 3.1416 ≈ 6.7 in |
| Circumference of a circle | diameter × π | 6 × 3.1416 ≈ 18.8 in |

Then round the stitch count to fit the stitch multiple, and recheck the finished measurement with the rounded count.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Round to the multiple, then recheck
> If the math says 71 stitches but the pattern is a multiple of 4, choose 68 or 72, then work out what width each choice gives. Pick the closer one on purpose.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Convert units once
> Pick inches or centimetres and stick to them for the whole calculation. Mixing units mid-way is where impossible numbers come from.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Measure the person, measure a garment they love, and trust the garment.
