A sweater is a system of connected surfaces and openings. Its construction controls where you can add width, length, or shaping, and when you can evaluate fit. Learn one construction clearly before combining several. The same yarn and chest circumference can produce very different garments when the shoulder and sleeve arrangements change.

## Compare the main construction families

A drop-shoulder sweater uses body panels that extend past the natural shoulder, often with relatively straight sleeve attachments. A modified drop shoulder removes some width at the underarm. A set-in sleeve uses a shaped sleeve cap and armhole. A raglan connects body and sleeves along diagonal shaping lines. A round yoke distributes shaping around the upper body without raglan corners. A dolman incorporates much of the sleeve and body into one broad shape.

![sweater_schematic (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/sweater_schematic.png)

Those descriptions identify geometry, not a difficulty ranking. Any construction can become complicated through fit, stitch pattern, or finishing. A simple rectangle can still make a poor garment if its arm opening is misplaced.

| Construction | Principal planning relationship | Useful checkpoint |
|---|---|---|
| Drop shoulder | Body width determines shoulder extension and sleeve start | Temporarily join shoulders before sleeves |
| Modified drop | Underarm removal changes sleeve attachment | Compare actual armhole and sleeve top |
| Set-in sleeve | Cap curve relates to armhole seam path | Baste and fit before final seaming |
| Raglan | Four shaping regions connect body and sleeves | Check yoke depth and section counts before division |
| Round yoke | Distributed increases control upper- body circumference | Check neck, shoulder coverage, and division depth |
| Dolman | Body and sleeve geometry are interdependent | Check reach and underarm fabric volume |

## Top-down and bottom-up are directions

A construction family can often be worked in more than one direction. Top-down work makes some length adjustments convenient, but a narrow or poorly balanced yoke is not automatically corrected by adding body rows. Bottom-up panels allow separate measuring and replacement, but their seams must be planned.

![raglan_yoke (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/raglan_yoke.png)

For a top-down raglan, label front, back, sleeves, and corner regions. If each of four corners adds two stitches, a shaping round adds eight total. Determine where those new stitches belong in each section. At division, body circumference includes the front, back, and new underarm bridges; sleeve circumference includes held sleeve stitches and its corresponding underarm bridge. Do not use the total yoke count as the body count.

For a round yoke, increases must create enough shoulder coverage at the required depth. More even rounds increase depth without increasing circumference. More increase rounds change both. Separate those goals when adjusting fit.

## An original drop-shoulder pullover prototype

The Everyday Panel Pullover teaches a complete seamed construction in one count set. Status: original, unsampled instructional prototype. Counts and nominal dimensions are calculated; actual fit, neckline entry, seam take-up, drape, yarn use, and washing remain to be tested. It is not a graded commercial pattern or a promise of fit for a named size.

Design basis: hypothetical washed single crochet gauge of 16 stitches and 20 rows per 4 inches, equivalent to 4 stitches and 5 rows per inch. Each body panel is 88 stitches wide and 120 rows high, nominally 22 by 24 inches before seam effects. Two panels give a nominal 44-inch body circumference. Each sleeve grows from 40 to 64 stitches across 72 rows, nominally 10 to 16 inches around and 14.4 inches long before seam and cuff effects. The dropped shoulder supplies part of the overall neck-to-cuff length.

Materials: smooth garment-suitable yarn, hook that produces the stated sample fabric, locking markers, blunt yarn needle, and scissors. Yarn quantity must be established from representative body and sleeve samples; no measured yardage is claimed. Make a seam sample before fixing finished dimensions. If the specified gauge creates an unsuitable fabric, redesign rather than forcing it.

US terms. Ch-1 at row starts does not count. Work ordinary sc under both top loops. All pieces are worked flat. Decide and mark the intended RS after the first few rows.

## Back panel

Ch 89. Row 1: sc in second chain from hook and each remaining chain; 88 sc. Rows 2–120: ch 1, turn, sc in each stitch; 88 sc. Fasten off and secure the beginning tail. Mark the first and last 28 stitch positions along the upper edge as shoulder regions. The center 32 stitches remain the back neckline edge.

The arithmetic is 28 + 32 + 28 = 88. The unshaped back neck is a deliberate simplification of this prototype. It must be assessed for neckline comfort and garment balance during fitting.

## Front panel and shoulder sections

Ch 89. Work Rows 1–110 exactly as for the back, keeping 88 sc per row. For Row 111, ch 1, turn, sc in first 28 stitches only; leave the other 60 unworked. Rows 112–120: ch 1, turn, sc in each of these 28 stitches. Fasten off.

Return to the top of Row 110. Mark the center 32 stitches and the remaining far-side 28 stitches. Join yarn at the outer edge of the far-side shoulder section in the orientation that lets Row 111 proceed toward the neck. Make 28 sc across that shoulder region. The joining method must not add a counted stitch. Work its Rows 112–120 as 28-sc turned rows. Fasten off.

Both shoulders have ten rows above the common 110-row base. The front opening is nominally 8 inches wide and 2 inches deep before finishing. Its perimeter, fabric stretch, and shoulder-seam arrangement may not allow comfortable head entry for a particular wearer. Test the actual opening before permanently closing the shoulders. If entry is inadequate, change the planned neckline or include a designed closure; do not pull the garment over the head by force.

## Sleeves make two

Ch 41. Row 1: sc in second chain and across; 40 sc. Work through Row 72 with a non-counting ch-1 and a turn at every row start.

Increase rows are 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48, 54, 60, 66, and 72. On each increase row, work 2 sc in the first stitch, 1 sc in every internal stitch, and 2 sc in the final stitch. Work one sc per stitch on all other rows. Twelve increase rows add 24 stitches, ending with 64 sc. Counts after successive increase rows are 42, 44, 46, 48, 50, 52, 54, 56, 58, 60, 62, and 64.

Mark the center between stitches 32 and 33 of the final row. Mark both sleeve RS faces consistently. Compare the two finished sleeve dimensions after the same treatment. A matching count does not guarantee matching gauge.

## Assemble in a controlled order

Finish or block the pieces using the intended care procedure, then compare them with the plan. Temporarily connect the matching 28-stitch shoulder regions. Leave the front and back center neckline regions open. Check the neckline and overall shoulder extension on the intended wearer before permanent seaming.

On each body side, mark a point 40 row ends down from the shoulder on the front and the back. These mark the lower ends of the sleeve attachment. Lay the body open at its shoulder seam and align the sleeve- top midpoint with that seam. Attach each half of the 64-stitch sleeve top across its corresponding 40-row- end armhole edge. Each half maps 32 sleeve stitches to 40 row ends, or four stitch positions per five row ends. Use the seam method and distribution validated in your sample.

Fold the garment into wearing position. Seam each sleeve’s row-end edges from cuff to underarm, then the body side edges from underarm to hem. The body side seam closes the lower 80 row ends on each side. Check that the underarm junction is secure without a bulky knot or gap. Weave all remaining ends.

This prototype leaves the neckline, hem, and cuffs without added bands. If adding bands, sample their contribution and entry effects and update the dimensions and instructions. A narrower finishing edge can invalidate the neckline test made before it was attached.

## Cardigans and sleeveless tops

A cardigan replaces the front panel with two fronts and a planned opening. Account for bands and overlap in the closed circumference. A deep V front has different row schedules from a square-neck pullover. A sleeveless top needs armhole finishing and shoulder stability rather than simply omitting sleeves from an oversized body.

Use the prototype’s construction to understand the relationships, not as a universal conversion formula. Redraw the front and opening, recalculate section counts, and fit the revised version.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Try the shoulders and neck before attaching sleeves. Those are inconvenient places to discover a passage problem after the rest is permanently sewn.
>
> Write the sleeve increase row numbers on one line and check them off for both sleeves. Two individually plausible sleeves can still disagree with each other.
>
> A garment can match chest circumference and still fail at the upper arm. Keep those measurements independent.

## Construction families compared

| Construction | How it is made | Fit | Difficulty | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Drop shoulder | Rectangles seamed together | Relaxed | Beginner | Little shaping; sleeves join at a straight edge |
| Raglan | Diagonal increases (top-down) or decreases (bottom-up) from neck to underarm | Close at shoulders | Intermediate | Often worked in one piece |
| Set-in sleeve | Shaped armholes and sleeve caps | Tailored | Advanced | Most shaping |
| Yoke (circular) | Increases spread around a round yoke | Varies | Intermediate | Good for colorwork bands |
| Granny-square or motif | Motifs joined to a shape | Relaxed | Beginner–intermediate | Plan motif sizes carefully |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Top-down lets you try it on
> Top-down sweaters can be tried on as you go. You can stop the body or sleeves at exactly the length you want.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Crochet sweaters are heavy
> A dense crochet sweater can stretch in length under its own weight. Choose lighter stitches or lighter yarn, and measure length with the piece hanging, after blocking.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Your first sweater should be one you would wear on a Saturday, not to a wedding.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Drop shoulders shorten the sleeve
> In a drop-shoulder sweater, the body panel extends past the wearer's shoulder. That extra width hangs down the upper arm, so the sleeve needs to be shorter than the arm measurement by about that overhang. Measure on the wearer, or on a drop-shoulder sweater they like.
