An opening needs enough room for entry, enough structure to remain usable, and a shape that works with the body or object. A neckline that fits around the neck may still fail to pass over the head. A sleeve can have the right wrist measurement and still restrict the upper arm. Plan openings as connected dimensions rather than isolated stitch counts.

## Define the opening and its edge treatment

Record the intended finished width, depth, circumference where relevant, and finishing treatment. A neckline’s unfinished edge is not necessarily its final size after a band is added. A firm border can reduce stretch. A buttoned or zippered opening can allow entry without requiring the entire edge to stretch over the head.

![neckline (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/neckline.png)

Measure an appropriate garment and the intended wearer, then make a representative sample of the edge. Do not infer head-entry stretch from a plain swatch when the finished neckline will have a different border or seam.

## Create a square opening in a panel

A simple square neckline can be made by working one shoulder section, leaving a central group of stitches unworked, and completing the other shoulder separately. In later assembly, those unworked center stitches form the horizontal lower edge of the opening. The side edges come from the rows worked independently above them.

For a count-only example, a 40-stitch panel can divide into 12 shoulder stitches, 16 unworked center stitches, and 12 opposite shoulder stitches. The totals account for all 40. If both shoulders continue for eight rows, their additional row height creates the opening depth. The physical dimensions come from stitch and row gauge, and the neckline band changes the final result.

State how the second shoulder yarn is attached, which face is toward the worker, and whether its shaping is mirrored. Do not tell the reader simply to “make the other side the same” when the working direction or decrease placement differs.

## Shape a V neckline

A V commonly narrows each front section at its neckline edge as the work approaches the shoulder. The rate of decreases controls the slope. A decrease every row makes a different angle from a decrease every second or third row. The row gauge determines how far apart those events are physically.

Keep armhole and neckline shaping separate in the schedule if they occur simultaneously. A row table can list neckline action, armhole action, and resulting count. Without that separation, it is easy to apply one decrease twice or overlook another.

At the V point, a border needs an appropriate decrease or corner treatment to turn cleanly. The exact method depends on the stitch pattern, angle, and band width. Sample the point with its border before assuming a familiar center decrease will lie flat.

## Shape a curved neckline

A curve usually combines a central opening with staged reductions near its sides. Larger early steps remove width near the base, while later smaller changes smooth the approach to the shoulder. Crochet rows create discrete steps; an edging can soften their appearance but does not erase incorrect geometry.

Record each side’s input count, removed center stitches, step reductions, and final shoulder count. Check that the two shoulders match their intended widths. A deep front and shallow back can share a shoulder seam while having different neckline depths.

## Armhole openings

An armhole’s depth, width, and curve interact with sleeve construction. A dropped-shoulder garment may use a relatively straight opening with a simple sleeve top. A set-in sleeve needs a shaped cap related to the armhole seam line. A raglan creates diagonal joining or increase lines. These are distinct constructions, not interchangeable sleeve labels.

Measure the actual joining path along the seam line rather than only its straight vertical depth. A curved edge is longer than its vertical projection. If a fitted sleeve cap has intentional easing, its amount and placement must be designed and fitted in the chosen crochet fabric.

## Sleeve width and taper

Begin with wrist or cuff requirements, upper-arm requirements, intended ease, and sleeve length. Use the fabric gauge to calculate counts, then distribute shaping events over the intended length. If each event adds two stitches, the number of events equals half the required net increase, provided the difference is even.

For a cuff-to-upper-arm change from 36 to 56 stitches, ten two-stitch increase events are needed. The row schedule depends on length and gauge. If 50 rows are available, shaping every fifth row is one possible schedule, but cuffs, straight upper sections, and cap preparation may require a different distribution.

Check movement as well as standing fit. Bend the elbow, reach forward, and assess whether the sleeve pulls the body or neckline. The garment’s balance may require changes beyond a larger upper-arm count.

## Bridge an opening with chains

A horizontal opening can be created by replacing a group of stitches with a chain bridge. On the next row, work into the chain links or space as explicitly directed. If six stitches are skipped and six chains replace them, the nominal count can remain the same, but the bridge’s width and elasticity may differ from six ordinary stitches.

For a sample slit, begin with 20 sc. Next row: ch 1, turn; sc in first 7 sts, ch 6, skip 6 sts, sc in last 7 sts. There are 14 sc and six chain positions. Following row: ch 1, turn; sc in first 7 sc, sc in each of six chains, sc in final 7 sc; 20 sc. The beginning chain does not count.

## Pockets and access openings

A patch pocket is made separately and sewn onto a surface. An inset pocket needs an opening and a backing or bag. A chain slit by itself is not a complete pocket. Specify where the pocket body attaches and how its weight is supported.

Make a seam and opening sample with the intended contents. Heavy keys, phones, or tools can stretch a soft pocket. A decorative pocket may not be suitable for carrying weight. Avoid promising load capacity without testing.

## Troubleshoot an opening

| Problem | Check before changing counts |
|---|---|
| Neckline fits neck but not head entry | Edge stretch and closure construction |
| Neckline flares | Edging density, shaping, and band gauge |
| V point puckers | Center treatment and number of border stitches |
| Sleeve pulls at upper arm | Circumference, armhole shape, cap, and movement |
| Slit sags | Bridge length, tension, and reinforcement |
| Pocket distorts garment | Load, attachment area, and fabric stability |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Test the opening after its border is added. The unfinished edge may stretch generously while the finished band refuses to cooperate.
>
> If two kinds of shaping happen at once, put them in separate columns. Your future self should not have to decode a paragraph while counting sleeve decreases.
>
> A slit is an opening, not a reinforced opening. The intended use determines whether it needs more structure.

## Opening edges: what each treatment does

| Treatment | Effect | Use on |
|---|---|---|
| Round of sc | Firms and neatens | All openings |
| Round of sl st | Firm, low-profile | Necklines that stretch too much |
| BLO sc rib (worked sideways and seamed) | Stretchy band | Crew necks, cuffs |
| FPdc/BPdc rib | Stretchy, raised | Cuffs, waistbands |
| Reverse sc | Corded edge | Decorative finish |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Pick up fewer stitches on curves
> On a curved neckline, picking up one stitch per row end can make the edge ripple. Pick up slightly fewer stitches and check that the band lies flat before you finish.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Try the opening on before the band
> Before working a neckband or armhole edge, try the garment on, or test the opening on the measurement it must pass. A band can tighten an opening, but rarely loosens it.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> A neckline that fits over the head is a neckline that will be worn.
