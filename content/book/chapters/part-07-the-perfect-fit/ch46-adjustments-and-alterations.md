Adjust the dimension that is wrong while preserving the relationships that are already useful. Adding width everywhere can solve a tight chest and create oversized shoulders. Adding length everywhere can cover the front while making the back droop. First identify where the fabric is needed, then decide how the construction can supply it.

## Diagnose the garment before changing it

Put the finished or temporarily assembled garment into its intended wearing configuration. Check whether it was treated according to its care plan. Compare its actual measurements with the specification. Verify counts and gauge before redesigning a shape that was simply crocheted incorrectly.

Record the symptom in concrete terms: front hem rises 2 inches relative to the planned level; upper-arm circumference is 1 inch below target; neckline migrates backward while reaching. A description such as “the whole thing fits weird” is understandable but difficult to calculate from.

## Length changes in a straight section

For a bottom-up straight body, add or remove rows below the armhole while preserving pocket and motif placement. For a top-down garment, change body rows below division if the opening and upper body fit. For a sideways garment, body length may be established by stitch count instead of row count; identify the working direction first.

At six rows per inch, adding 1.5 inches requires nine rows. A four-row repeat cannot simply gain nine rows and finish at the same phase. Add eight or twelve and accept the resulting length, adjust a plain section, or write a tested partial-repeat ending. Keep the decision visible.

## Width changes and front-back distribution

A 4-inch circumference increase at 4 stitches per inch requires 16 additional nominal stitches around. In a symmetric two-panel garment, eight per panel gives an even distribution. If the extra width is needed primarily in front, splitting it evenly may not solve the problem.

Compare front and back arc measurements or a garment that fits well. A front panel can need additional width or length without a wider cross-back. A side panel, localized shaping, or a different front count can allocate the fabric deliberately. Update shoulders, neckline, and armhole relationships after any count change.

## Shoulder and upper-arm changes

In a drop-shoulder garment, increasing body width moves the sleeve attachment outward. If the desired neck-to-cuff length stays the same, the sleeve may need to shorten. For example, increasing each panel’s overall width by 2 inches can shift each outer shoulder edge about 1 inch farther from a centered neckline. Check the actual assembled geometry rather than applying a universal sleeve subtraction.

A sleeve’s upper-arm count and taper can be adjusted independently of cuff size. If the cuff remains 40 stitches and the upper sleeve changes from 64 to 72, the needed increase becomes 32 instead of 24. With two added stitches per event, 16 increase events are required instead of 12. Redistribute those events over the measured sleeve length; do not cluster the extra four events arbitrarily near the top.

For a set-in sleeve, changing upper-arm width also affects its cap and joining curve. Rework those relationships or choose another construction. A few extra stitches under the arm are not automatically a complete fitted-sleeve alteration.

## Short rows add local depth

A short row turns before reaching the usual row end. It adds fabric over only part of the width. Several partial rows can raise the back neck, add front length, form a slope, or shape a heel. They change local depth without adding the same number of rows everywhere.

The turning point needs a defined treatment. Different stitches and methods leave different steps or gaps. Do not import a knitting wrap-and-turn procedure into crochet without explaining and sampling its construction. Some crochet short-row designs accept a small step; others use a specific joining stitch to reduce it.

## An explicit short-row wedge experiment

It is not a polished garment short-row method. Ch 13. Row 1: sc in second chain and across; 12 sc. Row 2: ch 1, turn, sc in first 8 stitches; leave the final 4 unworked. Row 3: ch 1, turn, sc in those 8 stitches back to the original side edge; 8 sc.

Row 4: ch 1, turn, sc in the 8 stitches just worked, then continue into the 4 remaining unworked stitches from Row 1; 12 sc. At the transition, do not count the edge chain as an extra destination. Row 5: ch 1, turn, sc in all 12 stitches. Fasten off and secure ends.

The eight-stitch region received two additional partial rows compared with the four-stitch region. A visible step or gap may appear at the transition. Inspect it, compare both faces, and measure the slope after finishing. Any technique intended to conceal that transition must receive its own verified sample and exact instructions before garment use.

For a front-length adjustment of 2 inches at six rows per inch, the center front needs 12 additional local rows. Six pairs of partial rows can supply that number in the fully covered region, but their widths and turn positions determine the curve. This arithmetic does not prescribe a bust-shaping method or prove fit.

## Alter an already finished piece cautiously

First determine the working direction and where the yarn ends were secured. Many ordinary crochet fabrics can be undone from the most recently worked edge if the finishing end is accessible. The foundation edge generally does not unravel backward in the same straightforward way. Fuzzy, felted, split, or heavily seamed yarn can resist recovery.

If the hem is the final worked edge, removing it and adding rows may be possible. If the hem is the foundation, extending it can require adding fabric from that edge, a separate band, or more complex reconstruction. The new direction and stitch anatomy may remain visible. Test the method on a sacrificial sample first.

Do not cut a garment merely because an internet summary says a row can be removed. Crochet loops can lose their support in ways unlike live knitting stitches. Chapter 35 develops controlled unravelling; cutting and sectional replacement require their own structural plan and sample.

## Rework, insert, or accept

Reworking from an accessible edge can preserve a continuous fabric. Inserting a side panel changes the design but may be easier than reconstructing the upper body. Adding a hem band changes length and appearance. Accepting a different fit may be reasonable if it is comfortable and intentional.

Choose based on material condition and the result you want. Do not describe an obvious contrasting insert as an invisible repair. A visible design change can be attractive and honest without pretending the original fabric was never altered.

## Adjustment planning example

| Question | Example answer |
|---|---|
| What is wrong? | Upper sleeve is 1 inch below intended circumference |
| What already fits? | Cuff, sleeve length, and body width |
| What gauge governs it? | 4 stitches per inch after washing |
| What net count change is needed? | 4 additional stitches at upper sleeve |
| How will it be distributed? | Two additional two-stitch increase events |
| What connected region changes? | Sleeve top and armhole attachment |
| What confirms success? | Measured fit, movement, and compatible seam |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Mark the last correct row before starting an alteration. A known return point makes experimentation less dramatic.
>
> When you add width to a drop shoulder, check total sleeve reach. The sleeve starts farther out than it did before.
>
> Photograph the fit problem before unravelling. The original symptom is easier to solve when it remains visible in the record.

## Alteration decision guide

| Problem | Option 1: Easiest | Option 2: Better | Option 3: Most work |
|---|---|---|---|
| Too long | Fold a hem | Frog rows from the hem (only if the piece was worked top-down) | Rework the body |
| Too short | Add a border | Add rows at the edge | Insert a section |
| Too wide | Block slightly narrower | Take in the side seams | Rework with fewer stitches |
| Too narrow | Block slightly wider | Add side panels | Rework with more stitches |
| Neckline too wide | Add a tighter band | Rework the band | Rework the shoulders |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Crochet unravels only from the last stitch
> Crochet generally unravels from the end where you finished. To shorten a bottom-up garment from the hem, you would have to undo everything above. Plan length changes before you start, or choose top-down.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Block before you alter
> Many "fit problems" disappear after blocking. Wash and block the piece first, then decide.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Sometimes the fix is a belt. Sometimes it is a new sweater. Knowing which is a skill.
