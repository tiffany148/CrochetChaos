A dog sweater needs an appropriate neckline, room for the chest, correctly placed foreleg openings, and a belly hem that stays clear in use. The following prototype gives a complete count sequence so those relationships can be studied and altered deliberately. It is not a universal size derived from weight or breed.

## Lakeside Basic Dog Pullover

Status: original instructional prototype; arithmetic checked, no physical crochet sample or dog fitting completed. Do not dress a dog in it unless its measured dimensions, finished openings, comfort, movement, and care behavior have been assessed successfully. Choose a different opening construction if head or leg passage is unsuitable.

![dog_sweater (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/dog_sweater.png)

Design basis: hypothetical washed gauge of 16 sc and 20 rounds or rows per 4 inches. Nominal finished targets are 12 inches at the neckline, 18 inches around the main body, 8 inches of total belly coverage, and 12 inches of total back coverage. These exclude any effect of additional edging and are not observed dimensions.

Materials: smooth, appropriately washable yarn that makes a comfortable garment fabric at the stated gauge; suitable hook; locking markers; blunt needle; scissors. Determine quantity from representative washed samples of the yoke, tube, and back flap. No measured yardage is claimed. Exclude strings, beads, pompoms, and detachable decorative parts.

US terminology. Ch-1 used for flat turns does not count. Work spiral rounds after the foundation round; no joining slip stitches are added at subsequent boundaries. Ordinary increases are 2 sc in one stitch. The round start will become one edge of the back region; mark the true center back separately at division.

## Neckline and yoke

Foundation: ch 48. Confirm that the chain is untwisted and join to the first chain with a slip stitch. Round 1: ch 1, sc in each chain around; 48 sc. Mark the first sc. Do not join. Rounds 2–4: sc in each stitch; 48 sc. The finished neckline sample must permit comfortable dressing before proceeding with a real fitting.

Round 5: [sc in next 7 sts, 2 sc in next st] six times; 54 sc. Rounds 6–7: sc in each stitch; 54 sc.

Round 8: [sc in next 8 sts, 2 sc in next st] six times; 60 sc. Rounds 9–10: sc in each stitch; 60 sc.

Round 11: [sc in next 9 sts, 2 sc in next st] six times; 66 sc. Rounds 12–13: sc in each stitch; 66 sc.

Round 14: [sc in next 10 sts, 2 sc in next st] six times; 72 sc. Rounds 15–16: sc in each stitch; 72 sc.

Move the round marker at every round. Before the opening round, mark the four regions in order: 40 back stitches, 8 first-leg stitches, 16 chest stitches, and 8 second-leg stitches. The back consists of the first 40 consecutive stitches after the round-start marker. Mark its true midpoint between stitches 20 and 21 with a different-colored marker. The four region counts total 72.

## Foreleg openings and body tube

Round 17: sc in first 40 sts; ch 8, skip next 8 sts; sc in next 16 sts; ch 8, skip final 8 sts. There are 56 sc and 16 chains, making 72 working positions for the next round. The two openings are bounded by their skipped stitches and chain bridges.

Round 18: sc in first 40 sc, sc in each of next 8 chains, sc in next 16 sc, sc in each of final 8 chains; 72 sc. Do not work into the empty opening in place of the individual chain links for this prototype. Rounds 19– 40: sc in each stitch; 72 sc.

The slit openings must be inspected and measured on a model before any dog fitting. Their eight-chain bridges are not an assurance of leg passage or clearance. A longer bridge changes the count unless another change compensates for it. Chapter 48 explains that accounting.

At the stated hypothetical vertical gauge, Round 16 reaches about 3.2 inches from the top and Round 40 about 8 inches, but shaped fabric and actual care can change those distances. If the foreleg landmark is wrong, change the yoke depth at a suitable stage rather than placing the openings at an arbitrary distance from the dog’s legs.

## Longer back flap

After Round 40, the live loop is again at the beginning of the marked 40-stitch back region. Row 41: sc in the first 40 stitches only; leave the other 32 body stitches unworked. Row 42: ch 1, turn, sc in each of these 40 stitches. Rows 43–60: repeat Row 42. There are 20 flat back-flap rows in total, counting Row 41.

Fasten off and secure all ends. The back flap adds a nominal 4 inches at five rows per inch. Its lower edge remains straight in this prototype. It must not interfere with tail movement or drape inappropriately when the dog sits. A shaped rear edge would need its own counted decrease schedule.

## Edge treatment and final assessment

The pattern does not include a constricting rib or added neckline band. Inspect the unembellished edges first. If an edging is needed, sample it on the actual garment fabric and recheck every opening afterward. Adding a tightly worked slip-stitch edge can reduce passage even while the main stitch counts stay unchanged.

Finish according to the yarn’s instructions. Record actual neckline, body circumference, opening positions, back and belly lengths, and any growth. Follow the fitting sequence in Chapter 47. If the dog’s measured requirements differ, revise the design before attempting use.

## Modify one relationship at a time

To lengthen the chest before the leg openings while keeping the same circumference schedule, add even rounds in an appropriate yoke region. To change body girth, calculate a new count and redistribute back, chest, and leg regions. To shorten belly coverage while retaining the back, begin flat back rows earlier and adjust their number to preserve the intended back length.

For example, ending the tube at Round 35 instead of Round 40 removes five nominal rounds, about 1 inch at the illustrative gauge. Adding five flat back rows can preserve the original nominal back length while leaving a shorter belly. Check the movement and transition; the calculation does not establish that the new hem shape is suitable.

## Hoodie-style detail without enclosed headwear

The default prototype has no worn hood. If you want to study hoodie construction, make a separate rectangle on a model: ch 25, then work 20 turned rows of 24 sc with a non-counting ch-1. Fold it vertically between stitch columns 12 and 13 so its two 20-row side edges meet; leave those row-end edges open as the face opening. Join the two halves of the final 24-stitch row with a 12-pair seam, beginning by matching stitch 12 to stitch 13 at the fold and continuing outward through pairs 11/14 to 1/24. That seam closes the top. The fold forms the back, and the foundation edge remains the lower neck edge, arranged as two 12-stitch segments.

Status: count-based shape study only, not a measured canine hood. Do not place it around a dog’s head. A hoodie-style back detail may instead be secured flat and completely out of the head and ear region after separately assessing bulk, snagging, and attachment. It must not create an accessible pocket or loop for claws. If that cannot be achieved, omit it. A foldable decoration is optional; the body garment’s function does not depend on it.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Mark the true center back separately from the round start. A counting boundary is not automatically an anatomical landmark.
>
> Make the belly shorter by changing where the tube ends, not by guessing that the dog will hold the hem out of the way.
>
> If head passage is unsuitable, change construction. A pullover is one option, not an obligation imposed by the yarn.

## Fit adjustments for dog sweaters

| Fit issue | Change |
|---|---|
| Too tight around the chest | Add stitches in the body tube, keeping the stitch multiple |
| Gaps at the neck | Make the neck band with fewer stitches or a smaller hook |
| Leg openings rub | Move them further apart, make them larger, or add a soft edging |
| Too long on the belly | End the underbelly panel sooner, so the belly area stays clear |
| Rides up the back | Add length to the back panel or a slight short-row shaping |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Try it on before the edging
> Do a fitting before any edging round. Edgings firm up openings, so test the openings first.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Machine-washable yarn for dogs
> Dog sweaters get muddy. Choose a yarn you can wash easily and that the dog does not react to.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> A dog does not care about the stitch pattern. It cares that the sweater does not tickle.


> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — A marker in every round
> In spiral rounds, move the marker up every round. Without it, a spiral is almost impossible to count.
