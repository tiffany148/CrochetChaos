A harness-friendly garment preserves access to the actual harness and does not transfer leash force to crochet fabric. Decide whether the harness will be worn over or under the clothing using the equipment’s instructions and a combined fitting. There is no universal layer order that suits every harness and garment.

Ruffwear’s own layering guidance distinguishes fitted layers worn under a harness from looser garments with leash portals worn over one. That is useful evidence that both arrangements exist; it is not an endorsement of a homemade garment or permission to ignore another manufacturer’s instructions.

## Map the equipment while it is correctly fitted

Record the location of the leash connection, buckles, adjustment points, and major straps. Measure their positions relative to the same neck-base and center-back landmarks used for the garment. Note whether the connection is a single ring or a pair of rings and how the clip must approach it.

![harness_gap (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/harness_gap.png)

Check the movement of the ring and clip during ordinary handling. The garment needs an opening that permits access without catching or being pulled into the connection. A small hole that exposes only the top of a ring may be insufficient for the actual clip.

Do not move or modify a harness attachment point to match a convenient location in the crochet. Move the garment’s opening or change the design. The equipment’s intended function governs its own structure.

## Portal orientation

A horizontal slit follows a row and can be made with a chain bridge. A vertical slit runs across several rows and may require working two sections separately before rejoining them. A planned opening is easier to specify and reinforce than an improvised cut in completed crochet.

Mark the portal’s center, width, height, and orientation on the garment map. State dimensions both before and after any edging. A border can shrink the useful opening. Compare the finished opening with the actual hardware, not merely the number of skipped stitches.

Row 11: ch 1, turn; sc in first 12 sts, ch 8, skip 8 sts, sc in final 12 sts. There are 24 sc and eight chain positions. Row 12: ch 1, turn; sc in first 12 sc, sc in each of eight chains, sc in last 12 sc; 32 sc. Rows 13– 20: ch 1, turn; sc in each stitch; 32 sc. Fasten off and secure ends.

The nominal width is eight stitch positions, or 2 inches at four stitches per inch. The useful opening height and passage behavior cannot be deduced from that width. Sample any reinforcement and test access with the actual disconnected harness and leash hardware on a work surface first. The garment must never be clipped in place of the harness.

## An exact vertical portal sample

Ch 33 and work a 32-sc panel through Row 10 as above. Divide the next working row into two groups of 16. Row 11A: ch 1, turn, sc in first 16 stitches only. Rows 12A–16A: ch 1, turn, sc in those 16 stitches. Fasten off.

Return to the other 16 stitches of Row 10. Join yarn at the outer edge in the orientation that lets Row 11B proceed toward the center. Work 16 sc. Rows 12B–16B: ch 1, turn, sc in those 16 stitches. Both sections now have six additional rows. Fasten off if necessary to establish the next full row in a consistent working direction.

Row 17: join at an outer edge and work 16 sc across one section and 16 across the other, without inserting extra chains between them; 32 sc. Rows 18–20: ch 1, turn, sc in each stitch; 32 sc. Secure all ends. The split between the two sections remains a six-row vertical slit.

At five rows per inch the slit has a nominal 1.2-inch vertical extent. Its ends and side edges still need evaluation. This example teaches how to create the opening without cutting; it does not establish that the resulting portal suits a particular ring or clip.

## Reinforce without creating a hard pressure point

Inspect the opening ends because force can concentrate there when the fabric is moved. A sample may need a carefully distributed edge or a compatible sewn reinforcement. Do not simply pile a large knot at each end. Added material must remain comfortable and must not change the harness’s position or catch its hardware.

Crochet around a portal only with the garment off the animal and the equipment removed. Identify exact edge destinations and avoid narrowing the opening unpredictably. Wash the sample and test again because edging and base fabric may change differently.

## An opening garment rather than a pullover

If head passage or dressing is difficult, split the garment along a planned line and add a tested closure. A center-front opening changes the chest panel and may alter leg placement. A back opening may conflict with the harness connection. A side closure can affect how the body remains centered. Choose the location after mapping both dog and equipment.

A counted conversion should specify which stitches are divided, the new edge stitches, band widths, overlap, and resulting closed circumference. For a hypothetical 72-position body, adding two 4-stitch bands does not automatically produce an 80-position wearing circumference because the bands may overlap. Use measured closed dimensions.

## Equipment access test record

| Test | Evidence to record |
|---|---|
| Hardware access on a work surface | Clip can reach intended ring without catching garment |
| Opening dimensions | Useful width and height after treatment |
| Combined fitting | Harness retains its required fit and access |
| Movement | Garment does not pull into ring or strap path |
| Removal | Garment can be removed without confusing its fastening with equipment |
| Reinspection | Opening and reinforcement remain sound after care |

These observations do not certify equipment performance. If the garment interferes with proper harness function, redesign or omit it rather than accepting an inconvenient compromise.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Bring the actual leash clip to the sample table. A ring-shaped hole is not necessarily a clip-shaped access route.
>
> Locate the portal from the correctly fitted equipment. “Somewhere behind the neck” is a description of a region, not a pattern instruction.
>
> Add a visible distinction between clothing closures and restraint hardware. A useful garment should not invite a mistaken attachment.

## Harness opening reminders

- Fit the harness to the dog first, then measure the position of the D-ring or leash attachment with the sweater on.
- Openings must not pinch or cut into the dog when the leash pulls.
- Reinforce openings with a round of sc or a buttonhole-style edge; avoid hard, stiff edges that rub.
- Check the sweater does not slip and cover the attachment point during walks.

> [!SAFETY] The harness must work as designed
> The crochet garment must never interfere with how the harness fits or works. Check the harness adjustment and the leash attachment each time, and follow the harness maker's guidance.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Mark the opening with a safety pin first
> Put the sweater on over the harness and mark where the D-ring sits with a locking marker. Measure from there, not from a guess.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> A good harness opening is the one you never think about on a walk.


> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Hide tails inside
> Pull long tails through the stuffed body and snip them close; they disappear inside the toy.

> [!HINT] Stuff with small handfuls
> Small wisps of stuffing pack evenly; big clumps make lumps.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Embroidered eyes for young children
> For toys meant for babies and young children, embroider the eyes and nose instead of using safety eyes.

> [!HINT] Work toys in one color first
> Make your first version of a new toy in one color. Color changes are easier when you know the shape works.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> A toy that has been hugged flat is a toy that was loved.
