Amigurumi combines stitch counts with volume. Increases make room for a wider region; even rounds extend it; decreases narrow it. Stuffing supports the shell but should not be used to force a badly planned fabric into an unrelated shape. A correct count is necessary evidence, not proof of a particular three- dimensional form.

## Choose the shell fabric

Make a small sample of the intended single crochet. Choose yarn and hook so the fabric can be worked without force and does not expose filling under the expected shape and handling. A smaller hook can reduce openings, but extreme tightness can split yarn and strain hands.

Use the same yarn-over or yarn-under method consistently if the design specifies one. These motions can change stitch appearance and dimensions. A sample worked one way does not approve a pattern made another way. Record the actual method with gauge and yarn information.

## Distinguish a disk from a rounded form

A flat disk needs circumference to expand with radius. A rounded form transitions from expanding rounds through an approximately even region to decreasing rounds. The number of even rounds changes its height; the rate and location of shaping affect the curvature.

A six-increase single crochet schedule is a common starting model. Its physical flatness and curvature depend on stitch width-to-height ratio. A sphere-like object made from that schedule may be slightly oval in a particular fabric. Describe the observed sample honestly rather than assuming the name sphere establishes mathematical accuracy.

## Read a shape schedule

| Region | Count behavior | Common effect to sample |
|---|---|---|
| Increasing base | More stitches each round | Expands circumference |
| Even middle | Same count | Extends the body |
| Decreasing end | Fewer stitches | Narrows toward closure |
| Unevenly placed increases | Same additions in selected regions | Creates localized volume |
| Different increase intervals | Faster or slower expansion | Changes taper or curvature |

Keep track of the intended top, bottom, front, and back as the object develops. A round marker identifies an accounting boundary but can drift relative to a visible surface because the work spirals. Add separate positioning markers for later assembly when necessary.

## Orbit Pebble original rounded-form pattern

Status: original count-based prototype, not physically sampled. It is a decorative shape study with no promised size, measured yardage, toy-safety approval, or pet-toy suitability. Materials: smooth yarn, hook producing suitable dense fabric, locking marker, appropriate clean stuffing, blunt yarn needle, and scissors. Exclude weights, pellets, wire, and detachable features from this exercise.

US terms. Work a spiral without joins or beginning chains after Round 1. Ordinary inc means 2 sc in one stitch. Use ordinary sc2tog for every decrease in this pattern; a later sample may compare an explicitly defined invisible decrease without changing the count.

Round 1: make a single adjustable ring, ch 1 that does not count, work 6 sc into ring; mark first sc. Tighten gently and secure the beginning tail while accessible. A ch-2 start with 6 sc in the second chain may be used instead; inspect its center.

Round 2: inc in each stitch; 12 sc. Round 3: [sc in next st, inc in next st] six times; 18 sc. Round 4: [sc in next 2 sts, inc in next st] six times; 24 sc. Round 5: [sc in next 3 sts, inc in next st] six times; 30 sc. Rounds 6–10: sc in each stitch; 30 sc per round.

Round 11: [sc in next 3 sts, sc2tog] six times; 24 sc. Round 12: [sc in next 2 sts, sc2tog] six times; 18 sc. Add stuffing in small portions, arranging it without forcing the fabric open. Keep filling clear of the next stitch destinations.

Round 13: [sc in next st, sc2tog] six times; 12 sc. Add a final small amount of stuffing if needed while the opening remains accessible. Round 14: sc2tog six times; 6 sc. Fasten off, leaving a closure tail. Pass it through the front loop of each remaining stitch in order, draw the opening together gently, secure the end within sound fabric, and hide the remainder without leaving an exposed loop.

The schedule contains 300 worked single crochet output stitches in total, counting each sc2tog output as one stitch. That number does not measure yarn consumption because a decrease uses more yarn than one ordinary sc and finishing also consumes yarn.

## Fill gradually and inspect the surface

Use small amounts appropriate to the opening and object size. Distribute them into the lower region before adding more above. A large compressed lump can leave voids around it while making the center hard. A blunt tool may help place filling, but it must not puncture or damage the shell.

![stuffing (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/stuffing.png)

Pause to inspect gaps and seams. If filling becomes visible between ordinary stitches, determine whether the fabric is too open or too stretched by the amount of filling. Removing some filling can diagnose overpacking; a genuinely open shell may require a different fabric.

Keep the stitch path clear near the closing opening. Catching filling with the hook can pull fibers to the surface and make decreases difficult. Do not use scissors against the shell to trim stray fibers while the yarn is under tension.

## Density and stability

The required firmness depends on the design. A display sculpture may need different support from a soft squeezeable object. Record the actual stuffing material and, if useful, its mass for a sample. Equal mass in different stuffing types does not guarantee equal firmness.

A rounded object can roll even when it is correctly crocheted. A stable flat base may require a separately designed base geometry rather than simply adding more filling. Do not add loose heavy contents casually to prevent rolling. Their containment, laundering, intended user, and failure consequences require separate design and assessment.

## Ovals, capsules, and cones

Lengthening the even middle tends to produce an elongated rounded form. Changing the increase schedule can produce a wider or narrower end. A cone uses a gradual circumference increase over height; a cylinder maintains a relatively constant side circumference. A flat oval commonly begins around both sides of a foundation chain and increases at its ends.

Those are distinct constructions. Do not obtain every shape by stuffing the same sphere harder. Make an unstuffed sample and inspect where the yarn actually supplies room, then compare it with the filled version.

## Shape comparison exercise

Make the Orbit Pebble once as written and once with Rounds 6–10 replaced by ten even rounds instead of five. Renumber later rounds accordingly. Both versions have the same maximum count and the same decrease sequence; the second has five additional 30-stitch rounds, or 150 additional output stitches.

Compare the unstuffed shells, then use the same stuffing type and a documented filling approach. Record height, width, and surface tension. Do not claim a fixed size ratio until measured. The purpose is to connect an exact schedule change with an observed shape change.

> Stuff before the opening becomes too small to reach the corners. Finishing is easier when the filling does not need to negotiate a six-stitch doorway.
>
> If a shape looks lumpy, compare the empty shell and the filled shell. That separates a crochet problem from a distribution problem.

## Stuffing guide

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Lumpy surface | Large clumps of fiberfill | Pull stuffing apart into small wisps |
| Stitches gape over stuffing | Hook too large or stuffing too firm | Smaller hook; a little less stuffing |
| Piece goes flat over time | Too little stuffing | Stuff more firmly than feels necessary |
| Neck wobbles | Neck not stuffed firmly | Stuff the neck very firmly; consider a separate firm neck section |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Stuff as you go
> Stuff narrow parts before they close. Use a chopstick or the blunt end of a hook to push stuffing into small spaces.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Watch for inside-out pieces
> In spiral amigurumi, the side facing you as you work is usually the outside (right side). Small pieces often curl so the wrong side faces out; push them right side out before stuffing. A few designers prefer the texture of the other side, and their patterns say so.

> [!SAFETY] Weighted fillings
> Pellets or beads in toys are a choking hazard if a seam fails. Do not use them in toys for young children; if used at all, enclose them in a sealed inner pouch.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Stuff it until it's firm, then add one more pinch.


> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Carry loosely in tapestry
> Carried yarn should lie relaxed inside the stitches. Pulling it tight puckers the fabric.

> [!HINT] Turn the work, untwist the yarns
> In two-color work, turn the work one way at the end of a row and the opposite way at the end of the next. The strands untwist themselves.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Lace is mostly air. Count the air like stitches and the lace will come out right.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Starch is for display pieces
> Starched lace holds its shape beautifully but is often rinsed out before long storage, because starch may attract insects.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Wind a center-pull ball for each color
> For two-color work, pull one color from the center and one from the outside of separate balls to reduce tangling.
