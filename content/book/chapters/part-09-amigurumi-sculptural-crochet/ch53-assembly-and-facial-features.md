Assembly turns separate shapes into a recognizable object. Placement, orientation, seam tension, and stuffing interact. A head attached a little farther forward can change the expression and balance even when every component has the correct count. Plan the relationship before hiding the seam under matching yarn.

## Establish landmarks on the main form

Identify the intended front, back, top, and bottom. Use contrasting scrap markers that can be removed completely. Place paired features relative to a centerline and a shared round or measured level. Do not use the spiral boundary as the only facial center unless the design intentionally aligns it there.

![ami_assembly (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/ami_assembly.png)

Record both stitch locations and physical appearance. A pair of features separated by six stitches can look different after a shape is filled more firmly. Attach at a stage when the main form has enough of its final volume to assess placement, while preserving any access needed to secure the ends.

## Temporary placement

Arrange components on the table or a suitable support. If using pins during construction, keep them accounted for and remove every one before handling the object as finished. For a safer construction workspace with fewer sharp points, removable yarn basting can hold a component for evaluation.

Look from the front, side, and above. Check whether an attachment will be hidden behind another part or whether it must support that part’s weight. Photograph the arrangement before moving it. A photograph is a useful placement record, not proof that the eventual seam will be secure.

## Join an open rim to a closed surface

Keep the rim’s actual stitch tops identifiable. Use a compatible seaming length and a blunt needle. Attach the rim to sound surface strands along its intended footprint, advancing around the rim in order. Keep the yarn snug enough to bring the surfaces together without cinching a groove into the main form.

Match landmarks such as top, bottom, and side midpoints before distributing the remaining rim stitches. The closed surface may not offer one stitch for every rim stitch. Use the planned footprint and an appropriate distribution rather than stretching one part until the counts appear equal.

Leave access to adjust filling if the component requires it, then close the remaining seam. Secure the seaming end within fabric paths appropriate to the material. Hiding a tail inside stuffing alone is not the same as establishing a retained textile path. Inspect for exposed loops and gaps afterward.

## Join two closed pieces

Choose an attachment footprint on each piece. A few stitches at one tiny point can create a hinge or weak join where a broad attachment was intended. Baste the footprint, check position, and distribute the final sewing around its boundary and any necessary internal supports according to the design.

Do not pull the pieces together so strongly that both collapse. If they will not meet along the intended footprint without distortion, revisit their shapes or filling. More seam tension is not a substitute for compatible geometry.

## Embroider simple features

Use a yarn or embroidery material compatible with the shell and care plan. For a straight embroidered eye, anchor the thread or yarn inside accessible fabric, bring it out at one planned endpoint, insert at the other, and secure the path without leaving a loose surface loop. Use several shorter stitches when a long span would snag or sag.

For a curved smile, mark the curve and use a series of small stitches that follow it. Do not draw a tight straight thread across a curved stuffed surface and expect it to remain a gentle arc. A tensioned feature can indent the shape; choose that effect only if deliberately designed and sampled.

Felt or crocheted appliqué needs a securely attached perimeter appropriate to the intended use. Adhesive alone is not the default attachment in this book. Wire, magnets, buttons, and rigid inserts require additional design and risk assessment and are excluded from the introductory prototypes.

## The name safety eyes is not a certification

Plastic eyes and noses can create small parts if components detach. Their commercial name does not approve a handmade toy. CPSC guidance addresses small-part hazards and age-related product requirements; consult the current applicable guidance if an object will be supplied as a children’s product.

Embroidered features avoid adding the same rigid eye hardware, but they do not by themselves certify the whole object. Seams, filling, loops, materials, and intended use still matter. None of this book’s untested decorative prototypes is presented as an approved baby or pet toy.

## Original Pebble Sprout assembly study

Make one Orbit Pebble from Chapter 52. Use its complete 14-round pattern, count schedule, and finishing instructions. This study adds one flat leaf and optional short embroidered marks. Status: original decorative prototype, physically untested. It has no detachable hardware, hanging loop, or added weight.

Leaf: ch 7. Round 1, worked around both sides of the chain: sc in second chain from hook, hdc in next chain, dc in each of next 2 chains, hdc in next chain, 3 sc in final chain. Rotate the chain to work into its remaining underside loops. Hdc in the next available chain position, dc in each of next 2, hdc in next, sc in the final position at the starting end. There are 13 actual stitches. Sl st to the first sc; the join does not count. Fasten off, leaving a seaming end.

This leaf has five stitches along each side plus three at the far end. The two root stitches are the first and thirteenth actual stitches near the beginning tail. Identify them with markers. The shape may curl or need finishing; its exact outline must be established by a physical sample.

Choose a top on the finished pebble away from the final closure lump. Position the leaf root there. Baste its two root stitches and a small additional base area to a sound footprint on the shell. Check that the leaf lies as intended, then sew the attachment securely and finish both leaf ends. Do not leave a long free seaming loop beneath the leaf.

If adding an expression, mark two small eye positions and a short smile on the intended front. Embroider with short retained stitches. No fixed placement is claimed to produce a particular expression in every gauge. Record the actual sampled placement for the publication version.

## Assembly audit

| Check | What to inspect |
|---|---|
| Position | Intended symmetry or deliberate asymmetry from several views |
| Rim | Every planned stitch attached; no unexplained gap |
| Tension | No unintended indentation or collapsed component |
| Ends | Retained within fabric without exposed loops |
| Tools | All temporary pins, needles, and markers removed |
| Care | Materials and attachments tolerate the planned treatment |

Home handling can reveal an obvious failure, but do not describe it as regulated use-and-abuse testing. Record the observation and its limits. Independent review and any applicable product testing remain separate tasks.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Photograph a face before sewing it. A tiny change in eye position can move an expression from cheerful to deeply suspicious.
>
> Attach paired pieces with temporary yarn first. Matching yarn hides a seam beautifully and can also hide the fact that the ears are on different levels.
>
> Count your construction pins out and back in. A finished plush should never contain an undocumented surprise.

## Placement and attachment tips

- Pin all parts in place with long pins or locking markers before sewing anything.
- Take a photo from the front and from the side, and compare with the reference.
- Sew limbs with the yarn tail left from the piece, matching colors.
- Attach heads with a strong, stitch-by-stitch seam all the way around the neck.
- Embroider features after the head is stuffed, so the surface tension is correct.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Eyes go in before closing
> Safety eyes with washers must be inserted before the piece is closed and stuffed. Decide their position early, and test with the post through the fabric before snapping on the washer, which is very hard to remove.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Low eyes look cute
> Placing eyes slightly below the middle of a head often gives a younger, "cuter" look. Try a few positions with pins before committing.

> [!SAFETY] Features for young children
> For babies and young children, embroider eyes and noses instead of using safety eyes, buttons or beads. No fastening method in this book is certified as child-safe.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> The face is everything. Spend more time on the eyes than on the whole body.
