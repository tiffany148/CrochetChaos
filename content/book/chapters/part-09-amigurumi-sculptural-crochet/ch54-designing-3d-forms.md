Design a three-dimensional object as a collection of surfaces and volumes. Decide where it widens, where it maintains circumference, where it narrows, and where separate parts attach. Stitch counts then describe a proposed shell. A sample reveals whether that shell behaves like the intended form.

Begin with a simple silhouette and a few cross-sections. A mushroom might combine a cap, stem, and underside. An animal might combine a head, torso, muzzle, limbs, and ears. The pieces do not need to be mathematical solids, but simple shape vocabulary makes their relationships easier to plan.

## Use a dimensional skeleton

Record overall height, maximum width, depth, and important attachment levels. Draw front and side views at the same scale. A form can look correct from the front and be too shallow from the side. Mark which dimensions are essential and which can vary slightly.

Convert a desired circumference into an approximate stitch count using the actual shell gauge. At 5 sc per inch, a 6-inch circumference suggests 30 stitches. An ideal circular cross-section at that circumference would have a diameter near 1.91 inches. The filled crochet shell can depart from that ideal, so use the calculation as a planning model.

## Shape families

A cylinder maintains a side count. A cone changes count gradually with height. A rounded capsule expands at one end, keeps an even middle, and narrows at the other. A flat oval grows around a chain with increases concentrated at its ends. A shaped head can localize increases near cheeks or a muzzle instead of spreading them evenly.

The same maximum count does not establish the same shape. Thirty stitches around a short rounded object and thirty around a long tube produce different silhouettes. Keep the entire round schedule with the design rather than recording only its largest count.

## Original gradual cone study

Status: original structural prototype; count arithmetic checked, physical curvature untested. Use smooth yarn, a suitable hook, and a first-stitch marker. Work a spiral with no joins. The beginning ch-1 does not count. Inc means 2 sc in one stitch.

Round 1: 6 sc in an adjustable ring; mark first sc. Tighten and secure the starting tail as appropriate. Round 2: sc in each stitch; 6 sc. Round 3: [sc in next st, inc in next st] three times; 9 sc. Round 4: sc in each stitch; 9 sc. Round 5: [sc in next 2 sts, inc in next st] three times; 12 sc. Round 6: sc in each stitch; 12 sc. Round 7: [sc in next 3 sts, inc in next st] three times; 15 sc. Round 8: sc in each stitch; 15 sc. Round 9: [sc in next 4 sts, inc in next st] three times; 18 sc. Round 10: sc in each stitch; 18 sc. Fasten off without stuffing and retain a tail if later assembly is planned.

The opening remains 18 stitches. Each increase event adds three. Events occur every second round, so circumference grows more gradually than a six-increase-every-round disk. The exact cone angle, height, and whether visible corners form must be sampled.

Make a second version with an extra even round after each increase round. Compare its slope with the original. You changed height between expansion events without changing their output counts. Record the actual result rather than predicting a universal angle.

## Cylinder and base relationships

To make a side tube with 24 sc around, work 24 per round after establishing an appropriate base or foundation. A flat base with 24 perimeter stitches can be attached one-to-one only if its actual edge length and shape match the tube opening. Matching count helps but does not prove that a stiff base and soft tube fit without distortion.

A separate base lets you compare the pieces before joining and provides access for adjusting filling. A continuous base-to-side transition reduces one seam but fixes their relationship earlier. Choose based on the structure, not an assumption that fewer seams always means better design.

## Plan local volume with landmarks

For a hypothetical 24-stitch head, label four six-stitch sectors. If the front needs more volume, put a planned number of increases in its sector while preserving a clear total. For example, two increases in each of two front sectors add four stitches overall, producing 28. The new distribution is no longer four equal sectors unless you redefine it.

Keep the increased regions mapped through later rounds. A note saying “increase around the cheeks” is not enough for a tester. State exact stitches relative to markers and whether markers move with the original boundary or with a newly defined center.

## Attachment footprints

An appendage’s open rim needs a footprint on the body. Compare its perimeter and intended angle. A circular rim attached at a slant can create a different visible length from the same rim attached straight outward. The receiving surface’s curvature matters.

Make the component and body samples before committing to many identical limbs. Temporarily position the parts and inspect balance. If a head droops, investigate the attachment area, proportions, and filling rather than immediately adding rigid internal supports. Any armature introduces separate construction and suitability requirements.

## Scale a form carefully

Doubling every stitch and round count does not simply produce the same form at twice its size. Increase rates, curve sampling, and stitch proportions can change. Changing to thicker yarn can preserve the count schedule but change stiffness, surface detail, and weight distribution.

For geometrically similar solid objects, doubling linear dimensions multiplies surface area by four and volume by eight. That model explains why a larger plush can need much more filling than a small one. Crochet shells and actual packing are not perfect solids, so measure material use rather than treating the geometric ratios as exact purchasing quantities.

## Create a prototype record

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Intended silhouette | Front and side sketches with dimensions |
| Material | Yarn, hook, stitch method, shell gauge |
| Schedule | Every round and count, including even rounds |
| Landmarks | Front, back, attachment sites, and marker rules |
| Filling | Material and documented approach or measured mass |
| Assembly | Footprints, seam paths, sequence, and access |
| Observations | Actual dimensions, balance, gaps, and failures |
| Revision | Exact schedule changes and their reasons |

Change one major variable at a time when possible. If the next version changes yarn, hook, increase rate, filling, and seam placement together, you may improve the object without knowing which change helped. A controlled comparison produces reusable knowledge.

## Three-dimensional design exercise

Use the Orbit Pebble from Chapter 52 and the cone above as separate components on a work surface. Sketch three different possible objects without attaching them. Identify which silhouette depends on a wider base, which needs a different cone angle, and which requires a flattened attachment region.

Choose one and write the changes needed before sewing. Make the revised component rather than forcing the original through seam tension. Record the result and whether it meets the dimensional skeleton. This exercise develops design decisions; it does not declare the resulting untested object a finished toy pattern.

>
> Draw a side view. Many mysterious three-dimensional problems were invisible in the front sketch because the depth was never planned.

## Shape families and their counts

| Shape | How it is made | Typical structure |
|---|---|---|
| Sphere | Increase, work even, decrease | 6 increases per round up, even rounds, 6 decreases per round down |
| Oval or egg | Increase, longer even section, decrease | As a sphere, with more even rounds |
| Cylinder | Flat circle base, then even rounds | Work in BLO for a crisp edge at the base |
| Cone | Gradual increases from a point | Fewer increases per round, spread out |
| Capsule | Start on a foundation chain, working around both sides | Increases at the two ends only |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — BLO makes a corner
> Working one round in the back loop only turns a flat circle into the side of a cylinder with a crisp edge. It is how most flat-bottomed pots, baskets and feet are made.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Fewer increases make a cone
> A circle with 6 increases per round lies flat. With fewer increases per round, or increases every other round, it rises into a cone.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Every animal is a ball, a tube and a few cones. Learn those three, and you can make anything.


> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Tunisian needs a bigger hook
> Tunisian fabric is dense. Many crocheters go up one or two hook sizes from the yarn label.
