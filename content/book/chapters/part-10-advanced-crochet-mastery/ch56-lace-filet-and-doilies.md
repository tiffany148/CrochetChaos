Lace depends on planned solid areas and openings. The empty space is part of the design, not missing work. A successful lace instruction accounts for where each stitch attaches, how far chains bridge, how repeats meet, and how the finished fabric is treated.

Filet organizes those decisions into a grid. A doily may use circular repeats and a changing perimeter rather than a rectangular grid. Both require the count, repeat geometry, and actual stitch proportions to agree.

## Shared-edge filet math

This chapter uses a three-position-per-block filet convention. One boundary double crochet starts the row. An open block then adds ch 2 and the next boundary dc. A filled block adds three dc: two interior stitches and the next boundary. Neighboring blocks share a boundary rather than each owning four new stitches.

![filet (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/filet.png)

For B blocks across, the row has 3B + 1 positions. Five blocks need 16 positions, not 20. Ten blocks need 31. Other filet conventions exist; the chart key must state which one is used.

An open block contains a hole bounded by neighboring dc posts and a chain bridge. A filled block contains the corresponding interior double crochets. Do not count open cells as missing blocks or give every filled block four additional dc.

Foundation: ch 18. Setup Row 0: dc in fourth chain from hook and each remaining chain; the three skipped chains count as the first dc, giving 16 dc. This setup row is below the chart and is not one of its five rows.

For each chart row: ch 3, turn; this replaces the first boundary dc, so do not work into the first actual position beneath it. Process the five blocks in working order. For O: ch 2, skip the next two positions, dc in the next boundary position. For X: dc in each of the next three positions. A position can be a dc top or an individual chain from the preceding row. In this sample, work into individual chain links when filling a previously open cell, preserving the indexed grid.

The last boundary of every row goes into the top of the preceding beginning ch-3. Mark that top chain. Complete chart Rows 1–5, then fasten off and secure ends.

Rows with one X have 8 dc including the beginning chain and 8 newly made chain links, totaling 16 positions. Rows with three X have 12 dc and 4 chains. The all-filled middle row has 16 dc. The chart contains 13 X cells and 12 O cells across 25 cells.

## Why a square chart can make a stretched image

The width of a block comes from three stitch-position widths, while its height comes from one tall-stitch row. Those dimensions may not be equal in your fabric. A round-looking chart design can become wide or tall after crocheting.

Measure the actual finished block width and row height. Draw future designs on a grid with those proportions when an image’s shape matters. A different stitch height, such as a defined extended double crochet, may be investigated, but it changes the fabric and needs another sample. Do not promise that one stitch substitution makes every filet cell square.

## Thread and hook choices

Crochet thread numbering and ordinary yarn categories are different systems. Compare the actual thread specification and metric hook size. A fine thread can still make an open, unstable grid if the hook and tension are unsuitable. A heavier yarn can produce filet, but the image and drape may differ from delicate cotton lace.

Choose clear stitch definition for a first sample. A halo or multicolor strand can obscure the contrast between open and filled blocks. Save the label and sample after the intended finishing treatment. That finished sample supplies the meaningful gauge.

## Circular lace and doily repeats

A circular lace pattern distributes repeats around a center. Each round must consume the previous round’s complete arrangement, including its spaces. A new shell may attach in a chain arch rather than a stitch top; a decrease may combine units across a boundary.

Audit one complete repeat, then multiply by the number around the circle. Include any special beginning and ending treatment. If the repeat begins halfway through a shell to disguise a seam, the two halves still need to combine into the correct full unit.

The circumference needed for a flat doily grows with radius, but tall stitches and chain arches determine how much distance each round adds. Do not assume six increases per round applies to every lace fabric. Ruffling and cupping require both count and physical-geometry checks.

## Finish lace without inventing stability

Wash or otherwise treat the sample only as its materials allow. Arrange it with controlled, even tension if blocking is part of the design. Mark and measure the relaxed result after it is fully dry and released from pins. Record whether the openings remain defined.

A lace edge held under extreme tension can look convincing while pinned and shrink back after release. A rigid starch or other stiffener creates a different finished object and care regime. Test compatibility and do not treat a stiffened display sample as proof of an unstiffened garment’s behavior.

## Design a filet name panel

Sketch letters on a block grid, then check their orientation. Determine block count B and calculate 3B + 1 positions. Add any border outside that grid explicitly. Test a small letter or corner before committing to a long name panel.

Keep a symbol or letter key in addition to color so the chart remains readable in grayscale. Mark row starts and alternate working directions for turned rows. A chart’s printed visual order and the crocheter’s current working direction are related but not identical concepts.

## Filet counting rule

In shared-edge filet with dc, a row of n squares uses 3n + 1 stitches, counting each chain of a ch-2 as one stitch. Open squares are (dc, ch 2, skip 2); filled squares replace the ch 2 with 2 dc.

| Squares across | Stitches (3n + 1) | Foundation chain (3n + 3, when the first dc goes in the 4th ch from the hook) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 31 | 33 |
| 20 | 61 | 63 |
| 30 | 91 | 93 |

Foundation conventions differ between patterns. Follow the pattern's own setup, and check the row count against 3n + 1.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Filet squares are not square
> Crochet mesh squares are usually taller or shorter than they are wide, so charts can look stretched in the finished piece. Swatch, and choose a chart that allows for the real proportion.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Starch for lace
> Doilies and lace are often blocked with starch or a stiffening solution to hold their shape. Test on a swatch, and use only products made for fabric.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> In lace, the holes are the pattern. Count them like stitches.
