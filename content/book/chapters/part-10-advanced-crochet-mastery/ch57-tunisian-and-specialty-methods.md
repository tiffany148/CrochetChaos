Specialty methods may change how many loops remain on the hook, which tool supports them, or how a strip is assembled. Read their complete working cycle before applying ordinary crochet assumptions. In Tunisian crochet, a row commonly includes a forward pass and a return pass. In broomstick lace, elongated loops are temporarily supported on a large tool. In hairpin lace, a loom organizes loops around a central crochet spine.

## Tunisian tools and working space

Use a Tunisian hook long enough, or with a suitable cable, to hold the sample’s loops without crowding them into the handle. A standard hook may serve for a narrow sample if its shaft is unobstructed and loops cannot slide off unexpectedly. Do not force a wide project onto a short shaped handle.

Choose a relaxed sample fabric and enough room for the retained loops. The nominal hook size alone does not establish Tunisian gauge. Keep forward- and return-pass tension consistent, and do not pull the return chain tightly to compensate for loose pickup loops.

## Tunisian simple stitch foundation

For an original 12-column exercise, ch 12. The live loop will represent the first retained column. Beginning in the second chain from the hook, insert into a back bump, yarn over, and draw up a loop, leaving it on the hook. Repeat through the remaining chain bumps. Eleven new loops plus the initial live loop give 12 loops on the hook.

![st_tunisian_return_pass (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_tunisian_return_pass.png)

Return pass: yarn over and draw through one loop only; this establishes the edge. Then repeat yarn over and draw through two loops until one live loop remains. Starting with 12 loops, the two-loop reduction occurs 11 times. Do not turn the fabric for the ordinary method used here.

Status: the exercise and loop counts are audited, but no physical sample has been made for this edition. The first retained Tunisian loop’s role is specific to this construction; it does not change the ordinary rule that the live loop is not a completed foundation chain.

## Subsequent simple-stitch rows

With the public face toward you, identify the vertical bars. The live loop already represents the first edge column. Skip the first bar directly beneath it. Insert under the next front vertical bar, yarn over, and draw up a loop to retain. Repeat through the ten internal bars in this 12-column sample.

![st_tunisian_simple_stitch (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_tunisian_simple_stitch.png)

For the final edge column, identify both strands of the outer edge loop and insert under both, then draw up the last loop. You again have 12 loops. Complete the same return pass. That full forward-and-return cycle is one row in the counting convention here.

Repeat for a total of ten rows including the foundation cycle. Count loops at the end of every forward pass and confirm one remains after each return. If the edge leans, check whether you picked up the first bar unnecessarily or omitted the final edge.

## Bind off without collecting the whole row

After the final return pass, skip the first vertical bar as before. Insert under the next bar, yarn over, and draw through the bar and live loop as a slip stitch. Continue across the ten internal bars. Work the final slip stitch under both strands of the edge loop. Fasten off and secure ends.

![st_tunisian_slip_stitch_finish (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/st_tunisian_slip_stitch_finish.png)

The bind-off makes 11 worked slip stitches after the initial edge loop. Keep them relaxed enough to preserve width. An overtight bind-off can draw in a sample whose earlier rows were correctly sized.

## Read Tunisian fabric before adding variations

Identify the front vertical bars, the reverse bars, and the return-pass chains. Tunisian knit and purl stitches change the pickup path and yarn position rather than merely changing a familiar ordinary crochet name. Learn those paths from reliable demonstrations and verified samples before mixing them into a fitted design.

![tunisian_insert (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/tunisian_insert.png)

Curl can occur in Tunisian simple stitch. Evaluate tool size, tension, stitch balance, and finishing in a sample. A border may help manage an edge but cannot be assumed to eliminate all curl. Record actual behavior after care and rest.

## Five-loop broomstick lace experiment

Status: untested structural exercise. Use smooth yarn, a crochet hook, and a smooth large needle or dowel suited to holding elongated loops. Ch 16 and work 15 sc from the second chain. Do not turn for the pickup and grouping procedure described here.

![broomstick_hairpin (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/broomstick_hairpin.png)

Enlarge the live loop and place it on the large tool as the first elongated loop. From the next sc through the final sc, insert the hook, draw up an elongated loop, and transfer it to the tool in the same orientation. The initial elongated live loop plus 14 pickups gives 15 loops; do not also pick up a new loop in the first sc beneath the enlarged live loop.

Take the first five elongated loops together onto the crochet hook without twisting them. Catch the working yarn through the group and ch 1 to reach the top; this does not count. Work 5 sc through the center of that same five-loop bundle. Take the next five loops as a group and work 5 sc through their center. Repeat for the final five loops. You have three bundles and 15 sc. Fasten off for this one-band experiment.

The large tool controls loop length, while the grouping controls the visible eyelets. Keep the tool’s surface smooth and the loop orientation consistent. This single band is an investigation, not a complete repeating fabric pattern. A repeating version needs explicit pickup direction and edge handling after the first band, verified on a sample.

## Hairpin lace structure

A hairpin loom has parallel rods separated by a chosen distance. Yarn forms long side loops as the loom is rotated, while crochet stitches connect the center. Strips can be joined through individual loops or grouped loops to create different fabrics.

Count loops on each side separately and preserve their order when removing the strip. A lifeline through the side loops can retain their sequence during handling, but it does not specify the central stitch or joining method. Those remain part of the particular design.

Use the actual loom’s instructions for setup and rotation. A publication lesson must identify rod width, starting loop orientation, central stitch placement, strip length, removal, and joining. Do not improvise a finished garment from the statement “make a hairpin strip.” Method-specific checked photographs and additional written exercises remain necessary for a full hairpin lesson.

## Compare methods systematically

| Method | Temporary structure | Main count checkpoint |
|---|---|---|
| Tunisian simple stitch | Retained loops on hook | Same column count after each forward pass |
| Broomstick band | Elongated loops on large tool | Loop groups and output sc remain accounted for |
| Hairpin strip | Loops arranged around two rods | Equal planned side-loop totals and intact order |

Do not use a method’s decorative name as its gauge. Measure the completed, treated fabric and include its joins or border. A specialty strip can stretch differently along its length and across its loops.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Count Tunisian loops before the return pass. A missing edge loop is easier to locate while the entire forward pass is still visible.
>
> In broomstick lace, the enlarged live loop is already one elongated loop. Counting it and then picking up from every base stitch can create a bonus loop.
>
> Keep long loops in order. A beautifully even strip becomes less cooperative after it has been converted into a loose yarn accordion.

## Tunisian troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Fabric curls strongly | Normal for Tss and Tks | Larger hook; add a border; block; try Tfs or Tps |
| Left edge loose | Last stitch worked loosely | Work the last stitch under both loops at the edge |
| Right edge loose | First return-pass stitch too loose | Snug the first "through 1" of the return pass |
| Stitch count grows | Picking up the first bar, or working into extra edge loops | The first loop on the hook counts as the first stitch; skip the first vertical bar |
| Stitch count shrinks | Missing the last stitch | Mark the end stitch |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Skip the first bar
> In Tunisian forward passes, the loop already on your hook is the first stitch. Start by inserting into the second vertical bar. Picking up the first bar adds a stitch each row.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — A cabled hook holds a blanket
> For pieces wider than a long Tunisian hook can hold, use a Tunisian hook with a flexible cable. The loops rest on the cable during the forward pass.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Tunisian crochet is crochet that thinks it's weaving. Give it a bigger hook than you think and let it relax.
