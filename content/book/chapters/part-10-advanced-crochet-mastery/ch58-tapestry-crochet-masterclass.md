Tapestry crochet makes pictures and geometric patterns out of solid, sturdy fabric. Two or more colors travel together through every row: one is worked, while the other is carried along the tops of the previous row and enclosed inside the new stitches, waiting its turn. The result is dense, reversible-looking fabric with no long loose strands. It is the technique behind mochila-style bags, graphic pillows and pictorial wall hangings, and it rewards careful counting more than any other colorwork.

![tapestry_carry (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/tapestry_carry.png)

## The core movement

1. Work in single crochet (the usual tapestry stitch) with color A, laying color B across the tops of the stitches you are working into.
2. Each time you insert the hook, it goes under the stitch top **and** over color B, so B is trapped inside the new stitch.
3. When the chart changes to B, work the last A stitch until its final yarn over, then complete it with B (Chapter 11).
4. Now carry A and work with B until the chart changes again.

![color_change (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/color_change.png)

Because the carried strand is enclosed, it adds thickness and makes the fabric firm. That is a virtue for bags, baskets and rugs, and a consideration for garments, which will be warmer and stiffer than plain fabric.

## Reading a tapestry chart

A tapestry chart is a grid. Each square is one stitch; each row of squares is one row or round. Row 1 is at the bottom. For right-handed crocheters working in rounds, every round is read from right to left, the same direction you work. In turned rows, right-side rows are read right to left and wrong-side rows left to right. Left-handed crocheters read the mirror image: left to right on the right side.

The 12 × 12 heart above contains 78 pink and 66 cream stitches (144 in all). Worked flat: ch 13, sc in the second chain from the hook and in each chain across (12 sc), then follow the chart, carrying the unused color throughout.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
> Single crochet stitches are usually a little wider than they are tall, so a motif charted on square graph paper can come out squat. Measure your own swatch (stitches per inch and rows per inch) and chart on paper with the same proportion, or stretch the chart vertically by adding rows.
>
> Count stitches from the last color change, not from the start of the row. "Five teal, then three yellow" is easier to keep track of than "stitch 47."
>
> Your yarns will twist around each other as you work. Turn the work in the opposite direction at the end of each row (or set the balls on opposite sides of your chair) and they untwist themselves.

## Rounds versus rows

**In rounds** the right side always faces you, so every stitch looks the same and the carried strand stays inside. Round work is ideal for bags, baskets and hats. The catch is that single crochet worked in the round leans: each round shifts slightly in the working direction, so vertical lines in the chart drift into a diagonal. Common remedies are working a center single crochet (also called a waistcoat or split single crochet, inserting the hook between the two legs of the stitch below instead of under the top V), which stacks more vertically, or working in joined and turned rounds.

**In rows** the work alternates between right side and wrong side. Stitches look slightly different on the two faces, and the carried color can peek through on one side. Many crocheters bring the carried strand behind the work on wrong-side rows, or work every row from the right side by cutting yarn at the end of each row (leaving fringe) for wall hangings.

## Keeping the fabric flat and the colors clean

- **Tension the carried strand lightly.** Before each color change, stretch the last few stitches gently so the carried yarn has enough length. A carried strand that is too short puckers the fabric; too long and it can loop out.
- **Choose a dominant color.** The color you pick up from below the other strand often looks slightly bolder. Pick up the motif color from underneath consistently and the motif will pop.
- **Hide the carried yarn.** Use a smaller hook than usual for the yarn, so stitches close firmly around the carried strand. Dark carried colors show through light stitches more than the reverse; test a swatch.
- **Use smooth, firm yarns.** Cotton and wool-blend yarns with good stitch definition show motifs crisply. Fuzzy or very dark yarn blurs edges.
- **Three or more colors.** You can carry two colors at once, but the fabric gets thicker. For more colors, consider carrying only the colors needed in that section and dropping others to be picked up later.

## Tapestry, intarsia and stranded colorwork compared

| Method | Unused color | Fabric | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tapestry | Carried and enclosed in each stitch | Dense, thick, firm | Bags, baskets, rugs, graphic panels |
| Intarsia | Each color block has its own bobbin; no carrying across | Single thickness, flexible | Large blocks of color in garments and blankets |
| Stranded (floats) | Carried loosely across the back as floats | Lighter than tapestry; floats on the WS | Small repeats in hats and mittens |
| Mosaic | Only one color per row; pattern from long stitches | Single color per row, textured | Geometric patterns without carrying |

## Planning a tapestry project

1. **Swatch with both colors.** Work a small square in the chart’s stitch, carrying the second color the whole time. Measure stitches and rows per inch after washing.
2. **Fit the repeat.** For a band of repeating motifs worked in the round, the total stitch count must be a multiple of the repeat. The diamond above repeats every 9 stitches: 72, 81 and 90 sts all work; 80 does not.
3. **Chart the full piece** on proportional graph paper, including any plain rows above and below.
4. **Estimate yarn generously.** Every stitch contains both colors, so each color's yardage is closer to that of the whole fabric than you might expect. Weigh a swatch to estimate (Chapter 68).

> [!GRANNY] Granny’s Wisdom
> Put a sticky note just above the row you are working on the chart, and move it up every row. Your eyes will thank you, and when someone interrupts you mid-row you will know exactly where you were.

> [!HINT]
> If a vertical line in your round-worked motif is drifting sideways, do not fight it row by row. Either switch to center single crochet, or deliberately shift the chart one stitch per few rounds to compensate. Test on a swatch first.
