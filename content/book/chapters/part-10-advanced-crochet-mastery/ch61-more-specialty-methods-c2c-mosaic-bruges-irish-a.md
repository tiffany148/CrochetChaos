Chapter 57 introduced Tunisian, broomstick and hairpin lace, and Chapter 58 covered tapestry crochet in depth. This chapter adds the other specialty methods you will meet in modern patterns: corner-to-corner, mosaic, planned pooling, Bruges lace, Irish crochet, crochet cables and freeform. Each section explains how the method is built, what it is good for, and what to check before you start.

> [!STATUS]
> Methods are described from their standard constructions. Arithmetic examples are calculated; no new samples were made for this chapter.

## Corner-to-corner (C2C)

C2C fabric is built from small square tiles worked on the diagonal. You start in one corner, increase one tile each diagonal row until the piece is as tall (or wide) as you need, and then decrease back to the opposite corner. Because every tile is one "pixel", a C2C graph looks just like a cross-stitch chart.

![c2c (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/c2c.png)

### How a tile is made

A common tile uses a chain of 6 to start and three dc per tile (see the Stitch Encyclopedia for the steps). Some patterns use hdc or sc tiles for a smaller pixel. The method is the same; tile size changes.

### C2C arithmetic for a rectangle

For a rectangle W tiles wide and H tiles tall, where W is the longer side:

- Total diagonal rows: W + H − 1.
- Increase on both edges for the first H rows.
- Then increase on one edge and decrease on the other for W − H rows, so the diagonal length stays at H.
- Decrease on both edges for the last H − 1 rows.
- Total tiles: W × H.

> **Example (arithmetic only):** a graph 60 tiles wide and 40 tiles tall has 60 + 40 − 1 = 99 diagonal rows and 2,400 tiles. For a square graph of 50 × 50 there are 99 diagonals: 50 increasing, then 49 decreasing.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Mark the switch rows
> Put a stitch marker on the edge where you switch from increasing to decreasing. On a rectangle, the two edges switch at different times, and it is easy to forget which edge is which after a break.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Wind a little bobbin for every color block in a C2C picture before you start. Ten minutes of winding saves an hour of untangling.

### Reading C2C graphs

- Read each diagonal row in the direction you work it; mark the row on a copy of the graph with a pencil or highlighter.
- Carry a color behind only for one or two tiles; for longer gaps, use a separate bobbin.
- Weave in ends in the same color area so they cannot show through.

## Mosaic crochet in more depth

Mosaic crochet places color one row at a time. Each row uses one color; a lower row is reached with long stitches to pull its color up. There are two main families.

| Feature | Overlay mosaic | Inset mosaic |
|---|---|---|
| Base fabric | sc in back loops | sc and chain spaces |
| Color placement | dc worked in front, into a lower row | dc dropped into chain spaces of lower rows |
| Typical yarn ends | Often cut at the end of every row (fringe or border) | Often worked in rows and turned; fewer cut ends in many patterns |
| Reverse side | Shows the stripes and float-like ridges | Varies by pattern |
| Chart style | Squares with dots or Xs | Squares with specific symbols for ch-sp and dc |

> [!HINT] Before your first mosaic project
> Make a small swatch from the pattern's own chart and read the pattern's chart key. The two families use different symbols, and a mosaic chart from one family cannot be worked with the other's rules.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Mosaic does not need two balls at once
> Each row of mosaic uses only one color. You never carry the second color, which is why mosaic is easier on the hands than tapestry.

## Planned pooling

Variegated yarn repeats its colors in a sequence. With the right stitch count and tension, the same colors can land on top of each other each row, forming a pattern (often an argyle-like diamond). This is called planned pooling.

1. Pull out a length of yarn and identify one full color sequence.
2. Work a stitch pattern that uses a consistent amount of yarn per stitch; moss (linen) stitch is common.
3. Find the number of stitches one color section covers, and adjust the stitch count and tension until each color stacks diagonally.
4. Change hook size slightly to fine-tune if the pattern drifts.

Not every variegated yarn pools in a predictable way. Yarns with long or irregular color repeats may never form a pattern.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Pooling is tension, not magic
> If the argyle starts drifting, you are probably crocheting tighter or looser than when you started. Loosen or tighten slightly for a stitch or two to pull it back.

## Bruges lace

Bruges lace is made from a narrow tape, usually a few dc wide, worked in rows with a chain loop at the end of each row. As the tape curves, the loops on the inner edge are joined together and loops on the outer edge join to neighboring curves. The result is flowing lace with smooth curves.

- Count loops carefully: the pattern tells you how many loops to join on each curve.
- Block Bruges lace flat so the curves open evenly.
- Thread and fine hooks make the classic look; heavier yarn makes bolder lace.

## Irish crochet

Irish crochet developed in nineteenth-century Ireland. Motifs (roses, leaves, shamrocks) are worked separately, often over a padding cord for raised edges, then pinned in place on a template and joined by an irregular mesh of chains and picots.

1. Make all motifs first, and block them.
2. Draw the outline of the finished piece on paper or fabric, and baste the motifs face down on it.
3. Fill the spaces with a chain-and-picot mesh, joining to the motifs as you go.
4. Remove the template and block the piece.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Irish crochet was made to be adapted. Two pieces made from the same motifs rarely look alike, and that is the charm.

## Crochet cables

Crochet cables use post stitches that skip ahead and then cross back over or behind other post stitches. A typical two-over-two cross: skip the next 2 posts, FPtr around the following 2, then FPtr around the 2 skipped posts, working in front of the first pair.

- Cables pull fabric narrower; allow extra width in the stitch count and swatch the cable panel.
- Charts for cables usually include a key showing which stitches cross in front and which behind.
- Work a plain or background row between crossing rows so the cable has room to lean.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Count the posts, not the tops
> In a cable row, every stitch needs a post to wrap. Before crossing, look at the row below and count posts. Counting the tops of the current row is how cables go wrong.

## Freeform crochet

Freeform crochet builds small "scrumbles" of texture—spirals, bullions, bobbles, ruffles—and joins them into a larger fabric with no fixed pattern.

- Choose a limited palette (three to five colors) and a range of textures.
- Make several scrumbles about the size of your palm, then join them with sc or sl st seams.
- Fill gaps with short rows or small shapes.
- Step back often; the eye judges balance better from a distance.

Freeform is not "anything goes": the same accuracy rules apply. Keep each scrumble flat with even tension so that the joined fabric lies smoothly.

## Bead crochet

Beads are threaded onto the yarn before you start, then pushed up one at a time as you work.

1. Choose beads with holes large enough for the yarn to pass through, and thread them on with a big-eye beading needle or a loop of fine wire.
2. Push the beads down the yarn, away from the work, until you need them.
3. To place a bead in single crochet, slide one up to the hook, insert the hook, draw up a loop and finish the sc. The bead sits on the side of the fabric facing away from you.
4. Work beaded rows so that the beads land on the right side. In turned rows, that usually means beading on wrong-side rows.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Thread more beads than you think
> Count the beads on your chart and thread a few extra. Running out means cutting the yarn and re-threading.

> [!SAFETY] Beads and children
> Beads are a choking hazard. Do not use them on toys or items for babies and young children.

## Felted (fulled) crochet

Animal-fiber crochet that is not superwash can be deliberately shrunk and thickened with hot water and agitation. This is called felting or fulling. Crochet felts well into sturdy bags, slippers and bowls.

- Use 100% wool or another feltable animal fiber. Superwash and most synthetics will not felt. Light or white wools sometimes felt less readily than darker shades of the same yarn, so test each color.
- Work loosely with a larger hook than usual, so the fibers have room to move together.
- **Always felt a swatch first.** Measure it before and after. Shrinkage varies a great deal with yarn, water, machine and time, and is usually greater in length than in width. Use your swatch's results to size the project. This book gives no standard shrinkage percentage.
- Felt in a washing machine on hot with a little detergent, inside a zipped laundry bag, and check often. Or felt by hand in hot soapy water. Shape the piece while it is wet and let it dry over a form.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Felting can't be undone
> Once fibers felt, the stitches cannot be unravelled. Check the piece every few minutes; you can always felt more, never less.

## Rugs and repurposed yarns

Fabric strips, T-shirt yarn and "plarn" (strips cut from plastic bags) make sturdy rugs, baskets and mats.

- Work in sc or hdc with a large hook for a firm, flat fabric.
- Rugs worked in rounds follow the flat circle rules (Chapter 25). Thick strips often need fewer increases than yarn, so lay the rug flat often.
- Place a non-slip rug pad under any rug used on a hard floor.
- Join fabric strips by sewing or by a slit-and-loop join so there are no knots.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Big rugs are heavy
> A large T-shirt-yarn rug can weigh several pounds. Support its weight on your lap or the table as you work, to protect your wrists.

## Interlocking crochet

Interlocking crochet (sometimes called double filet) uses two colors of filet mesh, worked so that the two meshes weave through each other. Each color forms its own mesh, and the result is reversible: the colors swap on the back. It follows a color chart like tapestry crochet, but no yarn is carried. Work from a pattern written for the technique, because the row order and the mesh placement are specific to it.

## Wire and thread jewelry

Fine craft wire and crochet thread can be worked with small steel hooks into bracelets, necklaces and earrings, often with beads.

- Use wire sold for crafting, in soft (dead-soft) temper; it bends without springing back.
- Work loose chains and sc; wire does not stretch and cannot be pulled tight like yarn.
- Wear eye protection when cutting wire, and file cut ends smooth.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Every specialty method is just a hook and a loop wearing a different hat. Learn one new technique a season.

## Choosing a specialty method

| Goal | Good method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Pixel picture or lettering | C2C or filet | Graphs translate directly |
| Geometric color pattern, easy hands | Mosaic | One color per row |
| Dense colorwork in a firm fabric | Tapestry | Carried yarn hidden inside stitches |
| Drapey, airy shawl | Broomstick, hairpin, Bruges | Open structures |
| Heirloom lace | Irish crochet, filet, doilies | Fine thread and motifs |
| Cozy textured blanket | Cables, waffle, bobbles | Raised post stitches |
| Artful one-off piece | Freeform | No fixed pattern |
| Sturdy bag or slippers | Felted crochet | Fulling thickens the fabric |
| Sparkle and drape | Bead crochet | Beads sit in the stitches |
| Floor rug | T-shirt yarn or fabric strips | Thick, washable, firm |

> [!HINT] Photograph every stage
> Photograph prototypes at each stage, with a ruler in the shot. The photos become your pattern illustrations and your record.
