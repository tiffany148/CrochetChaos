Finishing should reveal how a fabric will behave in use. It can settle stitches, organize lace, and prepare pieces for joining. It cannot supply missing rows or turn an incompatible fiber into the original material. Treat washing and blocking as controlled material procedures, then assess the actual result.

## Choose the treatment from the components

Read the instructions for every yarn, trim, lining, closure, and attachment. Use a compatible method for the combined object. A machine-washable main yarn does not make a leather label or unknown embellishment washable.

If composition or dye stability is unknown, investigate on a sample or obtain suitable textile-care advice before treating a valuable object. Do not identify unknown yarn by a casual flame test or assume that all natural fibers tolerate the same treatment.

## Wet finishing sequence

When the materials permit washing, prepare a clean container or the specified machine setting and a suitable cleaning product. Handle the fabric according to its directions. Support a heavy wet object throughout lifting. Avoid pulling it by a single shoulder or corner.

![blocking (schematic illustration)](../../assets/figures/blocking.png)

Remove excess water using a permitted method without twisting or distorting the object. For compatible hand-washed textiles, gentle pressing between towels can help. Move it to a clean drying surface and arrange it to the intended shape without forcing it beyond the sample’s demonstrated behavior.

Measure again only when fully dry and relaxed. Record the care procedure so the result can be repeated. A one-time stretch to a target dimension does not establish that the object will retain that dimension during wearing.

## Spray and steam are different procedures

Spray blocking introduces moisture from the surface and may not affect a dense fabric as thoroughly as washing. Steam introduces heat as well as moisture. Their results are not interchangeable simply because both are called blocking.

Use heat only where material guidance and a separate sample support it. Keep steam tools away from skin and avoid direct pressure on unknown yarn. Synthetic fibers can change permanently under heat, and raised texture can be flattened. An irreversible softening treatment must not be presented as routine rescue without documenting the material and desired result.

## Pin with a dimensional plan

For lace, use suitable rust-resistant pins or wires to distribute tension at intended points. Match corresponding repeats rather than stretching only the corners. Count all pins in and out. Keep the setup away from children and animals.

For garment pieces, compare the schematic and measured seam relationships. Do not pin two unequal sleeves aggressively to the same outline and assume the fit problem is permanently solved. Record the relaxed dimensions after release, not only the pinned ones.

## Stabilize worn areas before they become holes

Inspect high-contact regions such as underarms, elbows, cuffs, bag corners, and attachment points. If yarn is thinning, determine whether the weakness is local or widespread. Reinforcement should connect to sound fabric beyond the worn area.

A compatible patch can distribute load over a larger footprint. A reconstructed stitch can restore a small local path when that path is known and surrounding yarn is sound. A separate component may be replaceable if its seams can be opened. These methods have different appearance, flexibility, and skill demands.

## Replace a worn component

Identify its original seam and attachment sequence. Measure the remaining opening and compare with the old component’s intended dimensions, recognizing that wear may have stretched it. Make a new component and sample its joining edge before removing all useful placement evidence.

Detach the old component using the seam-removal method in Chapter 35 where construction allows. Preserve neighboring yarn and mark landmarks. Attach the replacement with a compatible seam, inspect both faces, and test the intended movement. A replacement cuff should still allow passage and appropriate recovery after washing.

## Repair an opening center

If an adjustable ring has loosened but the first-round stitches remain sound, support the center and trace the original tail. It may be possible to reclose and secure it appropriately. Do not pull an abraded or fragile tail until it breaks inside the fabric.

If the ring strand itself is damaged, a new supporting path may need to pass through sound first-round structure. Prove that path in a duplicate sample. The exact repair depends on the stitch anatomy and damage; tightening an unrelated surface strand can distort the motif without restoring its center.

## Blend a repair honestly

Match fiber behavior and care before chasing a perfect color match. A visually similar repair yarn that shrinks differently can distort the mended region. If a compatible exact match is unavailable, a deliberate contrast may be more coherent than an unstable imitation.

Retained original yarn is useful, but inspect it for age, abrasion, and contamination. Treat a repair sample and compare it with the existing fabric. Do not assume old and new yarn remain identical because their labels once matched.

## Post-care mending audit

| Item | Check after treatment and rest |
|---|---|
| Dimensions | Repair and surrounding fabric remain compatible |
| Seam | No new gap, gathered edge, or emerging tail |
| Surface | No rigid lump or excessively open region |
| Movement | Intended stretch and bending remain usable |
| Color | Transfer or mismatch documented |
| Use | Repair suits the actual function rather than appearance alone |

For items with protective, medical, or restraint functions, ordinary crochet mending does not establish restored certified performance. Seek the appropriate manufacturer or specialist guidance rather than substituting this textile lesson for equipment approval.

## Before-and-after record exercise

Take the patch sample from Chapter 36. Measure its base width and height, patch footprint, and flexibility before treatment. Wash and dry according to the combined materials’ instructions. Repeat the observations after rest and inspect the seam closely.

Write one result that supports the method and one remaining uncertainty. “The patch stayed attached in this wash” is a useful observation. “This repair will never fail” is not established by it. Keep the sample and record for future decisions.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Repair a thinning area while there is still sound fabric to work with. A small worn patch is usually easier to assess than a missing section.
>
> Record the relaxed dimensions after blocking. Pins are temporary collaborators, not permanent garment components.
>
> Save compatible repair yarn with the finished object’s record. A few labeled lengths can make a later mend much less complicated.

## Blocking methods compared

| Method | How | Good for | Not for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wet blocking | Soak, squeeze out water (never wring), pin to shape, dry flat | Wool, cotton, most natural fibers | Items that must not get wet |
| Spray blocking | Pin dry, then mist with water and let dry | Delicate items, quick shaping | Big corrections |
| Steam blocking | Hold a steam iron above the fabric, never pressing; let cool pinned | Wool; careful use on acrylic to relax drape | Textured stitches that would flatten |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Use blocking mats and lots of pins
> Foam blocking mats with a grid let you square up granny squares quickly. Rust-proof pins every inch or two keep edges straight.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Never wring wet wool
> Squeeze water out, roll the piece in a towel, and press. Wringing and agitation can felt or stretch wool.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Blocking is the magic trick that makes all your stitches look like they went to finishing school.
