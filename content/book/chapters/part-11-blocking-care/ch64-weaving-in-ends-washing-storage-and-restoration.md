Finishing is the difference between a piece that looks handmade and one that looks home-made. This chapter collects the finishing jobs that come after the last stitch: weaving in ends so they stay put, choosing a blocking method for the fiber, washing and drying, storing pieces between seasons, and restoring older crochet. It builds on Chapter 63 (blocking, washing and mending) and Chapter 65 (care and storage).

> [!STATUS]
> Standard care practice. Always follow the yarn label first, and test any wet, steam or cleaning method on a swatch of the same yarn before treating a finished piece.

## Weaving in ends that stay woven

An end that is woven in only one direction can work its way out, especially in smooth or slippery yarn. The aim is to make the tail change direction at least once, travel through stitches of the same color, and stay hidden on the wrong side.

| Method | How | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Three-direction weave | Weave about 2 in in one direction, turn and weave back through different loops, then turn again for a short distance | Most yarns; slippery fibers |
| Along the posts | Run the tail up and down the posts of a column of stitches | Rows of dc or tr |
| Through the round | Follow the tops of a round on the wrong side, then reverse | Hats, motifs, amigurumi |
| Split-ply | Pass the needle through the plies of the yarn itself, not between stitches | Plied wool; reversible pieces |
| Crochet over the tail | Lay the tail along the tops and work the next row over it | Saving time on color changes |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Leave a 6-inch tail
> A tail long enough to thread on a needle with room to spare is far easier to weave than a 2-inch stub. Cut generous tails when you fasten off or change colors.

> [!HINT] Do not trim until after blocking
> Weave the ends, block the piece, then trim. Blocking can stretch the fabric enough to pull a short end back out to the surface.

## Choosing a blocking method by fiber

| Fiber | Wet blocking | Steam blocking | Spray blocking | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wool and other animal fibers | Yes | Yes, held above the fabric | Yes | Avoid agitation in warm water unless felting is the goal; superwash wool can grow when wet |
| Cotton and linen | Yes | Yes | Yes | Can take firm shaping; dries slowly |
| Acrylic | Limited effect | With great care: heat changes it permanently | Limited effect | Steam relaxes acrylic for good; test first |
| Silk | Yes, gently | Low heat only | Yes | Can lose strength when wet; support the weight |
| Blends | Follow the most delicate fiber | Test | Yes | Read the label percentages |

> [!SAFETY] Heat and steam
> Hold an iron or steamer above the fabric and never press down on crochet; pressing flattens texture permanently. Keep steam away from hands and children.

### Laying out a piece to block

1. Wet or dampen the piece as the method requires and press out excess water in a towel (do not wring).
2. Lay it on a blocking mat or folded towels.
3. Measure to the schematic dimensions and pin the corners first, then the midpoints of each side, then between them.
4. Use many pins along straight edges, or blocking wires threaded through the edge stitches.
5. Leave it until completely dry.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Pin points before scallops
> For lace edgings, pin the tip of every point or scallop. Pinning only the straight edge leaves the points curled.

## Washing finished crochet

- Use cool to lukewarm water and a mild detergent or wool wash unless the label allows more.
- Support the whole piece in your hands when lifting it out of water; wet crochet is heavy and stretches.
- Roll it in a towel to remove water, then dry it flat.
- Machine washing and drying are only safe when every yarn in the piece allows it. A machine-washable yarn joined with a hand-wash yarn makes the whole piece hand-wash.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Keep one ball band from every gift you make, clipped to a card with the recipient's name. When they ask how to wash it, you will know.

## Storing crochet between seasons

- Clean before storing; food marks and body oils attract insects and can set into stains.
- Store garments folded, not hung, so they do not stretch at the shoulders.
- Use breathable fabric bags or boxes; sealed plastic can trap moisture.
- Check wool and other animal fibers regularly for signs of moths or carpet beetles, such as small holes or larvae casings.
- Keep pieces out of direct sunlight, which can fade colors.

## Restoring older crochet

Vintage and heirloom crochet can often be repaired, but the safest approach is the gentlest one.

| Problem | First step | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Broken thread in lace | Stop the run by tying the broken ends to a nearby intact thread | Re-crochet the missing section with matching thread |
| Loose motif in a join | Find where the join stitch broke | Re-join with a slip stitch or whipstitch |
| Worn edging | Remove the damaged edging round | Work a new edging in a similar thread |
| Yellowing or stains | Test colorfastness on a hidden spot | Gentle soaking in plain water first; do not use bleach on old or unknown fibers |
| Very fragile fabric | Do not wash | Consult a textile conservator |

> [!SAFETY] Heirlooms and conservators
> Some old textiles are too fragile for home cleaning. If a piece has historical or family value and is weak, discolored or brittle, ask a textile conservator or a museum's advice service before washing or repairing it.

## Finishing checklist (reference)

| Step | Done when |
|---|---|
| Count and check | The last round or row matches the pattern count |
| Weave ends | Each tail changes direction at least once |
| Seam or join | Seams are flat and the same tension as the fabric |
| Block | The piece matches the schematic measurements |
| Trim ends | After blocking, close to the surface |
| Attach notions | Buttons, linings and labels sewn securely |
| Final check | Pulled gently in each direction; nothing gapes |
