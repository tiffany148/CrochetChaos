Running out of yarn halfway through a blanket, or buying three extra skeins you never use, are both avoidable with a little arithmetic. This chapter shows how to estimate how much yarn a project needs using numbers you can measure: your gauge swatch, the yarn label and a kitchen scale. Every method here is an estimate. The most reliable estimate always comes from your own yarn, hook and tension.

> [!STATUS]
> The formulas below are standard proportional arithmetic. The worked examples use round, illustrative numbers, not measurements from a sample. Always add a safety margin and buy from one dye lot where it matters.

## The two numbers on every label

A yarn label usually gives the **weight of the ball** (grams or ounces) and the **length** (yards or meters). Dividing one by the other gives the yarn's length per gram, which lets you convert between weight and length for that yarn.

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Yards per gram | label yards ÷ label grams |
| Grams per yard | label grams ÷ label yards |
| Meters to yards | meters × 1.0936 |
| Yards to meters | yards × 0.9144 |
| Grams to ounces | grams ÷ 28.35 |

Example: a 100 g ball of 200 yd has 200 ÷ 100 = 2 yd per gram.

## Method 1: weigh a swatch

This is the most reliable method you can do at home.

1. Make a swatch at least 6 in × 6 in in the pattern stitch, with your hook. Finish it as the project will be finished (wash and dry it if the project will be washed).
2. Measure its area: width × height in square inches.
3. Weigh it on a kitchen or postal scale that reads to 1 g or better.
4. Divide the swatch weight by its area to get grams per square inch.
5. Multiply by the area of the project (from the schematic).
6. Convert grams to balls using the label, then add a margin.

> [!HINT] Worked example (illustrative numbers)
> A swatch is 6 in × 6 in = 36 sq in and weighs 9 g, so 0.25 g per sq in. A throw of 40 in × 50 in = 2,000 sq in needs about 2,000 × 0.25 = 500 g. With 100 g balls that is 5 balls before margin. Adding about 10–15% (a common planning allowance, not a rule) gives 550–575 g, so buy 6 balls.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Small scales lie about small swatches
> A kitchen scale that reads in 1 g steps can be off by a gram or more on a tiny swatch, and that error is multiplied by the whole project. Weigh a bigger swatch, or weigh several swatches together.

## Method 2: measure yarn per stitch

Useful when a project is mostly one stitch, such as a hat or a blanket in rounds.

1. Measure out a known length of yarn, for example 2 yd, and mark it.
2. Work stitches until the mark reaches the hook, and count them.
3. Divide length by stitches to get yarn per stitch.
4. Multiply by the total number of stitches in the project.

> [!HINT] Worked example (illustrative numbers)
> 2 yd (72 in) makes 40 hdc, so 1.8 in per hdc. A blanket of 120 hdc × 90 rows = 10,800 hdc needs about 10,800 × 1.8 = 19,440 in = 540 yd, before margin.

Turning chains, joins, borders and ends add to this. Count the border separately with the same method.

## Method 3: scale from the pattern

If the pattern gives yardage for a size and you change the size, scale by area for flat pieces:

new yardage ≈ pattern yardage × (new area ÷ pattern area)

If you change only one dimension, scale by that dimension. Doubling the length of a scarf roughly doubles its yarn; doubling both length and width roughly quadruples it.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — A bigger hook does not automatically save yarn
> A looser fabric made with a larger hook covers more area per stitch, but each stitch also uses more yarn. The two effects partly cancel, so do not assume a looser version needs fewer balls; swatch and weigh.

## Substituting yarn by length, not weight

When you substitute a yarn, match **total length**, never number of balls. Balls of different yarns weigh and measure differently.

| Step | Example (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Pattern needs | 6 balls × 180 yd = 1,080 yd |
| Your yarn has | 220 yd per ball |
| Balls needed | 1,080 ÷ 220 = 4.9, round up to 5 |
| With margin | Buy 6 if you cannot get more of the same dye lot later |

## Stitch-hungry fabrics

Some stitches use much more yarn than others for the same area because they stack many loops into one place. As a general tendency, these use more yarn than plain sc or dc fabric: bobbles, puffs and popcorns; post-stitch textures and cables; crocodile stitch; dense tapestry crochet (which carries a second strand); and linked stitches. Lacy fabrics such as mesh and filet spaces tend to use less. Always swatch rather than guessing a percentage.

## Estimating what is left in a ball

Weigh a partly used ball and multiply by the yarn's yards per gram. Do this before the last section of a project, such as a border, so you know whether to start a new ball now or join one partway around.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Before the border, I always weigh what I have left and weigh the first full round of border. Divide one by the other and you know how many rounds you can afford.

## Making the last ball last

- Weigh the remaining yarn, work one row and weigh again; the difference tells you how many rows remain.
- For a stripe project, use the ball you have most of for the widest stripes.
- For two-sleeve garments, wind the remaining yarn into two equal balls by weight before starting the second sleeve, so both sleeves finish together.
