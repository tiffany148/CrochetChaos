A reproducible pattern lets another crocheter make the intended object without needing access to your memory. It states the materials, size, fabric, conventions, construction, stitch definitions, instructions, finishing, and checks. A photograph can show what you made, but it cannot supply every decision that produced it.

## Record while you design

Write down each row as it is worked. Record a change before making it if possible, then confirm the actual result afterward. If you unravel and revise, distinguish the abandoned row from the accepted row. A notebook filled with unlabeled crossed-out numbers becomes difficult to audit.

Give each version a date or identifier. Photograph major stages beside a ruler when dimensions matter. Keep swatches and label their treatment. Do not assign a tested gauge to a yarn you did not use simply because its label looks similar.

## Required pattern information

Begin with the project name, terminology system, intended construction, sizes, finished measurements, and intended ease where applicable. List exact yarn specifications used in the sample, actual consumption when measured, hook sizes, and notions. Provide gauge with stitch pattern, measurement span, and finishing condition.

Explain whether beginning chains count, whether rounds join, whether the work turns, where markers go, and what counts include. Define every special stitch. If multiple sizes share one instruction, state their order consistently. Include a schematic or dimension table for objects whose fit depends on several dimensions.

Materials estimates must match their evidence. “Estimated 250 yd; sample measurement pending” is honest. “Uses 250 yd” implies a level of observation that a mathematical guess does not provide. Do not invent a sample’s yarn brand, gauge, photographed size, or testing history.

## Write destinations explicitly

“Make six shells” is incomplete if the reader does not know where they go. “Work one defined shell in each of the next six chain-2 spaces” specifies both action and destination. “Decrease evenly” may be acceptable as a design exercise, but a published fitted pattern should normally provide a reproducible distribution or a clear method for calculating it.

Use “in each of next 3 stitches” when three separate destinations are intended. Use parentheses for a group in one destination under this book’s convention. State how many times a repeat is worked in total. Avoid “repeat again” where the total number is uncertain.

## State counts that help the reader

Put totals at meaningful checkpoints, especially after shaping. For a row that includes different unit types, list them separately if a combined number would be misleading. “18 dc and 6 ch-1 spaces” gives more information than “24” without explanation.

For a spiral, identify the first stitch and marker movement. For a joined round, name the exact joining destination and whether the join counts. For a turned piece, identify the RS at an appropriate stage. These details are small to write and large to reconstruct later.

## An original pattern written as a complete instruction set

Practice Needle Sleeve is a small folded single crochet pocket for organizing blunt yarn needles in a project bag. It is not childproof or puncture-resistant and is not intended for sharp sewing needles. Status: original instructional prototype; row counts audited; physical sample, usable size, retention, and laundering tests pending. Do not represent it as a tested storage product.

Materials: smooth yarn, compatible hook, scissors, blunt yarn needle for seaming, and two locking markers. Choose a fabric dense enough that the intended blunt needles do not pass through its openings. No yarn quantity or finished dimensions are claimed until a physical sample is made. This is a count-based prototype; adjust only after measuring the intended contents and a washed swatch.

Terminology: US. Turning ch-1 never counts. Work under both top loops unless stated otherwise. Work flat, then fold and seam. All rows contain 10 sc.

Foundation: ch 11. Row 1: sc in second ch from hook and each remaining ch; 10 sc. Mark first and last sc.

Rows 2–24: ch 1, turn; sc in each sc across; 10 sc. Move edge markers. Fasten off leaving a tail long enough for the actual seam method; if unsure, join a separate seaming length rather than claiming a fixed tail will always suffice. Secure the starting tail.

Assembly: lay the rectangle flat. Designate one short end as the pocket opening edge and the other as the back’s upper edge. Fold the opening end upward so the lower eight rows lie over the next eight rows. The remaining eight rows extend above the opening as a flap. Align the two layered side edges without stretching.

Join each layered side over its eight-row pocket depth using a defined whipstitch seam: pass the needle through corresponding edge strands from the front layer to the back layer, bring the yarn around the outside edge, and repeat at matched positions. Keep the seam snug enough to close the side but loose enough to avoid gathering. Leave the short pocket opening unseamed. Secure the seam ends within the fabric.

Fold the remaining extension over the opening as a flap. No closure is specified in this prototype. Do not add a loose decorative tie or small button without evaluating the intended use. Insert only appropriate blunt tools after checking that they are retained during ordinary handling. If the pocket is too open or shallow, revise its gauge or dimensions and record the revision.

Checks: 24 rows × 10 sc = 240 worked sc. The fold uses eight front rows, eight corresponding back rows, and eight flap rows. The actual depth and width depend on gauge and seam take-up. Arithmetic confirms the partition, not storage performance.

## What makes the prototype publishable later

Make the sample in a recorded yarn and hook. Measure it before and after the stated care treatment. Weigh or otherwise measure consumption. Test retention with the actual intended contents. Verify the fold and seam from the written directions without relying on a sketch. Photograph the completed stages and record every change.

Then ask an independent tester to follow the instructions. Their questions reveal missing assumptions. A tester’s successful sample in one yarn and size does not establish universal performance in every substitute. The published pattern should accurately state what was sampled and tested.

## Keep editorial checks separate

An arithmetic check verifies counts and relationships. A technical edit checks whether written directions are internally consistent and sufficiently specified. A physical sample checks whether the structure can be made and behaves as expected. Fit testing checks the object on its intended body or form. Copyediting improves language and consistency. Proofreading checks the assembled pages.

These tasks support each other but cannot replace each other. A perfectly proofread instruction can contain the wrong increase schedule. A beautiful sample can be impossible to reproduce from incomplete notes. Record each review separately rather than giving the pattern a single unexplained “approved” label.

## Pattern writing style guide (summary)

- Give each row or round a number and a stitch count at the end.
- Use the standard abbreviations (Chapter 81) and define any special stitch.
- Say whether turning chains count as stitches.
- Write repeats with one clear convention, and use it every time.
- State the finished measurements, the gauge (stitches and rows over a stated size), the hook, yarn weight, yardage and notions.
- State the testing status honestly: "tested by three testers", "tech-edited", or "not yet tested".

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Write the count, every row
> A count at the end of every row or round lets the crocheter check their work. It also lets you, the designer, catch your own arithmetic mistakes.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Choose US or UK terms and say so
> State at the top which terms the pattern uses. Mixing the two, even once, can turn a sc into a dc.
