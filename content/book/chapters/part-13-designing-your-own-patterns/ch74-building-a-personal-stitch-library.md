A stitch library is a collection of usable evidence. Each sample should tell you what it is, how it was made, how it was treated, and what changed. A beautiful unlabeled square can inspire a project; a labeled square can help reproduce one.

Organize the library around decisions you make repeatedly: choosing fabric, predicting dimensions, matching seams, creating openings, and diagnosing mistakes. It can contain successful swatches, rejected experiments, construction tests, and useful repair studies. Failure becomes valuable when its cause and limits remain attached to the sample.

## Give every sample an identity

Assign a simple code, such as ST-001 for a stitch sample, JN-001 for a join, or RP-001 for a repair. Use a sequence that will remain understandable when the collection grows. A code is an organizational aid, not a claim that different fabrics share the same gauge.

Attach a tag that cannot snag or damage the sample in storage. Keep loose small tags away from items intended for children or animals. A paper record stored separately can link through the same identifier and a photograph.

## Record the construction completely

Include yarn manufacturer and line, fiber declaration, color and lot if available, hook metric size and identifiable style, stitch definitions, starting count, rows or rounds, turning-chain convention, joining method, and working direction. For specialty fabrics, include forward/return procedures or loop-grouping rules.

Write special stitches in full or link to a stable definition in your own record. A note saying “moss” may become ambiguous because named textures vary. Record “sc, ch 1, skip 1; subsequent sc in chain spaces” when that is the actual construction, together with its edge instructions.

## Record treatment and dimensions

Measure before and after the intended care treatment. State how the sample was dried and whether it was pinned. Record relaxed dimensions after release and rest. If a load or stretch observation was made, describe the procedure rather than giving the result an unexplained technical rating.

For gauge, state the measured window and whether counts represent individual stitches, positions, or repeats. Save seam samples with the measurements of unjoined and joined pieces. A seam’s contribution is useful only when you know how it was measured.

## A practical library card

| Field | Example entry type |
|---|---|
| Sample ID | ST-014 |
| Fabric | Defined BLO single crochet in turned rows |
| Materials | Exact yarn declaration and hook |
| Counts | Starting chain, worked stitches, and rows |
| Conventions | Non-counting ch-1, no joins |
| Treatment | Actual wash and dry procedure |
| Dimensions | Before, after, and relaxed measurements |
| Gauge | Count and span away from edges |
| Behavior | Observed drape, recovery, curl, or snagging |
| Possible uses | Cuff candidate, pending actual fit sample |
| Limits | No durability or fitted-object test yet |

## Build reference sets

Make a base-stitch set in sc, hdc, dc, and tr using one yarn and hook. Make a placement set in both loops, BLO, and FLO. Make a raised-unit set using this book’s defined cluster, bobble, puff, and popcorn. Their comparison is more meaningful when the shared variables are recorded.

Add opening and joining sets: horizontal bridge, vertical slit, whipstitch, mattress-style seam, slip-stitch seam, and single crochet seam. Keep their reverse faces visible in photographs. A future design may depend on an inside surface that the original project photo never showed.

For lace and colorwork, save at least a complete repeat with its edges. One central fragment may fail to record how a row begins and ends. Include the chart and its key so the sample can be read independently of a website that may later change.

## Keep rejected samples

Label why a sample was rejected. “Too stiff for the planned cardigan” is different from “not a useful fabric.” The same sample may suit a different object. “Visible filling under this amount of stretch” identifies a specific limitation rather than condemning the entire yarn.

Do not quietly upgrade a rejected sample because time has passed and its color is appealing again. Repeat the relevant test if the intended use changes. Keep the old observation so the new result can be compared.

## Use the library to design

Begin a new project by choosing candidate samples whose behavior suits its needs. Make a fresh project- specific swatch when fit, new materials, or changed construction matter. A saved swatch narrows the search; it does not prove that your current tension or a new dye lot will reproduce it exactly.

Link the project record to its sample IDs. When an alteration succeeds, update the project’s final instructions and retain the earlier sample as history. Over time, you create a personal evidence base for the kinds of objects you actually make.

## Maintain digital and physical records together

Use clear filenames that include the sample code and version. Photograph the label, both faces, a ruler view, and important construction details. Back up editable notes and charts in a location you can recover. Do not rely on one app’s unsaved screen as the only copy of a tested pattern.

Keep a distinction between your own original instructions, notes adapted from another source, and references to a publisher’s pattern. Record source and permission information where relevant. A personal sample does not transfer rights to reproduce someone else’s written pattern or artwork in a book.

## A first library-building sequence

Start with the four base-stitch samples from Chapter 18. Add the placement sampler from Chapter 19, the shell sample from Chapter 20, the two-color grid from Chapter 14, one seam sample from Chapter 62, and the controlled patch from Chapter 36. Complete their records before adding more samples.

Then choose one original prototype from this book, make it in documented materials, and record the actual results. Its status can move from untested prototype to a personally sampled version when evidence exists. Independent testing, grading, and publication review remain separate steps.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I’d Known That!
>
> Label the swatch before it leaves the project bag. A collection of anonymous rectangles is technically a library, but mostly in the archaeological sense.
>
> Keep the failed version beside the improved one. The comparison explains the change better than either sample alone.
>
> Write what you would use the fabric for and what you would avoid. A library becomes valuable when it helps make the next decision.

## A sample library you can use

Label each swatch with a tag (a card or a tag attached with yarn) giving: yarn, fiber, weight, hook, stitch, stitch and row gauge, blocked or not, and date. Keep swatches in clear bags or a binder sleeve with the yarn label.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Knots on the tail can record the hook
> Tie a knot in a swatch's tail for each millimetre of hook size (for example, 5 knots for 5 mm). It survives when paper labels go missing.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Keep the failed swatches
> A swatch that did not work is information. Label it "too stiff" or "too loose" and keep it with the good one.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Your swatches are a diary of your hands. Look back at them on bad crochet days.
