Many crocheters eventually sell finished pieces, take commissions or sell patterns. This chapter covers the practical side: working out a price that covers your costs, photographing work so it sells, and presenting it well. Laws, taxes and marketplace rules vary by country, state and platform, so treat this as a planning guide and check the rules that apply to you.

> [!STATUS]
> General guidance only; not legal, tax or financial advice. Example numbers are illustrative.

## What a price has to cover

A sustainable price covers four things:

| Part | What it includes |
|---|---|
| Materials | Yarn, notions, labels, packaging |
| Time | Every hour of making, finishing, photographing and packing |
| Overheads | Tools, platform and payment fees, postage supplies, a share of software and equipment |
| Profit | What is left to grow the business or pay you beyond an hourly rate |

### A simple pricing formula

price = materials + (hours × hourly rate) + overheads + profit margin

> [!HINT] Worked example (illustrative numbers)
> Materials $18; 6 hours at $15 = $90; overheads $8; subtotal $116. A 10% profit margin adds $11.60, so about $128 before platform fees. Fees are often a percentage of the sale price, so add them on top.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Time yourself on the second one
> Your first version of a design takes longer than later ones. Time the second or third and price from that, but never stop counting finishing time; weaving ends and blocking are part of the job.

## When the price seems too high

Handmade crochet is slow, and many items cannot be priced to compete with factory goods. Options that keep the business honest:

- Choose items where crochet's look or customization is the point.
- Offer smaller items that take less time.
- Sell patterns, which you make once and can sell many times.
- Take commissions with a clear quote and deposit.

## Commissions

A written agreement prevents most problems. Include:

- what will be made, with size, yarn, colors and a sketch or photo reference;
- the price, deposit and when the balance is due;
- the delivery date and what happens if it changes;
- how many changes are included;
- care instructions supplied with the item.

## Photographing crochet

Good photographs sell crochet. Most of what matters is light and background, not equipment.

| Element | What works |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright indirect daylight near a window; avoid harsh direct sun and mixed indoor lighting |
| Background | Plain, uncluttered surfaces that contrast with the yarn |
| Color accuracy | Photograph a white card in the same light and use it to check white balance |
| Detail | One close-up of the stitches, one full view, one in use or worn |
| Scale | Include something that shows size, or state the measurements |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Shoot at the same time of day
> Light changes color through the day. Photographing a whole collection in one session, in the same light, makes the colors match from listing to listing.

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> A white foam board from the craft store, propped opposite the window, fills shadows better than any lamp I have tried.

## Writing a listing

- Name the item plainly and give measurements in inches and centimeters.
- List fiber content and care.
- Say whether it is made to order or ready to ship.
- For children's items and toys, check the safety and labeling rules for children's products where you sell; many places have specific requirements.

> [!SAFETY] Children's products
> Items for babies and children can be regulated (for example, small parts, cords and labeling rules). Research the requirements for each market before selling toys or baby items.

## Packing and shipping

Fold items around tissue, place them in a sealed bag to protect from moisture, and include a care card. Weigh a packed sample before setting shipping prices.

## Record-keeping

Keep a simple record of every sale, material purchase and fee. It helps with pricing, shows which items are worth your time, and is needed for taxes in most places.
