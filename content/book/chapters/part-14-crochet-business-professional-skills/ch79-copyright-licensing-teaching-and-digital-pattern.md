Designers, teachers and pattern sellers share a set of professional questions: who owns a pattern, what can be shared or sold, how to teach a class, and how to publish a digital pattern people can follow. This chapter introduces each one.

> [!STATUS]
> General information, not legal advice. Copyright and business law differ between countries. For anything you plan to rely on, check your country's official copyright office or ask a qualified professional.

## Copyright basics for crocheters

In many countries, including the United States, the written text, charts, photographs and illustrations of a pattern are protected by copyright as soon as they are created. Basic stitches and techniques themselves are generally treated as not protectable, because they are methods. This is why many crocheters can use the same stitch while each designer owns their own written pattern.

| Usually protected | Usually not protected |
|---|---|
| A pattern's written instructions | Individual stitches and techniques |
| Charts, schematics and diagrams | General ideas (a "granny square blanket") |
| Photographs | Common names and abbreviations |
| Illustrations | Facts such as standard measurements |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Write it in your own words
> When you base a design on a common stitch pattern, write the instructions yourself and draw your own charts. Copying someone else's wording, chart or photo is the part most likely to cause problems.

## Selling items made from someone else's pattern

Whether you may sell finished items made from another designer's pattern depends on the designer's terms and the law where you are. Many designers state their terms in the pattern. When in doubt, ask the designer and keep their reply. Credit the designer in your listing when they request it.

## Licensing your own patterns

When you sell patterns, decide and state your terms clearly:

- whether buyers may sell items made from the pattern;
- whether they must credit you;
- that the pattern itself may not be copied, shared or resold.

## Teaching crochet

A good beginner class is short, practical and has a finished result.

| Element | Suggestion |
|---|---|
| Class length | Short sessions with breaks, so hands rest |
| Group size | Small enough to see every learner's hands |
| Materials | Smooth, light-colored worsted yarn and a 5–6 mm hook, so stitches are easy to see |
| Content | One skill per session: chain, then sc, then turning |
| Handouts | Large, clear diagrams; right- and left-handed versions |
| Project | A small square or coaster that can be finished |

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> Bring spare hooks in several sizes. Half of all beginner frustration is a hook too small for the yarn.

See Chapter 12 for teaching left-handed crocheters.

## Publishing digital patterns

Digital (PDF) patterns are the most common way independent designers publish. A professional digital pattern usually includes:

1. A cover photo and a short description.
2. Skill level, finished sizes and measurements.
3. Yarn (weight, fiber, yardage per size), hook and notions.
4. Gauge, with the stitch and how it was measured.
5. Abbreviations and any special stitches.
6. Notes on construction and how to read the pattern.
7. Instructions with stitch counts at the end of rows or rounds.
8. Charts and schematics where helpful.
9. Finishing and care.
10. Copyright notice, terms of use and contact details.

> [!HINT] Test and edit before release
> Chapter 73 covers technical editing and pattern testing. A pattern that has been checked by a technical editor and worked by testers is far less likely to need corrections after release.

## Version control for patterns

Give every pattern a version number and date. When you fix an error, update the version, note the change on an errata page, and notify buyers if your platform allows it.
