This chapter is a quick reference for the two decisions behind every project: which hook and which yarn. The tables follow the Craft Yarn Council (CYC) Standard Yarn Weight System and hook-size guidelines (craftyarncouncil.com, checked November 2026). They are guidelines only. Your own gauge swatch always decides.

## Hook sizes: metric and US

Metric (mm) is the real measurement: the diameter of the shaft. US letter-and-number labels vary slightly between brands, so buy and record hooks by millimetres.

| Metric (mm) | US label | Metric (mm) | US label |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.25 | B-1 | 6.5 | K-10½ |
| 2.75 | C-2 | 7 | — |
| 3.25 | D-3 | 8 | L-11 |
| 3.5 | E-4 | 9 | M/N-13 |
| 3.75 | F-5 | 10 | N/P-15 |
| 4 | G-6 | 11.5 | P-16 |
| 4.5 | 7 | 15 | P/Q |
| 5 | H-8 | 15.75 | Q |
| 5.5 | I-9 | 19 | S |
| 6 | J-10 | 25 | T/U/X |

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Two "H" hooks can be different sizes
> A few brands label a 5 mm hook as H-8 and others use slightly different letters for nearby sizes. If your gauge changes when you swap brands, check the millimetre stamp on the hook, not the letter.

### Steel hooks

Steel hooks for thread run the opposite way to other hooks: the **larger the number, the smaller the hook**. A size 14 steel hook is far finer than a size 0. Because brands number steel hooks differently, always check the millimetre size printed on the hook or package.

## Yarn weights for crochet

The CYC system groups yarn into eight numbered categories. The ranges below are the CYC crochet guidelines: typical single crochet stitches per 4 in (10 cm) and recommended hook range. Lace weight is gauged in double crochet.

| Number | Name | Also called | Crochet gauge per 4 in | Hook range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Lace | fingering 10-count thread | 32–42 dc | steel 1.6–1.4 mm; regular 2.25 mm (B-1) |
| 1 | Super Fine | sock, fingering, baby | 21–32 sc | 2.25–3.5 mm (B-1 to E-4) |
| 2 | Fine | sport, baby | 16–20 sc | 3.5–4.5 mm (E-4 to 7) |
| 3 | Light | DK, light worsted | 12–17 sc | 4.5–5.5 mm (7 to I-9) |
| 4 | Medium | worsted, afghan, aran | 11–14 sc | 5.5–6.5 mm (I-9 to K-10½) |
| 5 | Bulky | chunky, craft, rug | 8–11 sc | 6.5–9 mm (K-10½ to M-13) |
| 6 | Super Bulky | super chunky, roving | 7–9 sc | 9–15 mm (M-13 to Q) |
| 7 | Jumbo | jumbo, roving | 6 sc and fewer | 15 mm and larger (Q and up) |

> [!GRANNY] Granny's Wisdom
> The number on the label tells you the family. The yards-per-ounce on the label tells you the yarn. Two "4" yarns can behave very differently.

## Reading the weight-to-length ratio

Two yarns in the same weight category can differ a great deal in thickness. A practical comparison is length per weight: divide the yardage on the label by its weight.

> **Example (arithmetic only):** a 100 g ball with 200 yd gives 2.0 yd per gram. A 100 g ball with 170 yd gives 1.7 yd per gram, so it is the thicker (or denser) of the two.

When substituting, choose a yarn whose length per weight is close to the original and whose fiber behaves similarly, then swatch.

## Substituting yarn: the arithmetic

1. Find the total length the pattern needs: number of balls × length per ball.
2. Divide by the length per ball of the new yarn.
3. Round **up** to the next whole ball, then add one extra ball for swatching and safety if the item is large.

> **Example (arithmetic only):** a pattern calls for 6 balls of 220 yd = 1,320 yd. The new yarn has 180 yd per ball. 1,320 ÷ 180 = 7.33, so buy 8 balls (9 to be safe on a garment).

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Buy all the yarn from one dye lot
> Skeins from different dye lots can differ visibly in color. Check that the dye-lot number on every label matches, and buy enough at once.

## Fibers at a glance

| Fiber | Stitch definition | Elasticity | Care notes (check the label) | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acrylic | Good | Low–moderate | Often machine washable | Blankets, toys, beginner practice |
| Wool | Good | High; holds shape | Many wools felt with heat and agitation unless labeled superwash | Hats, garments, warm accessories |
| Cotton | Very crisp | Low; can stretch with weight | Often machine washable; absorbent | Dishcloths, summer tops, amigurumi |
| Bamboo / viscose | Smooth, shiny | Low; drapey | Can grow when wet; follow label | Drapey shawls and tops |
| Alpaca | Soft halo | Low; can droop | Hand wash usually advised | Warm accessories |
| Blends | Varies | Varies | Follow label | Most everyday projects |

> [!HINT] Before you buy
> Read the care symbols as well as the weight. A baby gift in hand-wash-only yarn is often not a gift the parents can use.

## Hook materials and shapes

- **Aluminum:** smooth and fast; inexpensive. Some crocheters find them cold or slippery.
- **Ergonomic handles:** thicker grips that may reduce hand strain for some people over long sessions.
- **Wood and bamboo:** warm and grippy; good with slippery yarn.
- **Plastic:** light; common in very large sizes.
- **Tunisian hooks:** long shafts, or a hook on a flexible cable, to hold many loops at once.
- **Inline versus tapered throats:** inline hooks have a throat the same width as the shaft; tapered hooks narrow. Neither is "correct"; changing style can change your gauge.

> [!DANG] Dang, I Wish I'd Known That! — Your hook style changes your gauge
> Switching from a tapered hook to an inline hook of the same size can change your stitch size. Swatch with the hook you will actually use.
